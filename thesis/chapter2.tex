\chapter{Bosonic control with ancilla and coupler}
\label{preliminary}
This chapter develops the control framework for bosonic modes in superconducting circuits. We begin with the quantized LC oscillator, which provides the basic description of a cavity mode and clarifies the limitations of purely linear dynamics. We then introduce the transmon as a weakly anharmonic ancilla and show how its dispersive coupling to the cavity enables conditional control, including ECD gates and arbitrary single-cavity unitaries. Finally, we introduce flux-mediated three-wave mixing, which provides beam-splitter for entangling multiple bosonic modes.

\section{Oscillator Circuit Hamiltonian}

\label{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian}

The simplest circuit is LC oscillator, which serves as the foundational circuit element of superconducting quantum circuits. Its importance is twofold. First, it provides a direct correspondence between circuit variables and canonical variables in quantum mechanics. Second, it illustrates a fundamental limitation: a purely linear oscillator, even when driven by classical fields, cannot generate non-Gaussian states such as Fock states from the vacuum. This limitation motivates the introduction of a nonlinear element in the next subsection.

\subsection{Quantization of the LC oscillator}

We begin with an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ connected in parallel. The natural generalized coordinate is the node flux
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} V(t')\,dt',
\end{equation}
where $V(t)$ is the node voltage. The conjugate variable is the node charge $Q$, which is associated with the capacitor branch. In terms of the flux coordinate, the classical Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\Phi,\dot{\Phi}) = \frac{C}{2}\dot{\Phi}^{2} - \frac{1}{2L}\Phi^{2}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the electrostatic energy stored in the capacitor, while the second term is the magnetic energy stored in the inductor.

The canonical momentum conjugate to $\Phi$ is
\begin{equation}
    Q \equiv \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\Phi}} = C\dot{\Phi}.
\end{equation}
This is precisely the capacitor charge. Performing the Legendre transform gives the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H = Q\dot{\Phi} - \mathcal{L}
      = \frac{Q^{2}}{2C} + \frac{\Phi^{2}}{2L}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the LC circuit is formally identical to a mechanical harmonic oscillator, with the correspondence
\begin{equation}
    \Phi \leftrightarrow x,
    \qquad
    Q \leftrightarrow p,
    \qquad
    C \leftrightarrow m,
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{L} \leftrightarrow m\omega^{2}.
\end{equation}
The resonance frequency is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\end{equation}

Canonical quantization promotes $\Phi$ and $Q$ to operators satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q}] = i\hbar.
\end{equation}
The quantum Hamiltonian is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{0} = \frac{\hat{Q}^{2}}{2C} + \frac{\hat{\Phi}^{2}}{2L}.
\end{equation}
It is convenient to introduce the zero-point fluctuation amplitudes
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar Z}{2}},
    \qquad
    Q_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2Z}},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    Z = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}
\end{equation}
is the characteristic impedance of the oscillator. We then define annihilation and creation operators through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Phi} = \Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}),
    \qquad
    \hat{Q} = -iQ_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}),
\end{equation}
with $[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}] = 1$. Substituting these expressions into $\hat{H}_{0}$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{0}
    =
    \hbar\omega_{0}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is the standard quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian.

The eigenstates of this Hamiltonian are the Fock states $\{\ket{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$, defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\ket{n} = n\ket{n}.
\end{equation}
These states have equally spaced energies,
\begin{equation}
    E_{n} = \hbar\omega_{0}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Because the level spacing is uniform, the linear oscillator does not distinguish one transition from another. This spectral degeneracy is the central obstacle to selective state preparation using only linear control.

\subsection{Classical driving of the oscillator}

The oscillator can be controlled by applying a classical microwave tone. For a capacitive drive, the control enters linearly through the charge or flux quadrature. A convenient form for the driven Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}(t)
    =
    \hat{H}_{0}
    -
    \epsilon(t)\hat{\Phi},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon(t)$ is a classical drive amplitude determined by the external voltage source and coupling geometry. Expressed in ladder operators, this becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}(t)
    =
    \hbar\omega_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
    +
    f(t)\hat{a}
    +
    f^{*}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger},
\end{equation}
up to an irrelevant constant, where $f(t)$ is proportional to the classical microwave envelope.

For a monochromatic tone near resonance,
\begin{equation}
    f(t) = \hbar\Omega e^{-i\omega_{d} t},
\end{equation}
the Hamiltonian reads
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}(t)
    =
    \hbar\omega_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
    +
    \hbar\Omega e^{-i\omega_{d} t}\hat{a}
    +
    \hbar\Omega^{*} e^{i\omega_{d} t}\hat{a}^{\dagger}.
\end{equation}
Moving to a frame rotating at the drive frequency and applying the rotating-wave approximation gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{rot}}
    =
    \hbar\Delta\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
    +
    \hbar\Omega\,\hat{a}
    +
    \hbar\Omega^{*}\hat{a}^{\dagger},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta = \omega_{0}-\omega_{d}$ is the detuning.

The corresponding time-evolution operator is a displacement operator,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\phi(t)} \hat{D}(\alpha(t)),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}(\alpha) = \exp\!\left(\alpha\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{a}\right).
\end{equation}
Therefore, a linearly driven harmonic oscillator maps the vacuum state to a coherent state,
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi(t)} = \hat{D}(\alpha)\ket{0} = \ket{\alpha}.
\end{equation}
More generally, if the Hamiltonian remains quadratic in $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^{\dagger}$, classical controls can generate only Gaussian unitaries. Besides displacement, this broader class includes squeezing through terms of the form
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{sq}}(t)
    =
    \hbar\lambda(t)\hat{a}^{2}
    +
    \hbar\lambda^{*}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger 2},
\end{equation}
which arise from parametric modulation rather than from a simple linear microwave drive. The associated unitary is the squeezing operator
\begin{equation}
    \hat{S}(\xi)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        \frac{1}{2}
        \left(
            \xi^{*}\hat{a}^{2}
            -
            \xi \hat{a}^{\dagger 2}
        \right)
    \right].
\end{equation}
Hence, the full reachable set of a classically controlled \emph{linear} oscillator is restricted to Gaussian transformations: displacements, phase-space rotations, and, when quadratic time-dependent control is allowed, squeezing.

\subsection{Why Fock states are unreachable in a linear oscillator}

This restriction has an immediate consequence for state preparation. The vacuum state $\ket{0}$ is Gaussian, and Gaussian unitaries preserve Gaussianity. Therefore, any state obtained from $\ket{0}$ under a Hamiltonian that is at most quadratic in $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^{\dagger}$ must also be Gaussian. Symbolically,
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi_{\mathrm{reachable}}}
    =
    \hat{U}_{\mathrm{Gaussian}}\ket{0}
    \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
    \ket{\psi_{\mathrm{reachable}}}
    \text{ is Gaussian.}
\end{equation}

Fock states with $n\geq 1$ are non-Gaussian. For example, their Wigner functions exhibit oscillations and negativity, which cannot be produced by Gaussian evolution starting from vacuum. In particular, no choice of classical linear drive can generate $\ket{1}$, $\ket{2}$, or any other excited number state from $\ket{0}$. The obstruction is not merely practical but structural: the Hamiltonian of a harmonic system preserves the Gaussian character of the state and lacks the nonlinearity required to isolate specific transitions in the equally spaced ladder.

Equivalently, one may view the problem from the spectrum. Because every adjacent transition has the same energy $\hbar\omega_{0}$, a resonant drive that couples $\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}$ also couples $\ket{1}\leftrightarrow\ket{2}$, $\ket{2}\leftrightarrow\ket{3}$, and so on. The drive cannot spectrally select a single pair of levels. As a result, linear control produces phase-space motion rather than deterministic preparation of a specific non-Gaussian Fock state.

This limitation provides the essential motivation for introducing a nonlinear circuit element. By breaking the exact harmonicity of the spectrum, a nonlinear oscillator acquires nonuniform level spacing, which enables selective addressing of individual transitions and allows the generation of genuinely non-Gaussian quantum states. In superconducting circuits, the most important realization of such a weakly anharmonic oscillator is the transmon, which we introduce in the next subsection.

\section{Transmon as a Weakly Anharmonic System}
\label{sec:transmon}

The linear LC oscillator provides a clean starting point for circuit quantization, but its equally spaced spectrum prevents selective preparation of non-Gaussian states. A superconducting qubit requires a circuit element that preserves low dissipation while breaking exact harmonicity. In circuit QED, this role is played by the Josephson junction. When a junction is shunted by a large capacitance, the resulting device---the transmon---forms a weakly anharmonic oscillator whose lowest two levels can be isolated and used as an effective qubit.

\subsection{Josephson Hamiltonian}

A Josephson junction is characterized by the nonlinear current--phase relation
\begin{equation}
    I = I_c \sin \varphi,
\end{equation}
and by the Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi},
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ is the superconducting phase difference across the junction and $\Phi_0 = h/2e$ is the flux quantum. When the junction is shunted by a capacitance $C_\Sigma$, the conjugate variables are the phase $\hat{\varphi}$ and the Cooper-pair number operator $\hat{n}$, satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\hat{\varphi},\hat{n}] = i.
\end{equation}
The charging energy is defined as
\begin{equation}
    E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}.
\end{equation}
Neglecting offset-charge effects for simplicity, the circuit Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
    \label{eq:JJ_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This Hamiltonian already reveals the essential difference from the LC oscillator. The charging term is quadratic, but the Josephson potential is cosine rather than quadratic, and therefore introduces the nonlinearity required for anharmonic energy levels.

\subsection{Transmon limit and weak anharmonicity}

The transmon operates in the regime
\begin{equation}
    E_J \gg E_C,
\end{equation}
where phase fluctuations are small and the cosine potential may be expanded around one of its minima, typically $\varphi = 0$:
\begin{equation}
    -E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}
    \approx
    -E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\hat{\varphi}^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\hat{\varphi}^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\hat{\varphi}^6).
\end{equation}
Substituting this into Eq.~\eqref{eq:JJ_hamiltonian} gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    \approx
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    +
    \frac{E_J}{2}\hat{\varphi}^2
    -
    \frac{E_J}{24}\hat{\varphi}^4
    -
    E_J.
\end{equation}
The first two terms define a harmonic oscillator, while the quartic correction produces a weak anharmonicity. To make this explicit, introduce ladder operators through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\varphi} = \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger),
    \qquad
    \hat{n} = i n_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{b}^\dagger-\hat{b}),
\end{equation}
with zero-point fluctuations
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{2E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/4},
    \qquad
    n_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{E_J}{32E_C}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
The quadratic part becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{harm}}
    =
    \hbar\omega_p
    \left(
        \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{b} + \frac{1}{2}
    \right)
    -
    E_J,
\end{equation}
with plasma frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \frac{\sqrt{8E_JE_C}}{\hbar}.
\end{equation}

The quartic term can be treated perturbatively. To leading order, the transmon spectrum is
\begin{equation}
    E_m
    \approx
    -E_J
    +
    \sqrt{8E_JE_C}\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)
    -
    \frac{E_C}{12}\left(6m^2+6m+3\right),
    \qquad m=0,1,2,\dots
    \label{eq:transmon_spectrum}
\end{equation}
which is nearly harmonic but not exactly equally spaced. The lowest transition frequency is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \hbar\omega_{01}
    =
    E_1-E_0
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_JE_C} - E_C,
\end{equation}
or, in angular-frequency units,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{01}
    \approx
    \frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\sqrt{8E_JE_C} - E_C\right).
\end{equation}
The anharmonicity is defined by the difference between adjacent transition frequencies,
\begin{equation}
    \alpha
    \equiv
    \omega_{12}-\omega_{01}.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:transmon_spectrum}, one finds
\begin{equation}
    \alpha \approx -\frac{E_C}{\hbar},
    \qquad
    \text{equivalently}
    \qquad
    \hbar\alpha \approx -E_C.
\end{equation}
The negative sign indicates that the level spacing decreases with excitation number. This weak anharmonicity is the defining feature of the transmon: it is large enough to distinguish the lowest transition from higher ones, yet small enough that the device retains many oscillator-like properties.

The choice $E_J \gg E_C$ also suppresses charge dispersion exponentially, which strongly reduces sensitivity to charge noise. The cost is that the spectrum remains only weakly anharmonic, so gate design must avoid leakage into higher levels.

\subsection{Flux-tunable transmon}

A fixed-frequency transmon is obtained from a single Josephson junction. Frequency tunability is introduced by replacing that junction with a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID), namely a loop interrupted by two Josephson junctions. The external flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$ threading the loop controls the effective Josephson energy and therefore the qubit frequency.

For junction energies $E_{J1}$ and $E_{J2}$, the SQUID behaves as a single junction with flux-dependent effective Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    =
    E_{J\Sigma}
    \sqrt{
        \cos^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
        +
        d^2
        \sin^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    },
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_{J\Sigma} = E_{J1}+E_{J2},
    \qquad
    d = \frac{E_{J1}-E_{J2}}{E_{J1}+E_{J2}}.
\end{equation}
In the symmetric case $E_{J1}=E_{J2}$, this reduces to
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    =
    E_{J\Sigma}
    \left|
        \cos\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    \right|.
\end{equation}
The transmon transition frequency therefore acquires flux dependence,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_q(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    \approx
    \frac{1}{\hbar}
    \left[
        \sqrt{8E_C E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})}
        -
        E_C
    \right].
\end{equation}
External flux thus provides direct control of the qubit spectrum. By changing $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$, one can bring the qubit into and out of resonance with other circuit elements, modulate detunings, and activate interactions that are otherwise off resonant.


\section{ECD Scheme for Arbitrary Single-Cavity Unitary}
\label{sec:ecd_single_cavity}

Section~\ref{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian} showed that a single linear cavity driven only by classical microwave fields remains confined to Gaussian dynamics. In particular, starting from vacuum, such control cannot produce Fock states or implement general non-Gaussian unitaries on the cavity mode. The transmon introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transmon} provides the missing nonlinearity. When the cavity is dispersively coupled to the transmon, the qubit can mediate conditional phase-space operations on the cavity, and these operations promote the cavity from a Gaussian system to a controllable bosonic mode. To formulate this control scheme correctly, however, one should begin from the full weakly anharmonic transmon ladder and only then reduce to an effective two-level ancilla.

\subsection{Dispersive cavity--transmon coupling}
\label{sec:ecd_dispersive_coupling}

The transmon is not fundamentally a two-level system, but a weakly anharmonic oscillator with eigenstates
\(\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0}^\infty\), obtained by diagonalizing the Josephson Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{T}}
    =
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
\end{equation}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:transmon}, its spectrum forms a ladder of levels with transition frequencies
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{j+1,j}
    =
    \frac{E_{j+1}-E_j}{\hbar}
    \approx
    \omega_{01} + j\alpha,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha<0\) is the transmon anharmonicity. Because \(|\alpha|\) is small compared with \(\omega_{01}\), the transmon remains close to a harmonic oscillator, but its level spacings are not exactly equal. This weak anharmonicity is sufficient to isolate the lowest transition under appropriate control conditions.

We now couple the transmon capacitively to a single cavity mode of frequency \(\omega_c\), described by the annihilation operator \(\hat{a}\). In the transmon eigenbasis, the uncoupled Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_0
    =
    \hbar\omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty \hbar\omega_j \ket{j}\bra{j},
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_j = E_j/\hbar\). The capacitive interaction arises through the transmon charge operator \(\hat{n}\), whose dominant matrix elements connect neighboring transmon levels. The coupled cavity--transmon system is therefore described by the generalized Jaynes--Cummings Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    \hbar\omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty \hbar\omega_j \ket{j}\bra{j}
    +
    \hbar\sum_{j=0}^\infty g_j
    \left(
        \hat{a}^\dagger \ket{j}\bra{j+1}
        +
        \hat{a}\ket{j+1}\bra{j}
    \right),
    \label{eq:multilevel_JC}
\end{equation}
where \(g_j\) is the cavity coupling matrix element for the \(\ket{j}\leftrightarrow\ket{j+1}\) transition. In the transmon regime, these couplings approximately scale as
\begin{equation}
    g_j \approx \sqrt{j+1}\,g_0,
\end{equation}
reflecting the near-oscillator character of the device.

The ECD protocol operates in the dispersive regime, where each relevant transmon transition is far detuned from the cavity,
\begin{equation}
    |\Delta_j|
    =
    |\omega_{j+1,j}-\omega_c|
    \gg
    g_j\sqrt{n+1},
\end{equation}
for all significantly occupied photon numbers \(n\). In this limit, real exchange of excitations is suppressed, and the interaction can be treated perturbatively. To second order, the energy shift of the bare state \(\ket{j,n}\) is
\begin{equation}
    \delta E_{j,n}^{(2)}
    =
    \hbar
    \left[
        \frac{(n+1)g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}}
        -
        \frac{n g_j^2}{\Delta_j}
    \right],
\end{equation}
with the convention \(g_{-1}=0\). Reorganizing these shifts yields the multilevel dispersive Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(\mathrm{multi})}
    =
    \hbar\omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty
    \hbar\left(\omega_j+\Lambda_j\right)\ket{j}\bra{j}
    +
    \hbar\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty
    \chi_j \ket{j}\bra{j},
    \label{eq:multilevel_disp}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_j = \frac{g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}},
    \qquad
    \chi_j
    =
    \frac{g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}}
    -
    \frac{g_j^2}{\Delta_j}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:multilevel_disp} shows that each transmon level pulls the cavity by a different amount. The cavity therefore acquires a transmon-state-dependent frequency shift, while the transmon levels acquire Lamb shifts.

At this stage the ancilla is still a multilevel system. The reduction to an effective qubit is an approximation that must be justified. In the present setting, that approximation is valid for three reasons. First, the control pulses are designed to act resonantly on the \(\ket{g}\equiv\ket{0}\) to \(\ket{e}\equiv\ket{1}\) transition, while the next transition is detuned by the anharmonicity \(|\alpha|\). Second, the qubit-drive Rabi rate and spectral bandwidth are chosen small enough that off-resonant excitation of \(\ket{f}\equiv\ket{2}\) and higher states remains negligible. Third, the cavity population is kept below the critical photon number associated with the dispersive approximation, so that the cavity does not strongly hybridize with higher transmon levels. Under these conditions, population leakage out of \(\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}\) is weak, and the transmon can be treated as an effective two-level ancilla for the purpose of gate synthesis.

Projecting Eq.~\eqref{eq:multilevel_disp} onto the lowest two transmon levels gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(ge)}
    =
    \hbar\omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \hbar(\omega_g+\Lambda_g)\ket{g}\bra{g}
    +
    \hbar(\omega_e+\Lambda_e)\ket{e}\bra{e}
    +
    \hbar\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    \left(
        \chi_g \ket{g}\bra{g}
        +
        \chi_e \ket{e}\bra{e}
    \right).
\end{equation}
Introducing
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}_z = \ket{e}\bra{e} - \ket{g}\bra{g},
\end{equation}
together with the dressed qubit frequency
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\omega}_q
    =
    (\omega_e+\Lambda_e)-(\omega_g+\Lambda_g),
\end{equation}
the mean cavity pull
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\chi} = \frac{\chi_e+\chi_g}{2},
\end{equation}
and the differential cavity pull
\begin{equation}
    \chi = \chi_e-\chi_g,
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(ge)}/\hbar
    =
    (\omega_c+\bar{\chi})\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \frac{\tilde{\omega}_q}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z.
    \label{eq:two_level_disp_from_multilevel}
\end{equation}
The first term simply renormalizes the cavity frequency and can be absorbed into a redefinition of \(\omega_c\). The last term is the effective dispersive interaction used throughout the ECD protocol.

For a weakly anharmonic transmon, Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_level_disp_from_multilevel} reduces to the familiar transmon dispersive shift. Using \(g_1 \approx \sqrt{2}\,g_0\) and \(\Delta_1=\Delta_0+\alpha\), one finds
\begin{equation}
    \chi
    \approx
    2g_0^2
    \left(
        \frac{1}{\Delta_0}
        -
        \frac{1}{\Delta_0+\alpha}
    \right)
    =
    \frac{2g_0^2 \alpha}{\Delta_0(\Delta_0+\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the standard qubit--cavity dispersive Hamiltonian is not imposed by assumption, but emerges as the low-energy reduction of the full multilevel cavity--transmon system.

We now add the classical cavity and qubit drives used for bosonic control:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}/\hbar
    =
    \epsilon^*(t)\hat{a} + \epsilon(t)\hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right],
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon(t)\) is the cavity-drive envelope and \(\Omega(t)\) is the qubit-drive envelope. In a frame rotating at the dressed cavity and qubit frequencies, the controlled dynamics reduce to
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}/\hbar
    =
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \epsilon^*(t)\hat{a}
    +
    \epsilon(t)\hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right].
    \label{eq:ecd_labframe}
\end{equation}
This Hamiltonian is the effective starting point for the echoed conditional displacement construction developed next.

To expose the useful interaction, we move to a displaced frame defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}[\alpha(t)]
    =
    \exp\!\left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger - \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right),
\end{equation}
with \(\alpha(t)\) chosen to follow the classical cavity response,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\alpha}(t) = -i\epsilon(t).
\end{equation}
Using
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\hat{a}\hat{D}(\alpha) = \hat{a}+\alpha,
\end{equation}
the dispersive term transforms as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\,
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,
    \hat{D}(\alpha)
    =
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \alpha \hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \alpha^* \hat{a}
    +
    |\alpha|^2.
\end{equation}
The displaced-frame Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{H}/\hbar
    =
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}
    \left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger + \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right)\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}|\alpha(t)|^2 \hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right].
    \label{eq:ecd_displaced}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ecd_displaced} contains three distinct qubit-dependent terms. The first term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z,
\end{equation}
is an unwanted conditional phase rotation of the cavity. The third term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}|\alpha(t)|^2 \hat{\sigma}_z,
\end{equation}
is an unwanted qubit \(Z\) rotation. The middle term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}
    \left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger + \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right)\hat{\sigma}_z,
    \label{eq:desired_conditional_drive}
\end{equation}
is the desired conditional linear drive on the cavity. The purpose of the echoed conditional displacement protocol is to retain Eq.~\eqref{eq:desired_conditional_drive} while canceling the other two terms.

\subsection{Universal gate set for the joint qubit--cavity system}

The ECD gate is combined with arbitrary single-qubit rotations
\begin{equation}
    R(\theta,\phi)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        -i\frac{\theta}{2}
        \left(
            \cos\phi\,\hat{\sigma}_x
            +
            \sin\phi\,\hat{\sigma}_y
        \right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:single_qubit_rotation}
\end{equation}
A generic control sequence then has the form
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_N
    =
    R(\theta_N,\phi_N)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_N)
    \cdots
    R(\theta_2,\phi_2)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_2)\,
    R(\theta_1,\phi_1)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_1).
    \label{eq:ecd_circuit}
\end{equation}
The pair
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}
    =
    \left\{
        \mathrm{ECD}(\beta),\,
        R(\theta,\phi)
    \right\}
\end{equation}
forms a universal gate set for the joint cavity--qubit Hilbert space, in the sense that arbitrary unitaries on a finite-dimensional truncation of the cavity together with the qubit can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy.

The physical reason is clear. The qubit rotations provide arbitrary control within the ancilla two-level system, while ECD supplies a non-Gaussian entangling operation between the ancilla and the bosonic mode. Once these two ingredients are available, repeated commutation and composition generate a sufficiently rich Lie algebra to span arbitrary operations on the truncated joint space. In contrast with the Gaussian controls of Section~\ref{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian}, the qubit-mediated ECD interaction is not restricted to affine transformations of phase space.

\subsection{Synthesis of arbitrary cavity unitaries}

To implement a target cavity unitary $\hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}$, one works in a truncated Fock space
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_d = \mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\dots,\ket{d-1}\},
\end{equation}
chosen large enough to contain the support of the states involved in the protocol. The control sequence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ecd_circuit} is then optimized so that, for every cavity state $\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_d$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_N
    \bigl(
        \ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{g}
    \bigr)
    \approx
    \bigl(
        \hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}\ket{\psi}
    \bigr)
    \otimes
    \ket{g}.
    \label{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition} expresses two requirements simultaneously: the cavity undergoes the desired unitary transformation, and the qubit is returned to its initial state so that no residual entanglement remains.

In practice, the parameters
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
        \beta_k,\theta_k,\phi_k
    \right\}_{k=1}^N
\end{equation}
are found by numerical optimization on the truncated Hilbert space. The optimization minimizes a cost function based on the overlap between the synthesized unitary $\hat{U}_N$ and the target operation $\hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}} \otimes \mathbb{I}_q$, or between the corresponding state-transfer maps when only a state-preparation task is required. Because the gate set is universal, increasing the circuit depth $N$ allows one, in principle, to approximate any desired single-cavity unitary to arbitrary accuracy on $\mathcal{H}_d$.

This synthesis strategy provides a concrete route from a small set of experimentally natural primitives to general bosonic control. Fock-state preparation, cat-state generation, logical-state encoding, and more general non-Gaussian cavity gates all arise as special cases of the same framework. The essential point is that the cavity is not controlled directly through a native cavity nonlinearity; rather, the nonlinearity is borrowed from the transmon and converted, through the dispersive interaction and echoed conditional displacements, into programmable phase-space control.

The remainder of this chapter uses this viewpoint throughout. Once the cavity--qubit system is equipped with the gate set $\{\mathrm{ECD}(\beta),R(\theta,\phi)\}$, single-mode bosonic control reduces to the problem of compiling the desired operation into an experimentally feasible sequence of echoed conditional displacements and qubit rotations.


\section{3-Wave Mixing and Beam-Splitting Interactions for Two-Mode Entanglement}
\label{sec:three_wave_mixing_two_mode}

The control framework developed so far addresses a single cavity mode. That is sufficient for
arbitrary single-mode bosonic unitaries, but it is not sufficient for genuinely multimode tasks such
as state transfer, nonlocal interference, and, most importantly, entanglement generation between
spatially distinct cavity modes. To reach that regime, one must go beyond single-mode control and
engineer a coherent interaction between two bosonic modes. In superconducting circuits, this is
naturally achieved by using a flux-tunable nonlinear element as a parametric coupler.

We therefore consider two cavity modes, \(\hat{a}\) and \(\hat{b}\), coupled through a flux-tunable
transmon or related Josephson nonlinear element represented by a third mode \(\hat{c}\). Under
flux modulation, the Josephson nonlinearity acts as a three-wave mixer and mediates coherent
exchange between the two cavity modes. The essential point is that the external pump does not
merely drive the system; it selects which interaction channel becomes resonant.

\subsection{Three-wave mixing from a flux-tunable nonlinear element}

The uncoupled Hamiltonian of the three modes is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_0
    =
    \hbar\omega_a \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \hbar\omega_b \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{b}
    +
    \hbar\omega_c \hat{c}^\dagger \hat{c}.
\end{equation}
Because the coupler is nonlinear and flux tunable, its Josephson energy becomes time dependent,
\begin{equation}
    E_J\bigl[\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)\bigr],
    \qquad
    \Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
    =
    \Phi_{\mathrm{dc}}
    +
    \Phi_p \cos(\omega_p t + \phi_p),
\end{equation}
so that the normal modes inherit parametrically activated mixing terms. To leading order, the
resulting nonlinear interaction can be written as a cubic Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{3\mathrm{wm}}
    =
    \hbar g_3
    (\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)
    (\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger)
    (\hat{c}+\hat{c}^\dagger),
    \label{eq:full_3wm}
\end{equation}
where \(g_3\) is the effective three-wave-mixing strength set by the circuit parameters and the flux
bias point. Expanding Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_3wm} generates several operator products, including terms
such as
\begin{equation}
    \hbar g_3
    \left(
        \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger \hat{c}
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b}\hat{c}^\dagger
    \right),
\end{equation}
which describe coherent conversion between the two cavity modes mediated by the nonlinear mode,
as well as terms such as
\begin{equation}
    \hbar g_3
    \left(
        \hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{c}^\dagger
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{c}
    \right),
\end{equation}
which describe pair creation and annihilation processes.

To activate a controlled interaction between \(\hat{a}\) and \(\hat{b}\), the nonlinear mode
\(\hat{c}\) is driven strongly and treated in the stiff-pump approximation as a classical field,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{c}
    \;\rightarrow\;
    \xi_p e^{-i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)}
    +
    \xi_p^* e^{i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)},
\end{equation}
where \(\xi_p\) is the complex pump amplitude. Substituting this into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_3wm} yields an effective bilinear interaction between the two cavity modes,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
    =
    \hbar G
    \bigl(
        e^{-i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)}
        +
        e^{i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)}
    \bigr)
    (\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)
    (\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger),
    \label{eq:effective_bilinear}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    G = g_3 |\xi_p|.
\end{equation}
Thus, the strong pump converts the native three-mode nonlinearity into a tunable two-mode coupling.

\subsection{Pump selection and rotating-wave approximation}

To determine which interaction is realized, we move to the interaction picture with respect to
\(\hat{H}_0\). Using
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}(t)=\hat{a}e^{-i\omega_a t},
    \qquad
    \hat{b}(t)=\hat{b}e^{-i\omega_b t},
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_bilinear} becomes
\begin{align}
    \hat{H}_I/\hbar
    =
    G \Big[
        &
        \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
        e^{-i(\omega_a-\omega_b-\omega_p)t-i\phi_p}
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}
        e^{+i(\omega_a-\omega_b-\omega_p)t+i\phi_p}
        \nonumber\\
        &
        +
        \hat{a}\hat{b}
        e^{-i(\omega_a+\omega_b-\omega_p)t-i\phi_p}
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}^\dagger
        e^{+i(\omega_a+\omega_b-\omega_p)t+i\phi_p}
    \Big]
    +
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{fast}},
    \label{eq:interaction_picture_3wm}
\end{align}
where \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{fast}}\) collects the counter-rotating terms oscillating at frequencies
\(\omega_a \pm \omega_b + \omega_p\). When these fast terms oscillate much faster than the induced
coupling strength \(G\), they average to zero under the rotating-wave approximation.

The interaction selected by the pump is therefore determined by which exponential in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:interaction_picture_3wm} is made stationary.

\subsection{Beam-splitter interaction}

If the pump is chosen at the mode-difference frequency,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = |\omega_b-\omega_a|,
\end{equation}
the near-resonant terms are
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
    \qquad \text{and} \qquad
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b},
\end{equation}
while all others rotate rapidly and are discarded under the RWA. Assuming
\(\omega_b > \omega_a\) for definiteness, so that \(\omega_p=\omega_b-\omega_a\), the effective
Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{BS}}
    =
    \hbar G
    \left(
        e^{-i\phi_p}\hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
        +
        e^{i\phi_p}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b}
    \right).
    \label{eq:beam_splitter_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This is the beam-splitter, or frequency-conversion, interaction. It conserves the total excitation
number,
\begin{equation}
    \left[
        \hat{H}_{\mathrm{BS}},
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b}
    \right]
    = 0,
\end{equation}
and coherently swaps excitations between the two cavity modes. Its unitary evolution is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}(t)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        -itG
        \left(
            e^{-i\phi_p}\hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
            +
            e^{i\phi_p}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}
        \right)
    \right].
\end{equation}
Physically, Eq.~\eqref{eq:beam_splitter_hamiltonian} hybridizes the two modes exactly as an
optical beam splitter mixes two traveling fields. It is therefore the natural interaction for
state transfer, interference, and mode conversion.

By itself, however, the beam-splitter interaction does not create entanglement from the two-mode
vacuum, since
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{BS}}\ket{0,0}=0.
\end{equation}
Its role in entanglement generation is instead indirect but essential: when one mode is first
prepared in a nonclassical state, or when the beam-splitter is combined with single-mode bosonic
control from Section~\ref{sec:ecd_single_cavity}, the mixing operation converts local nonclassicality
into nonlocal correlations between the two cavities.

\subsection{Two-mode squeezing interaction}

If the pump is instead chosen at the mode-sum frequency,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \omega_a+\omega_b,
\end{equation}
then the near-resonant terms are
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}\hat{b}
    \qquad \text{and} \qquad
    \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}^\dagger.
\end{equation}
After the RWA, the effective Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{TMS}}
    =
    \hbar G
    \left(
        e^{-i\phi_p}\hat{a}\hat{b}
        +
        e^{i\phi_p}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}^\dagger
    \right).
    \label{eq:tms_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This is the two-mode-squeezing interaction. Unlike the beam-splitter Hamiltonian, it does not
conserve excitation number. Instead, it creates or annihilates photons in correlated pairs, one in
each cavity. The corresponding unitary is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_{\mathrm{TMS}}(t)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        -itG
        \left(
            e^{-i\phi_p}\hat{a}\hat{b}
            +
            e^{i\phi_p}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}^\dagger
        \right)
    \right].
\end{equation}
Acting on the two-mode vacuum, this unitary produces a two-mode squeezed state,
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi_{\mathrm{TMS}}}
    =
    \hat{U}_{\mathrm{TMS}}(t)\ket{0,0},
\end{equation}
which is an entangled Gaussian state with correlated photon-number fluctuations and squeezed
EPR-like quadratures. Thus, two-mode squeezing provides the most direct route to generating
entanglement between two cavity modes.

\subsection{Control perspective and relevance for the rest of the chapter}

The key advantage of three-wave mixing is that the same nonlinear hardware element can realize
different effective interactions simply by changing the pump frequency. A pump at the difference
frequency activates the beam-splitter channel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:beam_splitter_hamiltonian}; a pump
at the sum frequency activates the two-mode-squeezing channel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tms_hamiltonian}.
The pump amplitude sets the interaction strength \(G\), while the pump phase \(\phi_p\) sets the
interaction phase. In this way, the flux-tunable transmon functions as a programmable nonlinear
mixer for bosonic modes.

This flexibility is precisely what is needed for multimode bosonic control. The beam-splitter
interaction enables coherent transfer and interference between cavities, while the two-mode-squeezing
interaction provides a direct entangling resource. Together with the single-mode cavity control
developed earlier, these parametrically activated interactions furnish the basic toolbox for preparing
and manipulating two-mode entangled bosonic states in circuit QED.

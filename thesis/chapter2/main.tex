\chapter{Superconducting Qubits: Coherence and Control}
\label{chap:sc-qubits}

This chapter introduces the superconducting circuits and control techniques used throughout the thesis. The discussion is organized around the three hardware paradigms introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}: the transmon, fluxonium, and bosonic qubits. Detailed discussion of decoherence mechanisms and coherence metrics is deferred to Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}, and Sec.~\ref{sec:control} presents the analytical and numerical techniques used to control these systems.\ref{sec:control}
\section{Superconducting Qubits}
\label{sec:sc-qubits}

All superconducting qubits rely on one or more Josephson junctions, whose nonlinear inductance makes the circuit spectrum anharmonic. This anharmonicity allows individual transitions to be spectrally resolved and the lowest two levels to be addressed as an effective qubit. The central design trade-off is between spectral selectivity and robustness to fluctuations: stronger anharmonicity improves addressability and suppresses leakage to noncomputational states, while reduced parameter sensitivity improves stability against noise. Different qubits resolve this trade-off through their circuit topology, parameter regime, and operating point. The following subsections introduce the three platforms most relevant to this thesis.

The transmon is a Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitance. It is designed to operate in the regime $E_J \gg E_C$, where the spectrum remains anharmonic but the sensitivity to offset charge is strongly reduced. Its physics is governed primarily by two energy scales: the charging energy $E_C$ and the Josephson energy $E_J$.

\subsubsection{Josephson Hamiltonian}

A Josephson junction [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(c)] consists of two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier through which Cooper pairs tunnel coherently. The junction is characterized by the nonlinear current--phase relation
\begin{equation}
    I = I_c \sin \varphi,
\end{equation}
where $I_c$ is the critical current and $\varphi$ is the superconducting phase difference across the barrier.

The corresponding Josephson energy is
\begin{equation}
    E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi},
\end{equation}
with $\Phi_0 = h/2e$ the flux quantum. Unlike a linear inductor, whose energy is quadratic in current, the Josephson junction contributes an energy that is periodic in $\varphi$. This cosine dependence is the source of the circuit nonlinearity and, ultimately, of the anharmonicity needed for qubit operation.

Because the junction connects two superconducting islands, Cooper pairs tunnel across it in discrete units. The charge operator $n$, which counts the number of transferred Cooper pairs, therefore has integer eigenvalues. Its conjugate variable is the gauge-invariant phase difference $\varphi$. In the charge basis, the most natural phase operators are the exponentials $e^{\pm i\varphi}$, which shift the charge by one Cooper pair and satisfy
\begin{equation}
    [e^{\pm i\varphi},\, n] = \pm e^{\pm i\varphi}.
\end{equation}
The Josephson term can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    \cos \varphi = \frac{e^{i\varphi} + e^{-i\varphi}}{2},
\end{equation}
which is a well-defined operator in the charge basis.

The charging energy associated with the total shunt capacitance $C_\Sigma$ is
\begin{equation}
    E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}.
\end{equation}
Including a possible offset charge $n_g$, the circuit Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C (n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos \varphi.
\end{equation}
In the transmon regime $E_J \gg E_C$, the charge dispersion is exponentially suppressed, so to leading order one may set $n_g = 0$ and write
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C\, n^2 - E_J \cos \varphi.
    \label{eq:JJ_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
The charging term is quadratic, whereas the Josephson term is a cosine rather than a parabola. This departure from a harmonic potential is what produces nonuniform level spacing.

\subsubsection{Transmon limit and weak anharmonicity}

The transmon operates in the regime
\begin{equation}
    E_J \gg E_C,
\end{equation}
for which phase fluctuations are small.

In this limit the charge spectrum is effectively continuous, so $\varphi$ may be treated as an ordinary unbounded operator satisfying $[\varphi,n]=i$. This is the key simplification that allows the compact-phase description of the Cooper-pair box to be replaced by an expansion about the minimum of the cosine potential. Expanding around $\varphi=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
    -E_J \cos \varphi
    \approx
    -E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\,\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\,\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6).
\end{equation}
Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:JJ_hamiltonian} yields
\begin{equation}
    H
    \approx
    4E_C\, n^2
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\,\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\,\varphi^4
    - E_J.
\end{equation}

The quadratic terms define a harmonic oscillator with plasma frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \sqrt{8E_JE_C}.
\end{equation}
This is the Josephson analogue of the LC resonance frequency, with the Josephson inductance $L_J = (\Phi_0/2\pi)^2/E_J$ playing the role of the inductance. The quartic correction is small but essential: it shifts adjacent transition frequencies by different amounts and therefore produces a weak anharmonicity.

To make this structure explicit, introduce ladder operators through
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(b + b^\dagger),
    \qquad
    n = i\, n_{\mathrm{zpf}}(b^\dagger - b),
\end{equation}
with zero-point fluctuations
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{2E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/4},
    \qquad
    n_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{E_J}{32E_C}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
The quadratic part of the Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{harm}}
    = \omega_p\left(b^\dagger b + \frac{1}{2}\right) - E_J.
\end{equation}
The quartic term then shifts the level spacings away from the harmonic-oscillator values. To leading order, the anharmonicity is
\begin{equation}
    \alpha \approx -E_C,
\end{equation}
and the transition frequency between the two lowest levels is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_b \approx \omega_p - E_C = \sqrt{8E_JE_C} - E_C.
\end{equation}
Because $|\alpha|/\omega_b \sim (E_C/E_J)^{1/2} \ll 1$, the transmon is only weakly anharmonic. Even so, the level spacing is sufficiently nonuniform that the lowest transition can be selectively addressed and used as an effective qubit.

\subsubsection{Flux-tunable transmon}

Replacing the single Josephson junction by a SQUID loop [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(d)] makes the Josephson energy tunable by an external magnetic flux. A SQUID consists of two Josephson junctions connected in parallel around a superconducting loop. A flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$ threading the loop imposes a phase bias between the two branches, so the total tunneling amplitude is the coherent sum of two paths. The result is a flux-dependent effective Josephson energy.

For junction energies $E_{J1}$ and $E_{J2}$, the SQUID behaves as a single junction with effective Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    = E_{J\Sigma}
    \sqrt{
        \cos^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
        + d^2 \sin^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    },
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_{J\Sigma} = E_{J1} + E_{J2},
    \qquad
    d = \frac{E_{J1} - E_{J2}}{E_{J1} + E_{J2}}.
\end{equation}
Here $d$ quantifies the junction asymmetry. When $d=0$, the SQUID is symmetric and the tunability is maximal. For $d>0$, the effective Josephson energy no longer vanishes at half a flux quantum, which reduces the tuning range but maintains a nonzero minimum frequency.

The transmon transition frequency therefore becomes flux dependent:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_b(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_C\,E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})} - E_C.
\end{equation}
At $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$, the Josephson energy is maximal and has zero slope with respect to flux. The qubit frequency is therefore first-order insensitive to small flux fluctuations at this point, which is the familiar \emph{flux sweet spot}. Away from the sweet spot, the device becomes more flux sensitive, trading noise robustness for tunability.

In practice, flux tuning provides direct control of the transmon spectrum. It can be used to adjust detunings, bring the qubit into or out of resonance with other circuit elements, and activate interactions that are otherwise off-resonant. Since $E_C$ is set primarily by the shunt capacitance, the anharmonicity remains approximately $\alpha \approx -E_C$ over the tuning range, while the transition frequency can vary by several GHz.

Time-dependent flux modulation extends this idea further and enables parametric control. By modulating $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$, one can generate sideband interactions between the transmon and coupled cavity modes. Analytical and numerical optimization of such parametric drives is discussed in Secs.~\ref{subsec:analytical-control} and \ref{subsec:numerical-control}.

\subsection{The Fluxonium}
\label{sec:fluxonium}

The transmon suppresses charge sensitivity by operating in the regime $E_J/E_C \gg 1$, but this same limit also makes the spectrum only weakly anharmonic. As systems scale up, the resulting small spacing between neighboring transitions increases the risk of leakage and makes selective control more difficult \cite{Koch2007,Sank2016}. Fluxonium addresses this trade-off by modifying the circuit topology itself: instead of shunting the Josephson junction only with a large capacitance, it also includes a large linear inductance. The resulting circuit accesses a qualitatively different regime of superconducting-qubit physics and was first proposed by Manucharyan \emph{et al.}~\cite{Manucharyan2009}.

The fluxonium Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C (n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos\varphi + \frac{1}{2} E_L (\varphi + \phi_e)^2,
    \label{eq:fluxonium_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $E_C$ is the charging energy, $E_J$ the Josephson energy, and
\begin{equation}
    E_L = \left(\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\right)^2 \frac{1}{L}
\end{equation}
is the inductive energy associated with the linear inductance $L$. The reduced external flux
\begin{equation}
    \phi_e = \frac{2\pi \Phi_e}{\Phi_0}
\end{equation}
biases the loop formed by the junction and the inductor. Relative to the transmon, the new ingredient is the quadratic inductive term, which competes with the Josephson cosine and qualitatively reshapes the potential landscape.

The inductive shunt also changes the circuit boundary conditions. In a Cooper-pair box or transmon, the two superconducting islands are connected only through the junction, so charge is transferred in discrete units and the phase is a compact variable. In fluxonium, the superinductor galvanically connects the two sides of the junction and removes this isolated-island structure. As a result, $\varphi$ may be treated as an ordinary unbounded operator and $n$ as its continuous conjugate variable, satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\varphi,n] = i.
\end{equation}
This change is more than formal: it underlies one of the defining advantages of fluxonium.

In particular, the offset charge $n_g$ can be removed by a unitary transformation,
\begin{equation}
    U(n_g) = e^{-i n_g \varphi},
\end{equation}
under which
\begin{equation}
    U n U^\dagger = n + n_g,
    \qquad
    U \varphi U^\dagger = \varphi,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    U H(n_g,\phi_e) U^\dagger = H(0,\phi_e).
\end{equation}
The spectrum is therefore independent of $n_g$: unlike the transmon, fluxonium is intrinsically insensitive to offset charge without requiring the large-$E_J/E_C$ limit.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fluxonium_hamiltonian} contains three energy scales---$E_C$, $E_J$, and $E_L$---together with the external flux $\phi_e$. Different ratios of these parameters produce qualitatively distinct regimes, all described by the same Hamiltonian but with different spectra and operating characteristics. Examples include heavy fluxonium \cite{Earnest2018,Zhang2021,Ficheux2021}, standard fluxonium \cite{Nguyen2019,Somoroff2023,Bao2022,Ding2023}, the inductively shunted transmon \cite{Hassani2023,Zobrist2026}, and integer fluxonium \cite{Mencia2024}. Rather than viewing these as unrelated devices, it is useful to regard them as different parameter regimes of a common circuit model.

For pedagogical concreteness, it is useful to focus on heavy fluxonium near half-integer flux, $\phi_e = \pi$, where the device most clearly illustrates the distinctive physics of the fluxonium design. At this bias point, the potential
\begin{equation}
    V(\varphi) = -E_J \cos\varphi + \frac{1}{2} E_L (\varphi + \pi)^2
\end{equation}
is a symmetric double well. The low-energy spectrum is then determined not by weak perturbations of a harmonic oscillator, as in the transmon, but by tunneling and interference between states associated with the two wells.

Away from the half-flux sweet spot, the double well is tilted and the low-lying eigenstates are localized predominantly in one well or the other. In this regime, the matrix elements $\langle g|\varphi|e\rangle$ and $\langle g|n|e\rangle$ can become strongly suppressed because the two states have little spatial overlap. The qubit frequency, however, is also highly sensitive to flux in this regime, since the relative depth of the two wells changes directly with $\phi_e$.

At the sweet spot $\phi_e = \pi$, the two lowest eigenstates are approximately symmetric and antisymmetric superpositions of wavefunctions in the two wells. This restores wavefunction overlap and changes the relevant transition matrix elements. At the same time, the transition frequency becomes first-order insensitive to flux,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \omega_{ge}}{\partial \phi_e} = 0,
\end{equation}
which is the fluxonium analogue of a sweet spot.

Several important consequences follow from this operating point. First, the qubit retains a large anharmonicity because the low-energy splitting is set by tunneling in the double-well potential rather than by a weak quartic correction proportional to $E_C$. Second, the matrix element $\langle g|n|e\rangle$ can remain small, which suppresses coupling to capacitive loss channels. Third, symmetry can suppress quasiparticle-induced transitions; in particular, the matrix element
\begin{equation}
    \langle g|\sin(\varphi/2)|e\rangle
\end{equation}
vanishes at the symmetric point.

Fluxonium therefore combines large anharmonicity with intrinsic immunity to offset charge. Near half flux, the symmetric double-well potential also provides a first-order flux-insensitive operating point.

\subsection{Bosonic Qubits}
\label{sec:bosonic_qubits}

The transmon and fluxonium encode quantum information in the lowest two levels of an anharmonic circuit. A different strategy is to encode information in the large Hilbert space of a harmonic oscillator, typically a superconducting microwave cavity. Because the cavity mode contains no Josephson junction, it can be much longer lived than junction-based qubits, particularly in three-dimensional cavity architectures \cite{Paik2011,Reagor2016,Milul2023}. Its dominant error channel is often single-photon loss, rather than the broader combination of relaxation, dephasing, and leakage that affects nonlinear qubits. This pronounced noise bias makes bosonic modes a natural platform for hardware-efficient quantum error correction, where the encoding is designed around the dominant physical error rather than attempting to suppress all errors at the hardware level \cite{Cai2021,Copetudo2024}.

The central idea of bosonic quantum error correction is to use the many-dimensional oscillator Hilbert space to encode a logical qubit redundantly, so that the dominant error produces a detectable, and in favorable cases correctable, signature. The need for such redundancy is easiest to see by comparison with an ordinary transmon qubit. If a transmon is measured in $|0\rangle$, one cannot tell from that final measurement alone whether the computation genuinely produced $|0\rangle$ or whether an intended $|1\rangle$ relaxed before readout. The error acts within the computational subspace itself and leaves no direct flag.

A simple bosonic encoding that avoids this problem is the dual-rail code,
\begin{equation}
    |0_L\rangle = |1,0\rangle, \qquad |1_L\rangle = |0,1\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the logical qubit is stored across two bosonic modes. A single-photon loss maps both logical basis states to the vacuum state $|0,0\rangle$, which lies outside the code space. Photon loss therefore becomes a detectable leakage event: by monitoring the total excitation number, one can identify that an error has occurred. Although the dual-rail code does not by itself recover the original superposition coefficients, it illustrates the basic bosonic-code principle: the enlarged Hilbert space is used so that the dominant error becomes visible rather than remaining hidden.

More structured encodings go further by arranging the logical states so that photon loss maps the code space into a distinguishable error space that still retains the encoded amplitudes. A simple example is the binomial code
\begin{equation}
    |0_L\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |4\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad |1_L\rangle = |2\rangle.
\end{equation}
Here the logical states occupy only even-photon-number Fock states. A single photon loss sends the code space into the odd-parity sector,
\begin{equation}
    a|0_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\,|3\rangle, \qquad a|1_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\,|1\rangle,
\end{equation}
so the loss event is revealed by a change in photon-number parity. Unlike the dual-rail case, the error states remain distinct and continue to carry the logical amplitudes. For an encoded state $|\psi_L\rangle = \alpha|0_L\rangle + \beta|1_L\rangle$,
\begin{equation}
    a|\psi_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\left(\alpha|3\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\right),
\end{equation}
so the coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are preserved. The binomial code therefore illustrates how the oscillator Hilbert space can be engineered so that the dominant physical error is not merely detectable, but in principle correctable.

Other bosonic encodings follow the same logic with different code structures and error models. Cat codes \cite{Mirrahimi2014,Grimm2020} use superpositions of coherent states and typically exploit photon-number parity as an error syndrome, while Gottesman--Kitaev--Preskill (GKP) states \cite{Gottesman2001,CampagneIbarcq2020} encode information in a grid-like structure in phase space. Despite these differences, the common principle is the same: the encoding is chosen to match the dominant noise channel of the bosonic mode.

A cavity driven only by a classical voltage source, however, remains a linear oscillator and from the vacuum can generate only Gaussian states, such as coherent states. Preparing the non-Gaussian states required for bosonic encoding---Fock-state superpositions, cat states, or GKP states---therefore requires coupling the cavity to a nonlinear ancilla, most commonly a transmon.

In the dispersive regime, this ancilla enables qubit-state-dependent operations on the cavity and provides the basis for universal bosonic control, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:analytical-control}. The same coupling, however, also transfers ancilla noise to the cavity: fluctuations in the transmon frequency produce stochastic phase accumulation of the cavity through the dispersive shift. This inherited dephasing degrades the noise bias that makes bosonic encodings attractive in the first place, converting an otherwise loss-dominated channel into one with an appreciable dephasing component. Mitigating this inherited dephasing is the subject of Chapter~\ref{chap:stark-sweet-spots}, where a weak off-resonant drive on the ancilla is used to restore the cavity's intrinsic noise bias.

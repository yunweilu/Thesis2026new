\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}
Quantum computing offers a route to problems---most notably the simulation of quantum systems---for which classical methods appear fundamentally inadequate. The state space of a generic quantum many-body system grows exponentially with system size, so a faithful classical description quickly becomes intractable; for example, specifying the state of only 50 qubits already requires $2^{50} \approx 10^{15}$ complex amplitudes \cite{Feynman1982,Lloyd1996}. This observation motivates quantum computation broadly, but in practice the usefulness of any hardware platform is determined by a more specific question: how accurately can its quantum states be controlled, and how long can they remain coherent? In superconducting circuits, these two requirements---high-fidelity control and long coherence---define the central engineering problem addressed in this thesis.

A practical quantum computer must be built from physical qubits that can be initialized, coherently manipulated, entangled, and measured with high fidelity while preserving coherence on timescales long compared with gate durations \cite{DiVincenzo2000,Ladd2010}. Among the leading platforms, superconducting circuits are especially attractive because they combine fast gate operations, flexible microwave control, and scalable fabrication based on mature lithographic techniques \cite{Krantz2019,Kjaergaard2020}. They have consequently become one of the most developed gate-based architectures for quantum information processing. This thesis therefore focuses on superconducting circuits as a platform in which both control design and coherence engineering can be studied in detail.

Superconducting qubits encode information in the quantized energy levels of circuits containing Josephson junctions \cite{Krantz2019,Kjaergaard2020}. The Josephson junction provides the nonlinearity required to make the spectrum anharmonic, so that the two lowest levels can be addressed as a qubit without resonantly exciting higher states. The central design trade-off is that stronger anharmonicity generally improves addressability and suppresses leakage, whereas reduced sensitivity to environmental fluctuations favors longer coherence. Different qubit designs resolve this tension differently.

The transmon has become the workhorse of superconducting quantum computing because it strongly suppresses charge sensitivity while retaining simple fabrication, reliable control, and high-fidelity readout \cite{Koch2007,Houck2009,Kjaergaard2020,Jiang2025}. Other modalities, including fluxonium and more strongly protected circuits, pursue larger anharmonicity or hardware-level noise suppression at the cost of greater complexity \cite{Manucharyan2009,Nguyen2019,Zhang2021,Kalashnikov2020,Gyenis2021}. In parallel, bosonic encodings in superconducting microwave cavities provide a complementary architecture in which information is stored in long-lived oscillator modes rather than directly in weakly anharmonic junction circuits \cite{Cai2021,Copetudo2024}. These cavities can achieve coherence times far exceeding those of typical transmons and naturally exhibit a strong noise bias dominated by photon loss \cite{Paik2011,Reagor2013,Reagor2016,Milul2023,Romanenko2020,Joshi2021}. This makes them attractive for bosonic quantum error correction, but their control still relies on nonlinear Josephson elements whose fluctuations can induce inherited cavity dephasing. High coherence therefore does not eliminate the control problem; it changes its form.

Despite major advances in coherence, the practical limit on quantum performance is often set by control fidelity. For near-term algorithms and threshold-based fault-tolerant architectures alike, usable circuit depth is bounded by the physical gate error budget \cite{Fowler2012SurfaceCodes,Campbell2017Roads}. For fault-tolerant schemes such as the surface code, this requires physical error rates below a threshold of order $10^{-2}$, with practical targets often substantially lower. In superconducting devices, gate errors arise both from decoherence during the gate, determined by the finite relaxation and dephasing times $T_1$ and $T_2$, and from coherent control imperfections caused by imperfect drive pulses. Simple analytic envelopes such as square or Gaussian pulses do not explicitly suppress leakage to noncomputational levels and become increasingly fragile in the presence of weak anharmonicity, spectral crowding, crosstalk, control-chain distortion, and strong-drive multiphoton processes \cite{Motzoi2009DRAG,Dumas2024}. As system size and control complexity grow, pulse design becomes a constrained dynamical optimization problem rather than an analytical one.

This motivates numerical optimal control \cite{Brif2010Control}. In this approach, one searches directly for control waveforms that maximize a fidelity functional while satisfying experimental constraints such as bandwidth, amplitude, gate time, and robustness. Among the available methods, gradient ascent pulse engineering (GRAPE) remains one of the most widely used frameworks because it provides an efficient gradient-based procedure for high-fidelity pulse synthesis \cite{Khaneja2005GRAPE,deFouquieres2011GRAPE}. In this thesis, GRAPE serves as the main framework for studying how pulse optimization can be scaled to larger superconducting systems and applied to realistic control problems.

Control optimization alone, however, cannot overcome the fidelity ceiling imposed by intrinsic decoherence. Coherence must also be engineered at the Hamiltonian level. In superconducting circuits, the measured values of $T_1$ and $T_2$ depend not only on materials and fabrication, but also on the operating point of the device, including its transition frequency, bias conditions, and drive environment \cite{Gyenis2021NoiseProtected,Koch2007}. Static sweet spots exploit this dependence by making the qubit frequency first-order insensitive to a dominant noise source. Under continuous or parametric driving, the relevant eigenstates become dressed by the drive, and their noise sensitivities are modified accordingly. This opens the possibility of dynamical sweet spots, at which appropriately chosen drive conditions suppress sensitivity to fluctuations even away from a static sweet spot \cite{Huang2021DynamicalSweetSpots,Valery2022DynamicalSweetSpot}. Continuous driving can therefore be used not only to implement gates, but also to reshape the noise landscape experienced by the system.

The main contributions of this thesis lie in two directions: optimal control and coherence engineering. The thesis is organized accordingly. Chapter~\ref{chap:sc-qubits} provides the necessary background on superconducting qubits, decoherence, and control. Chapter~\ref{chap:grape} develops the optimal-control results of this thesis. There, we compare three gradient-evaluation strategies---full automatic differentiation, semi-automatic differentiation, and hard-coded analytical gradients---and show that the convenience of automatic differentiation comes with substantial memory overhead from storing intermediate states and propagators. We then show that a hard-coded gradient formulation removes this storage bottleneck and, together with improved numerical propagation based on the scaling and squaring method and the auxiliary matrix method, reduces both runtime and memory overhead. Benchmarks for state-transfer and gate-synthesis problems, together with applications to robust transmon control, demonstrate how these methods extend optimal control to larger Hilbert spaces and provide practical guidance for choosing among gradient strategies.

Chapter~\ref{chap:stark-sweet-spots} presents the coherence-engineering results of this thesis in cavity-based superconducting architectures. Although high-$Q$ cavities can exhibit negligible intrinsic dephasing, their control commonly relies on nonlinear Josephson elements whose frequency fluctuations induce inherited cavity dephasing and can compromise the noise bias required for bosonic error correction. We show that a weak off-resonant drive on the nonlinear element generates an AC-Stark shift that cancels the corresponding Lamb-shift fluctuations, thereby creating a Stark-induced sweet spot. We analyze this mechanism in both the weak- and strong-drive regimes, derive analytical estimates for the residual dephasing rate, and validate them with Monte Carlo simulations of a 3D cavity dispersively coupled to a flux-tunable transmon subject to $1/f$ flux noise. The resulting protocol extends the cavity dephasing time by more than an order of magnitude and generalizes to multi-transition protection in binomial codes and multi-mode protection in dual-rail encodings. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the key findings of both parts of the thesis and discusses opportunities for future research

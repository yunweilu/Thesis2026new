\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis addressed two bottlenecks that become acute as
bosonic quantum processors move toward larger encodings and
multimode architectures: the computational cost of optimizing
control pulses in large Hilbert spaces, and the physical
degradation of cavity coherence through flux-noise-induced
dephasing. The two parts of the thesis developed complementary
solutions to these challenges.

\section*{Part I: Quantum Optimal Control for large system}

Numerical quantum optimal control (QOC) is the systematic
approach to finding microwave pulse sequences that implement
target operations on a quantum system while respecting
experimental constraints such as bandwidth limits, leakage
suppression, and crosstalk avoidance. Current state-of-the-art
QOC frameworks rely on automatic differentiation (AD) to compute
the required gradients. While flexible, AD works by recording
every intermediate operation during the forward propagation of
the quantum state and replaying that record in reverse to
accumulate gradients. In large Hilbert spaces this record becomes
prohibitively expensive to store, and memory rather than
computation time becomes the practical bottleneck.

This thesis revisited the gradient computation underlying the
GRAPE framework and derived analytically hard-coded gradients for
key cost functions arising in state-transfer and
gate-optimization problems. These gradients avoid storing the
full propagation history and were evaluated numerically using the
scaling-and-squaring method and the auxiliary matrix method,
culminating in a memory-efficient forward-backward propagation
scheme. A systematic scaling analysis compared the runtime and
memory usage of hard-coded gradients, full-AD, and semi-AD
approaches across problem sizes, with benchmark studies on both
state-transfer and gate-optimization tasks. A decision tree was
developed to guide practitioners in selecting the optimal
numerical strategy for a given problem. As a concrete
application, the GRAPE framework was used to design
detuning-robust control pulses for transmon gates; the resulting
pulses were benchmarked against conventional DRAG pulses and
pulses from Q-CTRL, demonstrating improved robustness to
qubit-frequency fluctuations.

\section*{Part II: Suppression of Flux-Noise-Induced Cavity
Dephasing}

Superconducting cavities are attractive for bosonic quantum error
correction precisely because their noise is strongly biased
toward photon loss, with dephasing playing a comparatively minor
role. This noise bias is the property that makes bosonic
encodings efficient: when photon loss is the dominant error,
increasing the encoding size can strongly reduce logical failure
rates. However, to enable universal cavity control beyond what
dispersive coupling alone provides, one must couple the cavity to
a flux-tunable nonlinear element. Making a circuit element
flux-tunable opens a pathway through which magnetic-flux noise in
the environment modulates the coupler parameters, which in turn
dephases the cavity. This inherited dephasing erodes the
noise-bias advantage that makes bosonic codes attractive in the
first place.

This thesis developed a complete theoretical framework for this
dephasing mechanism, tracing the noise transduction pathway from
flux fluctuations through the Lamb shift to the cavity frequency.
It then introduced a driven sweet-spot protocol in which an
off-resonant microwave drive on the transmon renders the cavity
frequency first-order insensitive to flux fluctuations, without
any hardware modifications. Analytical sweet-spot conditions were
derived and confirmed by Floquet numerics. An effective Lindblad
master equation in the drive frame was developed to identify the
competing dephasing channels under sweet-spot driving and derive
their scalings analytically. Full Monte Carlo simulations
validated the analytical predictions, and time-domain simulations
demonstrated an approximately twentyfold enhancement of the
cavity dephasing time in a representative device. Finally, the
protocol was applied directly to bosonic quantum error
correction: a single drive setting was shown to simultaneously
suppress flux-noise sensitivity across all relevant transitions
of a binomial code and to reduce dephasing in both rails of a
dual-rail qubit, with improvements confirmed experimentally via
Wigner tomography and logical-subspace tomography.

\section*{Outlook}

The two contributions of this thesis point toward a common
frontier. As bosonic encodings grow more powerful---spanning
larger Fock-space supports and entangling more cavity
modes---both the control and the coherence challenges addressed
here will intensify. On the control side, extending the
hard-coded gradient framework to multimode systems that explicitly penalize pulse duration would further improve the fidelity of bosonic
gate sequences against decoherence. On the coherence side, the driven sweet-spot
mechanism applies to any circuit in which a flux-tunable element
mediates a coupling; generalizing the analytical framework to
SNAIL-based couplers and to multimode architectures with several
flux-tunable elements is a natural next step. More broadly, the
combination of memory-efficient optimal control and dynamical
noise suppression provides a practical toolkit for pushing
cavity-based quantum processors toward the regime where logical
error rates fall well below the physical error rates of their
components.

\chapter{Bosonic control with ancilla and coupler}
\label{preliminary}

This chapter develops the control framework for bosonic modes in superconducting circuits. Throughout this chapter, we set $\hbar=1$, so energies and angular frequencies are used interchangeably unless stated otherwise. The discussion begins with the quantized LC oscillator, which provides the basic description of a cavity mode and clarifies the limitations of purely linear dynamics. It then introduces the transmon as a weakly anharmonic ancilla and shows how its dispersive coupling to the cavity enables conditional control, including ECD gates and arbitrary single-cavity unitaries. Finally, it introduces flux-mediated three-wave mixing, which provides beam-splitter and two-mode-squeezing interactions for entangling multiple bosonic modes.

\section{Oscillator Circuit Hamiltonian}
\label{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian}

The simplest circuit is the lossless LC oscillator, which serves as the foundational circuit element of superconducting quantum circuits. Its importance is twofold. First, it provides a direct correspondence between circuit variables and canonical variables in quantum mechanics. Second, it illustrates a fundamental limitation: a purely linear oscillator, even when driven by classical fields, cannot generate non-Gaussian states such as Fock states from the vacuum. This limitation motivates the introduction of a nonlinear element in the next section.

\subsection{Quantization of the LC oscillator}

Consider an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ connected in parallel [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(a)]. The natural generalized coordinate is the node flux
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} V(t')\,dt',
\end{equation}
where $V(t)$ is the node voltage. The conjugate variable is the node charge $Q$, which is associated with the capacitor branch. In terms of the flux coordinate, the classical Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\Phi,\dot{\Phi}) = \frac{C}{2}\dot{\Phi}^{2} - \frac{1}{2L}\Phi^{2}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the electrostatic energy stored in the capacitor, while the second term is the magnetic energy stored in the inductor.

The canonical momentum conjugate to $\Phi$ is
\begin{equation}
    Q \equiv \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\Phi}} = C\dot{\Phi}.
\end{equation}
This is precisely the capacitor charge. Performing the Legendre transform gives the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H = Q\dot{\Phi} - \mathcal{L}
      = \frac{Q^{2}}{2C} + \frac{\Phi^{2}}{2L}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the LC circuit is formally identical to a mechanical harmonic oscillator, with the correspondence
\begin{equation}
    \Phi \leftrightarrow x,
    \qquad
    Q \leftrightarrow p,
    \qquad
    C \leftrightarrow m,
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{L} \leftrightarrow m\omega^{2}.
\end{equation}
The resonance frequency is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\end{equation}

Canonical quantization promotes $\Phi$ and $Q$ to operators satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q}] = i.
\end{equation}
The quantum Hamiltonian is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{0} = \frac{\hat{Q}^{2}}{2C} + \frac{\hat{\Phi}^{2}}{2L}.
\end{equation}

It is convenient to introduce the characteristic impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}},
\end{equation}
and the zero-point fluctuations
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \sqrt{\frac{Z}{2}},
    \qquad
    Q_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2Z}}.
\end{equation}
The annihilation and creation operators are then defined through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Phi} = \Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}),
    \qquad
    \hat{Q} = -iQ_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}),
\end{equation}
with $[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}] = 1$. Substituting these expressions into $\hat{H}_{0}$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{0}
    =
    \omega_{0}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is the standard quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian.

The eigenstates of this Hamiltonian are the Fock states $\{\ket{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$, defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\ket{n} = n\ket{n}.
\end{equation}
These states have equally spaced energies,
\begin{equation}
    E_{n} = \omega_{0}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Because the level spacing is uniform, the linear oscillator does not distinguish one transition from another. This spectral degeneracy is the central obstacle to selective state preparation using only linear control.

\subsection{Classical voltage drive of a linear oscillator}

A classical voltage source couples linearly to a linear LC oscillator [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(b)]. In the circuit description, a drive voltage $V_d(t)$ applied through a capacitive coupling modifies the electrostatic energy and therefore adds a term linear in the node charge operator $\hat Q$. After quantization, $\hat Q$ is proportional to a linear combination of $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$, so the driven oscillator Hamiltonian can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \hat H(t)
    =
    \omega_0 \hat a^\dagger \hat a
    +
    f(t)\hat a
    +
    f^*(t)\hat a^\dagger,
\end{equation}
up to a constant energy offset, which shifts all levels equally and can be absorbed into the zero of energy. The precise form of $f(t)$ depends on the drive amplitude and coupling geometry.

The dynamics generated by this Hamiltonian can be treated exactly by moving to a displaced frame. Define
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\tilde \psi(t)}
    =
    \hat D^\dagger[\alpha(t)] \ket{\psi(t)},
    \qquad
    \hat D(\alpha)
    =
    \exp\!\left(\alpha \hat a^\dagger - \alpha^* \hat a\right).
\end{equation}
Substituting this transformation into the Schr\"odinger equation and requiring that the terms linear in $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$ cancel yields the equation of motion
\begin{equation}
    \dot \alpha(t)
    =
    -i\omega_0 \alpha(t) - i f^*(t).
\end{equation}
With this choice of $\alpha(t)$, the transformed Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm disp}(t)
    =
    \omega_0 \hat a^\dagger \hat a
    +
    E(t),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E(t)
    =
    \omega_0 |\alpha(t)|^2
    +
    f(t)\alpha(t)
    +
    f^*(t)\alpha^*(t)
    -
    \frac{i}{2}
    \bigl(\alpha^*(t)\dot\alpha(t) - \dot\alpha^*(t)\alpha(t)\bigr)
\end{equation}
is a real $c$-number. Being a $c$-number, it contributes only a global phase and can be ignored. The driven evolution is therefore exactly equivalent to the usual harmonic evolution generated by $\omega_0 \hat a^\dagger \hat a$, which produces phase-space rotation at angular frequency $\omega_0$, together with a displacement set by $\alpha(t)$.

In particular, if the oscillator starts in the vacuum state and $\alpha(0)=0$, then classical voltage drive maps vacuum only to a coherent state
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi(t)}
    =
    e^{-i\phi(t)} \ket{\alpha(t)}.
\end{equation}

This immediately explains why excited Fock states cannot be prepared from $\ket{0}$ by classical driving alone. In a perfectly harmonic oscillator, every adjacent transition has the same energy $\omega_0$, so a resonant linear drive cannot isolate only the $\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}$ transition without also coupling $\ket{1}\leftrightarrow\ket{2}$, $\ket{2}\leftrightarrow\ket{3}$, and so on. As a result, the drive produces phase-space motion rather than deterministic preparation of a specific number state.

This limitation motivates the introduction of anharmonicity. Once the level spacings become nonuniform, individual transitions can be spectrally resolved, and nonclassical states such as Fock states become accessible.

\section{Transmon as a Weakly Anharmonic System}
\label{sec:transmon}

The linear LC oscillator provides a clean starting point for circuit quantization, but its equally spaced spectrum prevents selective preparation of non-Gaussian states. A superconducting qubit requires a circuit element that breaks exact harmonicity. This role is played by the Josephson junction. When a junction is shunted by a large capacitance, the resulting device---the transmon---forms a weakly anharmonic oscillator whose lowest two levels can be isolated and used as an effective qubit.
\subsection{Josephson Hamiltonian}

A Josephson junction [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(c)] consists of two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier through which Cooper pairs tunnel coherently. Unlike a linear inductor, whose energy is quadratic in current, the tunneling energy depends periodically on the superconducting phase difference across the barrier. This nonlinear phase dependence is the origin of the anharmonicity that distinguishes a Josephson circuit from an LC oscillator. The critical current $I_c$ sets the maximum dissipationless current that the junction can support.

The junction is characterized by the nonlinear current--phase relation
\begin{equation}
    I = I_c \sin \varphi,
\end{equation}
and by the Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi},
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ is the superconducting phase difference across the junction and $\Phi_0 = h/2e$ is the flux quantum. The superconducting phase $\varphi$ and the Cooper-pair number $n$ are conjugate variables, analogous to position and momentum, reflecting the number--phase uncertainty of the condensate. Upon quantization they become operators satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\hat{\varphi},\hat{n}] = i.
\end{equation}
The charging energy is
\begin{equation}
    E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}.
\end{equation}
In general the circuit Hamiltonian includes an offset charge $n_g$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    4E_C (\hat n - n_g)^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
\end{equation}
Here $n_g$ accounts for stray static charges that shift the effective island charge. In the transmon regime $E_J \gg E_C$, the resulting charge dispersion is exponentially suppressed, so to leading order one may neglect $n_g$ and write
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
    \label{eq:JJ_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This Hamiltonian already shows the essential difference from the LC oscillator. The charging term is quadratic, but the Josephson potential is cosine rather than quadratic, and therefore introduces the nonlinearity required for anharmonic energy levels. The transmon is the most widely used realization of this circuit, obtained by operating in the regime where the Josephson energy greatly exceeds the charging energy.

\subsection{Transmon limit and weak anharmonicity}

The transmon operates in the regime
\begin{equation}
    E_J \gg E_C,
\end{equation}
where phase fluctuations are small and the cosine potential may be expanded around one of its minima, typically $\varphi = 0$:
\begin{equation}
    -E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}
    \approx
    -E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\hat{\varphi}^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\hat{\varphi}^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\hat{\varphi}^6).
\end{equation}
Substituting this into Eq.~\eqref{eq:JJ_hamiltonian} gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    \approx
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    +
    \frac{E_J}{2}\hat{\varphi}^2
    -
    \frac{E_J}{24}\hat{\varphi}^4
    -
    E_J.
\end{equation}
The first two terms define a harmonic oscillator, while the quartic correction produces a weak anharmonicity. To make this explicit, we introduce ladder operators through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\varphi} = \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger),
    \qquad
    \hat{n} = i n_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{b}^\dagger-\hat{b}),
\end{equation}
where the zero-point fluctuations are fixed by requiring the quadratic Hamiltonian to take the standard harmonic-oscillator form. This gives
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{2E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/4},
    \qquad
    n_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{E_J}{32E_C}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
The quadratic part then becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{harm}}
    =
    \omega_p
    \left(
        \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{b} + \frac{1}{2}
    \right)
    -
    E_J,
\end{equation}
with plasma frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \sqrt{8E_JE_C}.
\end{equation}
This is the Josephson analogue of the LC resonance frequency $1/\sqrt{LC}$, with the identification
\begin{equation}
    L_J = \frac{(\Phi_0/2\pi)^2}{E_J},
    \qquad
    C = C_\Sigma.
\end{equation}
The Josephson inductance $L_J$ is the effective inductance obtained by linearizing the junction about $\varphi=0$; unlike a geometric inductance, it is set by the junction parameters and can be tuned in flux-controlled circuits.
\subsection{Flux-tunable transmon}

Replacing a single Josephson junction by a SQUID loop [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(d)] makes the effective Josephson energy tunable by external magnetic flux. A SQUID consists of two Josephson junctions connected in parallel around a superconducting loop. An external flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$ threading the loop imposes a phase bias between the two junctions, so the total Josephson tunneling amplitude is the coherent sum of two paths. As a result, the effective Josephson energy varies periodically with flux through constructive or destructive interference.

For junction energies $E_{J1}$ and $E_{J2}$, the SQUID behaves as a single junction with flux-dependent effective Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    =
    E_{J\Sigma}
    \sqrt{
        \cos^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
        +
        d^2
        \sin^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    },
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_{J\Sigma} = E_{J1}+E_{J2},
    \qquad
    d = \frac{E_{J1}-E_{J2}}{E_{J1}+E_{J2}}.
\end{equation}
The parameter $d$ measures the junction asymmetry. For $d=0$, the SQUID is symmetric and the tunability is maximal. For $d>0$, the effective Josephson energy no longer vanishes at half a flux quantum, so the tuning range is reduced but the spectrum retains a nonzero minimum frequency.

The transmon transition frequency therefore acquires flux dependence,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_q(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_C E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})}
    -
    E_C.
\end{equation}
At $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$, the Josephson energy is maximal and has zero slope with respect to flux, so the qubit frequency is first-order insensitive to small flux fluctuations. This operating point is the flux sweet spot and minimizes dephasing from low-frequency flux noise. Away from the sweet spot, the qubit becomes increasingly flux sensitive, which enables tunability but also increases susceptibility to noise.

In practice, flux tuning allows the qubit frequency to be varied over a range of several GHz, while the anharmonicity remains approximately $\alpha \approx -E_C$ because $E_C$ is set primarily by the circuit capacitance and is largely independent of flux. External flux therefore provides direct control of the transmon spectrum: it can be used to bring the qubit into and out of resonance with other circuit elements, adjust detunings, and selectively activate interactions that are otherwise off resonant.

This flux tunability is also the basis of parametric control. By modulating $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$ in time, one can generate sideband interactions between the transmon and coupled cavity modes, including beam-splitter, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:three_wave_mixing_two_mode}.


\section{ECD Scheme for Arbitrary Single-Cavity Unitary}
\label{sec:ecd_single_cavity}

Section~\ref{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian} showed that a single linear cavity driven only by classical microwave fields remains confined to Gaussian dynamics. In particular, starting from vacuum, such control cannot produce Fock states or implement general non-Gaussian unitaries on the cavity mode. The transmon introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transmon} provides the missing nonlinearity. When the cavity is dispersively coupled to the transmon, the qubit can mediate conditional phase-space operations on the cavity, and these operations promote the cavity from a Gaussian system to a controllable bosonic mode. To formulate this control scheme correctly, however, one should begin from the full weakly anharmonic transmon ladder and only then reduce to an effective two-level ancilla.

\subsection{Dispersive cavity--transmon coupling}
\label{sec:ecd_dispersive_coupling}

The transmon is not fundamentally a two-level system, but a weakly anharmonic oscillator with eigenstates
\(\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0}^\infty\), obtained by diagonalizing the Josephson Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{T}}
    =
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
\end{equation}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:transmon}, its spectrum forms a ladder of levels with transition frequencies
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{j+1,j}
    =
    E_{j+1}-E_j
    \approx
    \omega_{01} + j\alpha,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha<0\) is the transmon anharmonicity. Because \(|\alpha|\) is small compared with \(\omega_{01}\), the transmon remains close to a harmonic oscillator, but its level spacings are not exactly equal. This weak anharmonicity is sufficient to isolate the lowest transition under appropriate control conditions.

Now couple the transmon capacitively to a single cavity mode of frequency \(\omega_c\), described by the annihilation operator \(\hat{a}\) [Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(e)]. In the transmon eigenbasis, the uncoupled Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_0
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty \omega_j \ket{j}\bra{j},
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_j\) are the transmon eigenenergies. The capacitive interaction arises through the transmon charge operator \(\hat{n}\), whose dominant matrix elements connect neighboring transmon levels. Quantizing of the circuit Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit_elements}(e) \cite{vool2017circuitquantization}, the coupled cavity--transmon system is therefore described by the generalized Jaynes--Cummings Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty \omega_j \ket{j}\bra{j}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty g_j
    \left(
        \hat{a}^\dagger \ket{j}\bra{j+1}
        +
        \hat{a}\ket{j+1}\bra{j}
    \right),
    \label{eq:multilevel_JC}
\end{equation}
where \(g_j\) is the cavity coupling matrix element for the \(\ket{j}\leftrightarrow\ket{j+1}\) transition. In the transmon regime, these couplings approximately scale as
\begin{equation}
    g_j \approx \sqrt{j+1}\,g_0,
\end{equation}
reflecting the near-oscillator character of the device.

In the dispersive regime, define
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_j = \omega_{j+1,j}-\omega_c
\end{equation}
as the signed detuning of the \(\ket{j}\leftrightarrow\ket{j+1}\) transition from the cavity. The dispersive condition is then
\begin{equation}
    |\Delta_j| \gg g_j\sqrt{n+1},
\end{equation}
for all significantly occupied photon numbers \(n\). In this limit, real exchange of excitations is suppressed, and the interaction can be treated perturbatively. To second order, the energy shift of the bare state \(\ket{j,n}\) is
\begin{equation}
    \delta E_{j,n}^{(2)}
    =
    \left[
        \frac{(n+1)g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}}
        -
        \frac{n g_j^2}{\Delta_j}
    \right],
\end{equation}
with the convention \(g_{-1}=0\). Separating the photon-number-independent part as a level shift and the photon-number-dependent part as a cavity pull,
\begin{equation}
    \delta E_{j,n}^{(2)} = \Lambda_j + n\,\chi_j,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_j = \frac{g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}},
    \qquad
    \chi_j
    =
    \frac{g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}}
    -
    \frac{g_j^2}{\Delta_j},
\end{equation}
one obtains the multilevel dispersive Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(\mathrm{multi})}
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty
    \left(\omega_j+\Lambda_j\right)\ket{j}\bra{j}
    +
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty
    \chi_j \ket{j}\bra{j},
    \label{eq:multilevel_disp}
\end{equation}
where \(\Lambda_j\) is the Lamb shift of transmon level \(j\) induced by its virtual coupling to the cavity. Equation~\eqref{eq:multilevel_disp} shows that each transmon level pulls the cavity by a different amount.

At this stage the ancilla is still a multilevel system. The reduction to an effective qubit is an approximation that must be justified. In the present setting, that approximation is valid for three reasons. First, the control pulses are designed to act resonantly on the \(\ket{g}\equiv\ket{0}\) to \(\ket{e}\equiv\ket{1}\) transition, while the next transition is detuned by the anharmonicity \(|\alpha|\). Second, the qubit-drive Rabi rate and spectral bandwidth are chosen small enough that off-resonant excitation of \(\ket{f}\equiv\ket{2}\) and higher states remains negligible. Third, the cavity population is kept below the critical photon number associated with the dispersive approximation, so that the cavity does not strongly hybridize with higher transmon levels. Under these conditions, population leakage out of \(\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}\) is weak, and the transmon can be treated as an effective two-level ancilla for gate synthesis.

Projecting Eq.~\eqref{eq:multilevel_disp} onto the lowest two transmon levels gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(ge)}
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    (\omega_g+\Lambda_g)\ket{g}\bra{g}
    +
    (\omega_e+\Lambda_e)\ket{e}\bra{e}
    +
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    \left(
        \chi_g \ket{g}\bra{g}
        +
        \chi_e \ket{e}\bra{e}
    \right).
\end{equation}
Introducing
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}_z = \ket{e}\bra{e} - \ket{g}\bra{g},
\end{equation}
the dressed qubit frequency
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\omega}_q
    =
    (\omega_e+\Lambda_e)-(\omega_g+\Lambda_g),
\end{equation}
the mean cavity pull
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\chi} = \frac{\chi_e+\chi_g}{2},
\end{equation}
and the differential cavity pull
\begin{equation}
    \chi = \chi_e-\chi_g,
\end{equation}
one obtains
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(ge)}
    =
    (\omega_c+\bar{\chi})\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \frac{\tilde{\omega}_q}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z.
    \label{eq:two_level_disp_from_multilevel}
\end{equation}
The first term renormalizes the cavity frequency and can be absorbed into a redefinition of \(\omega_c\). The last term is the effective dispersive interaction used throughout the ECD protocol.

For a weakly anharmonic transmon, Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_level_disp_from_multilevel} reduces to the familiar transmon dispersive shift. Since \(g_{-1}=0\), one has
\begin{equation}
    \chi_g = -\frac{g_0^2}{\Delta_0}.
\end{equation}
For the excited state,
\begin{equation}
    \chi_e = \frac{g_0^2}{\Delta_0} - \frac{g_1^2}{\Delta_1}.
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    \chi
    =
    \chi_e-\chi_g
    =
    \frac{2g_0^2}{\Delta_0} - \frac{g_1^2}{\Delta_1}.
\end{equation}
Using \(g_1 \approx \sqrt{2}\,g_0\) and \(\Delta_1=\Delta_0+\alpha\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
    \chi
    \approx
    2g_0^2
    \left(
        \frac{1}{\Delta_0}
        -
        \frac{1}{\Delta_0+\alpha}
    \right)
    =
    \frac{2g_0^2 \alpha}{\Delta_0(\Delta_0+\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
In typical circuit-QED devices, \(\chi/2\pi\) ranges from tens of kHz to several MHz, depending on the detuning \(\Delta_0\) and coupling \(g_0\). Thus, the standard qubit--cavity dispersive Hamiltonian is not imposed by assumption, but emerges as the low-energy reduction of the full multilevel cavity--transmon system.

The classical cavity and qubit drives used for bosonic control are then added as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
    =
    \epsilon^*(t)\hat{a}
    +
    \epsilon(t)\hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
    +
    \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+,
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon(t)\) is the cavity-drive envelope and \(\Omega(t)\) is the full complex qubit Rabi rate. Moving to a frame rotating at the dressed cavity frequency \(\omega_c+\bar{\chi}\) and the dressed qubit frequency \(\tilde{\omega}_q\), the free-evolution terms are removed and the controlled Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \epsilon^*(t)\hat{a}
    +
    \epsilon(t)\hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
    +
    \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Echoed conditional displacement}

The basic idea of ECD \cite{eickbusch2022ecd,campagne-ibarcq2020} is to use the dispersive interaction to convert an ordinary cavity drive into a qubit-state-dependent displacement. Introduce a time-dependent displacement operator
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}[\alpha(t)] = \exp\!\left[\alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger-\alpha^*(t)\hat{a}\right],
\end{equation}
and choose \(\alpha(t)\) so that the unconditional cavity drive is absorbed into the displaced frame. Using
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\hat{a}\hat{D}(\alpha) = \hat{a}+\alpha,
\end{equation}
the dispersive term transforms as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\,
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,
    \hat{D}(\alpha)
    =
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \alpha \hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \alpha^* \hat{a}
    +
    |\alpha|^2.
\end{equation}
The displaced-frame Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{H}
    =
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}
    \left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger + \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right)\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}|\alpha(t)|^2 \hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right].
    \label{eq:ecd_displaced}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ecd_displaced} contains three distinct qubit-dependent terms. The first term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z,
\end{equation}
is an unwanted conditional phase rotation of the cavity. The third term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}|\alpha(t)|^2 \hat{\sigma}_z,
\end{equation}
is an unwanted qubit \(Z\) rotation. The middle term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}
    \left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger + \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right)\hat{\sigma}_z,
    \label{eq:desired_conditional_drive}
\end{equation}
is the desired conditional linear drive on the cavity.

An echo sequence composed of two displacement segments separated by a qubit \(\pi\) pulse cancels the unwanted dispersive phase rotation and the qubit \(Z\) rotation while preserving the conditional linear drive. The resulting unitary is the echoed conditional displacement gate,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        \frac{1}{2}
        \left(
            \beta \hat{a}^{\dagger}
            -
            \beta^{*}\hat{a}
        \right)\hat{\sigma}_z
    \right],
\end{equation}
where the complex amplitude \(\beta\) is determined by the integrated drive envelope. This gate displaces the cavity in opposite directions depending on the state of the ancilla and is therefore the basic non-Gaussian entangling primitive used for bosonic control.

\subsection{Universal gate set for the joint qubit--cavity system}

With the ECD gate defined in the previous subsection, we now show that alternating ECD operations and single-qubit rotations form a universal gate set for the joint qubit--cavity system. The ECD gate is combined with arbitrary single-qubit rotations
\begin{equation}
    R(\theta,\phi)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        -i\frac{\theta}{2}
        \left(
            \cos\phi\,\hat{\sigma}_x
            +
            \sin\phi\,\hat{\sigma}_y
        \right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:single_qubit_rotation}
\end{equation}
A generic control sequence then has the form
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_N
    =
    R(\theta_N,\phi_N)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_N)
    \cdots
    R(\theta_2,\phi_2)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_2)\,
    R(\theta_1,\phi_1)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_1).
    \label{eq:ecd_circuit}
\end{equation}

To make the universality claim precise, it is useful to work at the Hamiltonian level. Universal control of the oscillator alone means the ability to synthesize unitaries generated by Hamiltonians that are polynomial in the quadratures \cite{eickbusch2022ecd,braunstein2005}
\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}^\dagger+\hat{a}),
    \qquad
    p = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}^\dagger-\hat{a}).
\end{equation}
For the joint qubit--cavity system, the natural extension is control over Hamiltonians of the form
\begin{equation}
    q^j p^k \sigma_i,
    \qquad
    j,k \in \mathbb{N}_0,
    \qquad
    \sigma_i \in \{I,\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\}.
\end{equation}
Throughout this subsection, monomials \(q^j p^k\) are written with all factors of \(q\) placed to the left of all factors of \(p\). Since \([q,p]=i\), alternative orderings differ only by lower-order terms, so this convention does not affect the Lie-algebra argument. On a finite-dimensional truncation of the cavity Hilbert space, these operators span the full operator algebra of the joint system.

The key control-theoretic fact is that, on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, a set of Hamiltonians is universal if the Lie algebra generated by the corresponding skew-Hermitian operators spans the full matrix algebra up to an overall phase. It therefore suffices to show that sums and commutators of the available generators produce all polynomial operators on the truncated joint space.

The role of the available gates is then straightforward. Arbitrary single-qubit rotations provide the generators \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_y\). Infinitesimal ECD pulses generate conditional displacements along arbitrary directions in phase space, and therefore provide Hamiltonians proportional to \(q\sigma_z\) and \(p\sigma_z\). The basic set of accessible generators is thus
\begin{equation}
    \mathfrak{g}_0
    =
    \{q\sigma_z,\; p\sigma_z,\; \sigma_x,\; \sigma_y\}.
\end{equation}

The key point is that sums and commutators of available generators can themselves be synthesized. For sufficiently small \(\delta t\),
\begin{equation}
    e^{-iA\delta t}e^{-iB\delta t}e^{iA\delta t}e^{iB\delta t}
    =
    e^{-[A,B]\delta t^2 + O(\delta t^3)},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    e^{-iA\delta t/2}e^{-iB\delta t}e^{-iA\delta t/2}
    =
    e^{-i(A+B)\delta t + O(\delta t^3)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, once two Hamiltonians \(A\) and \(B\) are available, one can generate both their sum and, up to a sign, their commutator to leading order; the opposite sign is obtained by reversing the pulse ordering. Repeated application of these identities closes the Lie algebra generated by \(\mathfrak{g}_0\).

Starting from \(\mathfrak{g}_0\), one immediately obtains the full set of linear qubit--oscillator generators. For example,
\begin{equation}
    [q\sigma_z,\sigma_x] = 2i\,q\sigma_y,
    \qquad
    [q\sigma_z,\sigma_y] = -2i\,q\sigma_x,
\end{equation}
and similarly
\begin{equation}
    [p\sigma_z,\sigma_x] = 2i\,p\sigma_y,
    \qquad
    [p\sigma_z,\sigma_y] = -2i\,p\sigma_x.
\end{equation}
Together with
\begin{equation}
    [\sigma_x,\sigma_y] = 2i\,\sigma_z,
\end{equation}
this shows that the Lie closure of the control set contains
\begin{equation}
    \{\sigma_i,\; q\sigma_i,\; p\sigma_i\},
    \qquad
    i\in\{x,y,z\}.
\end{equation}

Quadratic generators follow from commutators of these linear terms. For instance,
\begin{equation}
    [q\sigma_x,q\sigma_y]
    =
    q^2[\sigma_x,\sigma_y]
    =
    2i\,q^2\sigma_z,
\end{equation}
and likewise
\begin{equation}
    [p\sigma_x,p\sigma_y] = 2i\,p^2\sigma_z.
\end{equation}
Mixed quadratic terms are generated in the same way, for example through commutators such as
\begin{equation}
    [q\sigma_x,p\sigma_y]
    =
    i(qp+pq)\sigma_z.
\end{equation}
Since \([q,p]=i\), differences between symmetrized and ordered products only produce lower-order terms, which are already contained in the generated algebra. Hence all quadratic polynomial generators can be constructed.

This argument can then be iterated. Once polynomial generators of degree \(d\) are available, commutators with \(q\sigma_i\) or \(p\sigma_i\) generate degree-\((d+1)\) terms, again up to lower-order contributions fixed by \([q,p]=i\). By induction, the generated Lie algebra contains all operators of the form
\begin{equation}
    q^j p^k \sigma_i,
    \qquad
    \sigma_i\in\{\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\}.
\end{equation}
Terms without a Pauli operator are obtained as well. For example,
\begin{equation}
    [q^{j+1}p^k \sigma_z,\; p\sigma_z]
    =
    [q^{j+1}p^k,\; p]
    =
    i(j+1)q^j p^k,
\end{equation}
so polynomial operators proportional to the identity on the qubit are also generated.

Consequently, the Lie algebra generated by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}
    =
    \left\{
        \mathrm{ECD}(\beta),\,
        R(\theta,\phi)
    \right\}
\end{equation}
contains all polynomial operators on the joint qubit--cavity system. On any finite-dimensional truncation of the cavity Hilbert space, these operators span the full matrix algebra, and the corresponding unitaries can therefore be approximated arbitrarily well by sequences of ECD gates and single-qubit rotations. In this sense, \(\mathcal{G}\) forms a universal gate set for the joint cavity--qubit Hilbert space.

This universality is stronger than the Gaussian controllability discussed in Section~\ref{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian}. There, the accessible Hamiltonians generate only affine transformations of phase space, meaning displacements and linear transformations of the quadratures that preserve the Gaussian character of any input state. Here, the qubit-mediated ECD interaction enlarges the Lie algebra to include nonlinear polynomial operators, which is precisely what enables universal control of the truncated joint system.

\subsection{Synthesis of arbitrary cavity unitaries}

To implement a target cavity unitary \(\hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}\), one works in a truncated Fock space
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_d = \mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\dots,\ket{d-1}\},
\end{equation}
with \(d\) chosen so that Fock-state amplitudes outside \(\mathcal{H}_d\) remain negligible throughout the protocol. In practice, \(d\) is taken comfortably above the largest photon number with appreciable occupation; for a code space with mean photon number \(\bar{n}\), a common rule of thumb is \(d \gtrsim 2\bar{n}\), with a larger cutoff required when the control sequence transiently populates higher Fock levels.

The control sequence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ecd_circuit} is then optimized so that
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_N
    \bigl(
        \ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{g}
    \bigr)
    \approx
    \bigl(
        \hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}\ket{\psi}
    \bigr)
    \otimes
    \ket{g},
    \qquad
    \forall\,\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_d.
    \label{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition} expresses two requirements simultaneously: the cavity undergoes the desired unitary transformation for every input state in the truncated space, and the qubit is returned to its initial state so that no residual qubit--cavity entanglement remains.

A convenient way to express the optimization target is through the projected cavity map
\begin{equation}
    \hat{M}_N
    =
    (\mathbb{I}_d\otimes\bra{g})
    \hat{U}_N
    (\mathbb{I}_d\otimes\ket{g}),
\end{equation}
which describes the effective action of the sequence on the cavity when the qubit begins and ends in \(\ket{g}\). The synthesis condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition} is satisfied if and only if $\hat{M}_N = \hat{U}_{\text{cav}}$ up to a global phase, which motivates minimizing the following infidelity
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{gate}}
    =
    1
    -
    \frac{
        \left|
            \mathrm{Tr}
            \left(
                \hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}^\dagger \hat{M}_N
            \right)
        \right|^2
    }{d^2}.
    \label{eq:projected_gate_infidelity}
\end{equation}
When only a state-preparation task is required, one may instead minimize the state infidelity
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{state}}
    =
    1
    -
    \left|
        \bra{\psi_{\mathrm{tar}}}
        \hat{M}_N
        \ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}
    \right|^2.
\end{equation}

In practice, the parameters
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
        \beta_k,\theta_k,\phi_k
    \right\}_{k=1}^N
\end{equation}
are found by numerical minimization of the fidelity over the truncated Hilbert space. Because the gate set is universal, increasing the circuit depth \(N\) allows one, in principle, to approximate any desired single-cavity unitary to arbitrary accuracy on \(\mathcal{H}_d\).

This synthesis strategy provides a concrete route from a small set of experimentally natural primitives to general bosonic control. Fock-state preparation, cat-state generation, logical-state encoding, and more general non-Gaussian cavity gates all arise as special cases of the same framework. The essential point is that the cavity is not controlled directly through a native cavity nonlinearity; rather, the nonlinearity is borrowed from the transmon and converted, through the dispersive interaction and echoed conditional displacements, into programmable phase-space control.

The remainder of this chapter uses this viewpoint throughout. Once the cavity--qubit system is equipped with the gate set \(\{\mathrm{ECD}(\beta),R(\theta,\phi)\}\), single-mode bosonic control reduces to the problem of compiling the desired operation into an experimentally feasible sequence of echoed conditional displacements and qubit rotations.

\section{Two-mode entanglement}
\label{sec:two_mode_entanglement}

The previous section showed that the single-cavity ECD scheme provides universal control of a bosonic mode. For the two-mode problem, there are likewise two complementary routes. One can extend ECD to two cavity modes and obtain universal control in the two-mode Hilbert space. Alternatively, in the cavity--flux-tunable-transmon--cavity architecture considered here, one can use flux modulation of the intermediate nonlinear element to activate an effective interaction between the two cavities. We discuss these two approaches in turn.

\subsection{Two-mode ECD as a universal-control approach}
\label{sec:two_mode_ecd}

The ECD framework extends naturally from a single bosonic mode to two bosonic modes. In that setting, conditional displacements and qubit-assisted control operations can be used to realize universal control over the two-mode Hilbert space and, in particular, to prepare and manipulate entangled cavity states. This provides a conceptually direct generalization of the single-mode protocol discussed in the previous section. A detailed treatment of the two-mode ECD protocol is given in Ref.~\cite{You2024}.

\subsection{Parametrically activated two-mode interaction in a cavity--FTT--cavity system}
\label{sec:parametric_two_mode_control}

To generate a coherent interaction between two cavity modes, the relevant hardware is not a directly coupled cavity pair, but a cavity--coupler--cavity system. Here we use a flux-tunable transmon (FTT) as the coupler. The two cavity modes do not couple directly to each other. Instead, each cavity couples to the same FTT, which mediates the effective interaction.

\subsubsection{Starting Hamiltonian and flux-dependent transmon frequency}

We consider an FTT capacitively coupled to two cavities, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:twomode}. Let \(\hat a\) and \(\hat c\) denote the two linear cavity modes, and let \(\hat b\) denote the FTT mode. The circuit Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    \hat H
    =
    \hat H_{\mathrm{lin}}(t)
    +
    \hat H_{\mathrm{nl}},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\mathrm{lin}}(t)
    =
    \omega_a \hat a^\dagger \hat a
    +
    \omega_c \hat c^\dagger \hat c
    +
    \omega_q\!\left(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)\right)\hat b^\dagger \hat b
    +
    g_{ab}(\hat a^\dagger \hat b + \hat a \hat b^\dagger)
    +
    g_{bc}(\hat c^\dagger \hat b + \hat c \hat b^\dagger),
    \label{eq:Hlin_bare}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\mathrm{nl}}
    =
    -\frac{K_b}{2}\hat b^{\dagger 2}\hat b^2 .
    \label{eq:Hnl_bare}
\end{equation}
Here the coupling terms have already been written in the rotating-wave approximation, so the linear part consists of the bare mode energies together with Jaynes--Cummings interactions between each cavity and the FTT. Importantly, there is no direct term of the form
\(
g_{ac}(\hat a^\dagger \hat c + \hat a \hat c^\dagger)
\).

The external flux is taken to be
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
    =
    \Phi_{\mathrm{dc}}
    +
    \Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}\cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d),
    \label{eq:flux_drive}
\end{equation}
and the FTT frequency is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_q(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_C E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})}
    -
    E_C .
    \label{eq:omegaq_flux}
\end{equation}
Expanding around the dc bias point gives
\begin{equation}
    \omega_q\!\left(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)\right)
    \approx
    \bar\omega_q
    +
    \delta\omega_q \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d),
    \label{eq:omegaq_expand}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \bar\omega_q = \omega_q(\Phi_{\mathrm{dc}}),
    \qquad
    \delta\omega_q
    =
    \left.
    \frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \Phi}
    \right|_{\Phi_{\mathrm{dc}}}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{ac}} .
\end{equation}
Thus, an AC flux drive produces an AC modulation of the FTT frequency.

\subsubsection{Black-box quantization of the static quadratic sector}

The black-box quantization strategy treats the linear circuit as a passive network whose normal modes are found first, while the nonlinear elements are then added perturbatively on top of these dressed modes \cite{PhysRevLett.108.240502bbq}. In the present setting, it is therefore convenient to separate the Hamiltonian into a static quadratic part and a perturbation,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H
    =
    \hat H^{(0)}
    +
    \hat H^{(1)}(t),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \hat H^{(0)}
    =
    \omega_a \hat a^\dagger \hat a
    +
    \omega_c \hat c^\dagger \hat c
    +
    \bar\omega_q \hat b^\dagger \hat b
    +
    g_{ab}(\hat a^\dagger \hat b + \hat a \hat b^\dagger)
    +
    g_{bc}(\hat c^\dagger \hat b + \hat c \hat b^\dagger),
    \label{eq:H0_static}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \hat H^{(1)}(t)
    =
    -\frac{K_b}{2}\hat b^{\dagger 2}\hat b^2
    +
    \delta\omega_q \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)\,\hat b^\dagger \hat b .
    \label{eq:H1_pert}
\end{equation}
Diagonalizing only the static sector is advantageous because the result is a time-independent basis in which the drive and nonlinearity can be treated perturbatively; the drive frequency \(\omega_d\) then selects which normal-mode transition is activated.

Writing the normal-mode transformation as
\begin{equation}
    \hat \mu
    =
    \sum_{\nu=a,b,c}
    u_{\mu\nu}\hat \nu_{\mathrm N},
    \qquad
    \mu=a,b,c,
\end{equation}
one obtains
\begin{equation}
    \hat H^{(0)}
    =
    \tilde\omega_a \hat a_{\mathrm N}^\dagger \hat a_{\mathrm N}
    +
    \tilde\omega_b \hat b_{\mathrm N}^\dagger \hat b_{\mathrm N}
    +
    \tilde\omega_c \hat c_{\mathrm N}^\dagger \hat c_{\mathrm N} .
    \label{eq:H0_diag}
\end{equation}
Here \(u_{\mu\nu}\) is an element of the inverse diagonalization matrix, expressing bare mode \(\mu\) in terms of normal mode \(\nu\).

In the parameter regime of interest, two of these normal modes remain predominantly cavity-like and one remains predominantly transmon-like. For notational simplicity, we henceforth drop the subscript \(\mathrm N\) and write \(\hat a\), \(\hat b\), and \(\hat c\) for the dressed normal modes unless stated otherwise. In this basis,
\begin{equation}
    \hat b
    =
    \sum_{\nu=a,b,c}
    u_{b\nu}\hat \nu ,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \hat b^\dagger \hat b
    =
    \sum_{\mu,\nu=a,b,c}
    u_{b\mu}^* u_{b\nu}\,
    \hat \mu^\dagger \hat \nu .
\end{equation}
The perturbation therefore becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat H^{(1)}(t)
    \approx
    -\frac{\tilde K_b}{2}\hat b^{\dagger 2}\hat b^2
    +
    \delta\omega_q \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)
    \sum_{\mu,\nu=a,b,c}
    \eta_{\mu\nu}\hat \mu^\dagger \hat \nu ,
    \label{eq:H1_normalmode}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mu\nu} = u_{b\mu}^* u_{b\nu}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Drive-selected interaction and rotating-wave approximation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:H1_normalmode} contains both diagonal frequency shifts and off-diagonal mode-conversion terms. For the cavity-like modes, the relevant off-diagonal coefficient is
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{ac} = u_{ba}^* u_{bc},
\end{equation}
with \(\eta_{ca}=\eta_{ac}^*\). The corresponding drive-induced interaction is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \hat H^{(1)}_{ac}(t)
    =
    \delta\omega_q \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)
    \left(
        \eta_{ac}\hat a^\dagger \hat c
        +
        \eta_{ac}^*\hat a \hat c^\dagger
    \right).
    \label{eq:Hac_drive}
\end{equation}
Writing
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{ac}=|\eta_{ac}|e^{i\theta_{ac}},
\end{equation}
we define the real modulation amplitude
\begin{equation}
    \delta g_{ac}=\delta\omega_q|\eta_{ac}|,
\end{equation}
and absorb the hybridization phase \(\theta_{ac}\) into an effective drive phase,
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=\phi_d+\theta_{ac}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Hac_drive} can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    \hat H^{(1)}_{ac}(t)
    =
    \delta g_{ac}\cos(\omega_d t+\phi_{\mathrm{eff}})
    \left(
        \hat a^\dagger \hat c + \hat a \hat c^\dagger
    \right).
\end{equation}

Moving to the interaction picture with respect to
\(
\tilde\omega_a \hat a^\dagger \hat a
+
\tilde\omega_c \hat c^\dagger \hat c
\),
the interaction becomes
\begin{align}
    \hat H_{I,ac}(t)
    =
    \frac{\delta g_{ac}}{2}
    \Big[
        &
        \hat a^\dagger \hat c\,
        e^{-i(\tilde\omega_c-\tilde\omega_a-\omega_d)t+i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}
        +
        \hat a \hat c^\dagger\,
        e^{+i(\tilde\omega_c-\tilde\omega_a-\omega_d)t-i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}
        \nonumber\\
        &
        +
        \hat a^\dagger \hat c\,
        e^{-i(\tilde\omega_c-\tilde\omega_a+\omega_d)t-i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}
        +
        \hat a \hat c^\dagger\,
        e^{+i(\tilde\omega_c-\tilde\omega_a+\omega_d)t+i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}
    \Big].
    \label{eq:Hac_interaction}
\end{align}
Choosing the drive frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_d = \tilde\omega_c-\tilde\omega_a
\end{equation}
makes the first pair of terms stationary, while the remaining terms oscillate at frequency \(2\omega_d\) and are dropped under the rotating-wave approximation. The RWA is valid when
\begin{equation}
    2\omega_d \gg G_{ac},
\end{equation}
so that the off-resonant terms average out on the interaction timescale. The effective Hamiltonian is then
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\mathrm{BS}}
    =
    G_{ac}
    \left(
        e^{+i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}\hat a^\dagger \hat c
        +
        e^{-i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}\hat a \hat c^\dagger
    \right),
    \label{eq:HBS_ac}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    G_{ac} = \frac{\delta g_{ac}}{2}.
\end{equation}

This is the parametrically activated beam-splitter interaction between the two cavity-like modes. Its physical origin is explicit: the two cavities are not directly coupled; the interaction is mediated by the FTT, whose AC-flux-modulated frequency produces the resonant mode-conversion term after diagonalization of the static linear sector and application of the rotating-wave approximation.

\chapter{GRAPE: Theory, Gradients, and Implementation}
\label{chap:grape}

One of the prominent techniques for implementing quantum optimal control is Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering (GRAPE). The central goal of optimal control is to adjust control parameters in such a way that the infidelity of the desired state transfer or gate operation is minimized. In GRAPE, the minimization is based on gradient descent and thus generally requires the evaluation of gradients. Automatic differentiation (AD) \cite{baydin2018automatic} is a convenient tool that has also made an impact on quantum optimal control with GRAPE \cite{pacleung2017speedup,pacqoc,abdelhafez2019gradient,grapead}.
Internally, AD builds a computational graph and applies the chain rule to automatically compute the gradient of a given function. 

However, the convenience of AD comes at the cost of memory required for storing the full computational graph. The use of semi-automatic differentiation (semi-AD) \cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum} partially reduces the memory overhead compared to full automatic differentiation (full-AD). This reduction can still be insufficient to tackle optimal control for quantum systems of rapidly growing size \cite{arute2019quantum,gong2021quantum}. This motivates us to revisit the original GRAPE scheme \cite{nmrkhaneja2005optimal} which is based on hard-coded gradients (HG), and has the smallest possible memory cost. We perform a scaling analysis of memory usage and runtime, and thereby develop a decision tree guiding the optimal choice of strategy among HG, semi-AD and full-AD. We exemplify the scaling behavior with benchmarks for concrete optimization tasks.

The outline of this chaper is as follows. Sec.\ \ref{sec:grape-theory} briefly reviews GRAPE and introduces necessary notation. In Sec.\ \ref{sec:gradient-strategies}, we review three strategies of gradient computation: full automatic differentiation, semi-automatic differentiation and hard-coded gradients. Sec.\ \ref{sec:numerical-gradients}illustrate derivation and memory-efficient implementation of the gradients for the most commonly used cost function contributions. Our numerical implementation of HG further improves the efficiency of state propagation. In Sec.\ \ref{sec:scaling-analysis}, we analyze and compare the scaling of runtime and memory usage scaling for HG, AD and semi-AD. Sec.\ \ref{sec:benchmark-studies}present results from concrete benchmark studies. In Sec.\ \ref{sec:decision-tree}, we formulate a decision tree that facilitates the choice of optimal numerical strategy for GRAPE-based quantum optimal control. 
\section{Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering: Theory and Notation}
\label{sec:grape-theory}
We briefly sketch the basics of GRAPE \cite{nmrkhaneja2005optimal}, mainly to establish the notion used in subsequent sections. 
\subsection{Pulse Parametrization and Time Discretization}
\label{subsec:pulse-param}
Consider a system described by the Hamiltonian
\begin{align}
     H(t) = H_s + a(t)\, h_c.
\end{align}
Here, $H_s$ denotes the static system Hamiltonian and $h_c$ is an operator coupling the classical control field $a(t)$ to the system\footnote{For simplicity, we consider one control channel with real-valued $a(t)$. Generalizations to multiple control channels and complex $a$ are straightforward.}. Quantum optimal control aims to adjust $a(t)$ such that a desired unitary gate or state transfer is performed within a given time interval $0\le t \le T$. To facilitate optimization, the total control time $T$ is divided into $N$ intervals of duration $\Delta t=T/N$, and the control field is specified by the amplitudes at the discrete times $t_n=n\Delta t$ ($n=1,2,\dots,N$). The discretization interval $\Delta t$ is chosen such that control amplitudes are approximately constant within each $\Delta t$. This treatment is actually quite close to modeling the actual drive output of an arbitrary waveform generator, where $\Delta t$ can be chosen to align with the available resolution. We denote the time-discretized values by
\begin{align}
\label{eq2}
    a_n \coloneqq a(t_n),
\end{align}
and group these to form the vector $\textbf{a}\in \mathbb{R}^{N}$.  
Under the influence of this piecewise-constant control field, the system's quantum state $\dket{\psi_n}\coloneqq \dket{\psi(t_n)}$ evolves by a single time step according to 
\begin{align}
\label{statepropagation}
  |\psi_n\rangle=U_n|\psi_{n-1}\rangle.
\end{align}
Here, $U_n$ is the short-time propagator
\begin{align}
\label{propagator}
  U_n\coloneqq U(t_n, t_{n-1}) =e^{-iH_n\Delta t}\qquad(\hbar=1),
\end{align}
where $H_n=\, H_s+a_n\, h_c $. 

\subsection{Cost Functions: State-Transfer Infidelity, Gate Infidelity, and Additional Contributions}
\label{subsec: cost functions}
Optimization of the control field proceeds via minimization of a cost function $C$. Typically, $C$ includes the state-transfer or gate infidelity, along with additional cost contributions employed to moderate pulse characteristics such as maximal power or bandwidth. Here we introduce the cost functions studied in this thesis.

For state-transfer problems, a central objective is to minimize the final-time state-transfer infidelity
\begin{equation}
\label{C1st}
C_1^{\mathrm{st}}
=
1-\left|\left\langle \phi_T \middle| \psi_N \right\rangle\right|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\dket{\phi_T}$ is the target state and $\dket{\psi_N}$ is the realized state at the final time $t_N$.

For gate optimization, the corresponding objective is the gate infidelity
\begin{equation}
C_1^{g}
=
1-\left|\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_T^\dagger U_R\right)/d\right|^2,
\end{equation}
where $U_T$ is the target unitary gate, $U_R=U_N\cdots U_1$ is the realized gate, and $d$ is the Hilbert-space dimension.

In addition to these final-time objectives, we also consider cost contributions that depend on the entire trajectory. One example is
\begin{equation}
\label{c2st}
C_2^{\mathrm{st}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N \dbra{\psi_{n'}}\Omega \dket{\psi_{n'}},
\end{equation}
which penalizes the integrated expectation value of a Hermitian operator $\Omega$. Depending on the choice of $\Omega$, this term can be used to suppress occupation of unwanted states or lossy subspaces.

Another useful contribution is
\begin{equation}
C_3^{\mathrm{st}}
=
1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\left\langle \phi_T \middle| \psi_{n'} \right\rangle
\right|^2.
\end{equation}
Unlike $C_1^{\mathrm{st}}$, which only measures the final-time infidelity, $C_3^{\mathrm{st}}$ accumulates the infidelity over all time steps. This term encourages the system to approach the target state earlier in the evolution and can therefore favor shorter state-transfer times.

For gate operations, the analogous trajectory-dependent cost is
\begin{equation}
C_3^{g}
=
1-\frac{1}{N d^2}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_T^\dagger U_{n',1}\right)
\right|^2,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
U_{n',1}=U_{n'}\cdots U_1
\end{equation}
is the propagator from the initial time to time step $n'$. This cost plays the same role as $C_3^{\mathrm{st}}$, but for gate synthesis: it promotes convergence toward the target gate throughout the pulse rather than only at the final time.

 
\subsection{Pulse Optimization Based on Gradient Information}
\label{subsec:pulse-opt-gradient}

After choosing cost functions of interest, a composite cost function $C(\textbf{a})=\sum_{\nu}\alpha_\nu C_\nu \parenthesis{\textbf{a}}$ is constructed, , where $\alpha_\nu$ are empirically chosen weight factors and $C_\nu$ are individual cost contributions. GRAPE utilizes the method of steepest descent to minimize $C(\textbf{a})$ by updating the control amplitudes according to the cost function gradient: 
\begin{align}
  \textbf{a}^{(j+1)}=\textbf{a}^{(j)}-\eta_j\,\nabla_{\textbf{a}}C(\textbf{a}^{(j)}).
\end{align}
Here, $j$ enumerates steepest-descent iterations and $\eta_j>0$ is the learning rate governing the step size.

\subsection{Hard-Coded Gradients: Analytical Derivation}
\label{subsec:hard-coded-gradients}
The convenience of AD is bought at the cost of significant memory consumption
which can be prohibitive for large quantum systems. Therefore, we are motivated to revisit hard-coded gradients
in a computationally efficient scheme for key cost contributions.
We now present analytical gradient expressions for the cost functions introduced in Sec\ \ref{subsec: cost functions}.

For the state-transfer infidelity $C_1^{\mathrm{st}}$, the gradient is
\begin{align}
\label{stgradient}
\frac{\partial C_1^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial a_n}
&=
-\left\langle \phi_T \middle| U_N\cdots \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n}\cdots U_1 \middle| \psi_0 \right\rangle
\left\langle \psi_N \middle| \phi_T \right\rangle
-\mathrm{c.c.}
\nonumber\\
&=
-\left\langle \phi_n \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1} \right\rangle
\left\langle \psi_N \middle| \phi_T \right\rangle
-\mathrm{c.c.},
\end{align}
where the backward-propagated state $\dket{\phi_n}$ is defined recursively by
\begin{equation}
\dket{\phi_n}=U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\phi_{n+1}},
\end{equation}
with terminal condition $\dket{\phi_N}=\dket{\phi_T}$. This is the standard backward-propagation construction.

For gate optimization, consider an orthonormal basis $\{\dket{\psi_0^h}\}_h$, where $h$ labels the basis states, and define
\begin{equation}
\dket{\psi_n^h}=U_n\cdots U_1 \dket{\psi_0^h}.
\end{equation}
Then the gradient of the gate infidelity $C_1^g$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{gategradient}
\frac{\partial C_1^g}{\partial a_n}
=
-\frac{1}{d^2}
\sum_h
\left\langle \phi_n^h \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1}^h \right\rangle
\sum_{h'}
\left\langle \psi_N^{h'} \middle| \phi_N^{h'} \right\rangle
-\mathrm{c.c.},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\dket{\phi_n^h}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_N^\dagger U_T \dket{\psi_0^h}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\dket{\phi_n^h}$ is obtained by applying the target unitary $U_T$ to the basis state $\dket{\psi_0^h}$ and then backward propagating the result to time step $n$.

We next turn to the additional cost contributions. For
\begin{equation}
C_2^{\mathrm{st}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N \dbra{\psi_{n'}}\Omega \dket{\psi_{n'}},
\end{equation}
the gradient is
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial C_2^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial a_n}
&=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\dbra{\psi_{n'}}\Omega
\frac{\partial}{\partial a_n}
\prod_{n''=1}^{n'} U_{n''}\dket{\psi_0}
+\mathrm{c.c.}
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{N}
\left(
\sum_{n'=n}^N
\dbra{\psi_{n'}}
\Omega
U_{n'}\cdots U_{n+1}
\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n}
\dket{\psi_{n-1}}
\right)
+\mathrm{c.c.}
\nonumber\\
\label{C2stgrad}
&=
\frac{2}{N}
\Real\left(
\left\langle \Phi_n \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1} \right\rangle
\right),
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_n}
:=
\sum_{n'=n}^N
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_{n'}^\dagger \,
\Omega \dket{\psi_{n'}}.
\end{equation}
The vector $\dket{\Phi_n}$ satisfies the recursion relation
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_n}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\Phi_{n+1}}
+
\Omega \dket{\psi_n}.
\end{equation}
This recursive form allows Eq.~\eqref{C2stgrad} to be evaluated efficiently using a forward-backward scheme.

For the trajectory-dependent state-transfer cost
\begin{equation}
C_3^{\mathrm{st}}
=
1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\left\langle \phi_T \middle| \psi_{n'} \right\rangle
\right|^2,
\end{equation}
the gradient is
\begin{equation}
\label{state time gradient}
\frac{\partial C_3^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial a_n}
=
-\frac{2}{N}
\Real\left(
\left\langle \Phi_{T,n} \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1} \right\rangle
\right),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}}
:=
\sum_{n'=n}^N
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_{n'}^\dagger
\dket{\phi_T}
\langle \phi_T | \psi_{n'} \rangle.
\end{equation}
This vector obeys the recursion relation
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\Phi_{T,n+1}}
+
\dket{\phi_T}\langle \phi_T | \psi_n \rangle,
\end{equation}
which again leads to a forward-backward evaluation scheme.

Finally, for the gate analogue
\begin{equation}
C_3^{g}
=
1-\frac{1}{N d^2}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_T^\dagger U_{n',1}\right)
\right|^2,
\end{equation}
the gradient becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C_3^g}{\partial a_n}
=
-\frac{2}{N d^2}
\sum_{h=1}^d
\Real\left(
\left\langle \Phi_{T,n}^h \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1}^h \right\rangle
\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}^h}
:=
\sum_{n'=n}^N
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_{n'}^\dagger
U_T \dket{\psi_0^h}\,
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_{n',1}U_T^\dagger\right).
\end{equation}
The corresponding recursion relation is
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}^h}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\Phi_{T,n+1}^h}
+
U_T \dket{\psi_0^h}\,
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_{n,1}U_T^\dagger\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, all gradients considered here can be evaluated with closely related forward-backward propagation schemes.

\section{Numerical Evaluation of Hard-Coded Gradients}
\label{sec:numerical-gradients}
The analytical gradient expressions, such as $\nabla C_1^{st}$ in Eq.\ \eqref{stgradient}, are generally complicated and involve a large number of contributing quantities. Depending on grouping and ordering of operations, these quantities may need to be stored simultaneously or computed repeatedly with critical implications for runtime and memory usage. The scheme originally proposed by Khaneja\ \textit{et al.}\ \cite{nmrkhaneja2005optimal} strikes a favorable compromise that avoids the proliferation of stored intermediate state vectors. More specifically, the final quantum state $\dket{\psi_N}$ is obtained by forward propagating $\dket{\psi_0}$. Then, backward propagation of $\dket{\psi_N},\dket{\phi_T}$ is carried out to calculate the matrix element $\langle\phi_{n}|\frac {\partial U_n }{\partial a_{n}}|\psi_{n-1}\rangle$ needed for the $n$-th component of the gradient, thus building up the gradient in descending order. For both propagation directions, only the current intermediate states are stored, leading to a memory cost of $\Theta(1)$. \footnote{Here, $f(x)=\Theta
(g(x))$ is the usual Bachmann–Landau notation for $f(x)$ bounded above and below by $g(x)$ in the asymptotic sense.}
The scheme is illustrated by Fig.\ \ref{fig:GRAPE}.

The calculation of gradients in Eqs.\ \eqref{stgradient} and \eqref{gategradient} requires numerical evaluation of expressions of the form $U_n\dket{\Psi}$  and $\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}$. 
Here, the short-time propagator $U_n$ is given by a matrix exponential $e^A$ and $A=-iH_n\Delta t$. 

\subsection{State Propagation via the Scaling and Squaring Method}
\label{subsec:scaling-squaring}

Scaling and squaring~\cite{codenotti1992error} is based on the identity
\begin{align}
\label{eq:scaling_squaring}
   e^A=\big(e^{A/s}\big)^s,
\end{align}
where the right-hand side matrix exponential now involves the matrix $B=A/s$ which has a norm that is reduced compared to $\norm{A}$. This generally allows for a lower truncation order $m $ of the exponential series\footnote{While Chebyshev expansion has faster convergence than Taylor expansion, their numerical implementations have the same runtime scaling. Here, I focus on Taylor expansion due to its simplicity.}, 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:eBm}
    e^B\approx e^B_m:=\sum_{q=0}^m\frac{ B^q}{q!}.
\end{align}
Typically, $m$ is smaller than the one required for the original $e^A$.
The goal is to approximate the state vector $e^A \dket{\Psi}\approx (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}$ where 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eBmpsi}
   e^B_m\dket{\Psi}= \parenthesis{1+B+\frac{B^2}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{B^m}{m!}}\dket{\Psi}
   =\dket{\Psi}+B\dket{\Psi}+\frac{B\parenthesis{B\dket{\Psi}}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{B\parenthesis{\cdots \parenthesis{B\dket{\Psi}}}}{m!}\text{   . }
\end{equation}
According to the last expression, approximation of $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ can thus proceed by repeated application of $B=A/s$ to a vector, without ever invoking matrix-matrix multiplication. This boosts runtime performance from $O(d^3)$ to $O(d^2)$.

To proceed with the evaluation of $e^A\dket{\Psi}$, the truncation order $m$ and scaling factor $s$ are determined in such a way that the error remains below the error tolerance $\tau$, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:error_def}
    \varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}(m,s) := \frac{\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-\llbracket (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}\rrbracket}}{\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}}}< \tau .
\end{equation}
Here, $\llbracket \bullet{}  \rrbracket$ denotes the floating point representation of $\bullet$. Since this error $\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}$ is difficult and costly to evaluate, I instead derive an upper bound $E_A(m,s)$ and resort to the inequality 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:accuracy_inequality}
    \varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}(m,s)<E_A(m,s)\leq \tau.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Bounding Numerical Errors}
\label{subsubsec:ss-bounding-errors}
The approximation of $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ required the determination of an upper bound $E_A(m,s)$ for the error $\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}(m,s)$ [Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_def}]. Here, I show how to acquire such bound.

Considering the expression for the error [Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_def}], the denominator is simply 1. The error $\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}$ is bounded as follows:
\begin{align}
\nonumber    &\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}
    =\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}+(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}-\llbracket (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}\rrbracket}_2\\
\nonumber    &\quad \leq \underbrace{\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}}_2}_{\varepsilon_t}+\underbrace{\norm{(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}-\llbracket (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}\rrbracket}_2}_{\varepsilon_r}.\\
\label{eq:epsilonA}
\end{align}
The error $\varepsilon_t$ arises from the truncation of the Taylor series and rounding error $\varepsilon_r$ is due to finite-precision arithmetics. I proceed to derive the upper bounds for these two errors separately.

\paragraph{Upper bound for the truncation error \texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon_t$}{epsilon\_t}.}
The truncation error can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_t = \norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-(e^B-R_m(B))^s\dket{\Psi}}_2=  \norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!} (-R_m(B))^i(e^B)^{s-i}\dket{\Psi}}_2,
\end{equation}
where $R_m(B)=\sum_{q=m+1}^{\infty}B^q/q!$ denotes the matrix-valued error of the truncated exponential series.
Employing the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity~\cite{higham2002accuracy}, one finds the bound
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_t \leq 
    \sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!}\big(R_m(\norm{B}_2)\big)^i\norm{e^B}_2^{s-i}\norm{\dket{\Psi}}_2=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!}\big(R_m(\norm{B}_2)\big)^i.
\end{equation}
Here the second equality utilizes the fact that the state is normalized and $e^B$ is unitary. 

This upper bound must be evaluated numerically to determine $m$ and $s$. However, computing the matrix 2-norm is inconvenient since it requires the use of an eigensolver. To mitigate this, I switch to the matrix 1-norm by exploiting\footnote{Generally, $\norm{B}_2\leq\sqrt{\norm{B}_\infty\norm{B}_1}$~\cite{wilkinson1994rounding} holds true for any $B$. When $B$ is anti-Hermitian, $\norm{B}_\infty=\norm{B}_1$ leads to the inequality in Eq.~\eqref{eq:norm_inequality}.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:norm_inequality}
    \norm{B}_2\leq\norm{B}_1.
\end{equation}
Monotonicity of $R_m$ leads to
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:truncation}
    \varepsilon_t \leq 
    \sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!}\big(R_m(\norm{B}_1)\big)^i
    < sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\frac{1-\big(sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\big)^s}{1-sR_m(\norm{B}_1)}.
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Upper bound for the rounding error \texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon_r$}{epsilon\_r}.}
\label{par:rounding_error}

The rounding error $\varepsilon_r$ originates from the finite precision of the floating-point representation of real numbers as well as the finite accuracy of basic arithmetic operations. The floating-point representation  $\llbracket \xi \rrbracket$ for the real number $\xi $ can be written as $\llbracket \xi \rrbracket=\xi(1+\delta)$ where $\delta=\delta(\xi)$ is the relative deviation~\cite{higham2002accuracy}. Its magnitude is always smaller than machine precision, i.e.\ $|\delta|<u$. Similarly, the complex number $z$ obeys 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:representation_error}
    \llbracket z\rrbracket=z(1+\delta)
\end{equation}
with $|\delta|\leq u$. Arithmetic operations such as addition and multiplication with two complex numbers $z_1, z_2$ incur a relative deviation $\delta=\delta(z_1,z_2)$ such that 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:operation_error}
    \llbracket z_1 \text{ + } z_2\rrbracket =(\llbracket z_1 \rrbracket+\llbracket z_2\rrbracket)(1+\delta),\ |\delta|\leq u, \quad\llbracket z_1 z_2 \rrbracket=\llbracket z_1\rrbracket \llbracket z_2\rrbracket(1+\delta),\ |\delta|\leq u',
\end{equation}
where $u':=2\sqrt{2}u/(1-2u)$~\cite{higham2002accuracy}. The relative deviation shown in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:representation_error}, \eqref{eq:operation_error} generally depend on both numbers involved as well as the operation in question. In the following, different $\delta$ are distinguished with subscript $\nu$.

Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:representation_error}, \eqref{eq:operation_error}, I derive an upper bound for the rounding error in the evaluation of the dot product, which is the basic arithmetic operation in evaluating $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:eBmpsi}.

\subparagraph{Upper bound for the rounding error associated with evaluating the dot product.}
The rounding error incurred when evaluating the dot product is given by $|\Sigma_d-\llbracket \Sigma_d\rrbracket|$ with $\Sigma_d=\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}z}$ ($\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol {z} \in\mathbb{C}^{d}$). Here, $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$ is a row of $B=i\delta t H/s$. Based on Eqs.~\eqref{eq:representation_error} and \eqref{eq:operation_error}, one has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c7}
    |\llbracket \Sigma_1\rrbracket-\Sigma_1|=|\llbracket w_1  z_1\rrbracket-w_1z_1|<|w_1||z_1|\big|\prod_{\nu=1}^3(1+\delta_\nu)-1\big|<\gamma_3 |w_1||z_1|,
\end{equation}
where $\big|\prod_{\nu=1}^n(1+\delta_\nu)-1\big|<\frac{nu'}{1-nu'}$~\cite{higham2002accuracy}, and $\gamma_n:=\frac{nu'}{1-nu'}$. For $d=2$, the upper bound of the error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c8}
     |\llbracket \Sigma_2\rrbracket-\Sigma_2|=|(\llbracket \Sigma_1\rrbracket+\llbracket w_2 z_2\rrbracket )(1+\delta_1)-\Sigma_2|<\gamma_4\sum_{i=1}^2|w_i||z_i|.
\end{equation}
The general upper bound for arbitrary $d$ is:
\begin{equation}
    |\llbracket \Sigma_d\rrbracket-\Sigma_d| <\gamma_{d+2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|w_j|| z_j|.
\end{equation}
Since $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$ is proportional to a row of the Hamiltonian matrix, sparsity of $H$ implies sparsity of $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$. Vanishing entries in $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$ are special because they do not incur rounding errors. Thus, one can obtain a stronger upper bound
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:C10}
    |\llbracket \Sigma_d\rrbracket-\Sigma_d|<\gamma_{\sigma+2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|w_j|| z_j|
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the number of non-zero elements in $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$.

\subparagraph{Upper bound for the rounding error of $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$.}
The vector $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$ is evaluated recursively via
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recursive_relation_1}
    b_j= \frac{B}{j}b_{j-1},\qquad e^B_j b_0=b_{j-1}+b_j
\end{equation}
where $j=1,2,\cdots,m$ and $b_0$ is a vector representation of $\dket{\Psi}$ under a choice of orthonormal basis. Before evaluating the upper bound for the error $\norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-e^B_mb_0}_2$, I first derive an upper bound for the error of a vector component $\big|\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket^{(i)}-(e^B_mb_0) ^{(i)}\big|$.

For the case $m=0$:
\begin{equation}
    \big|\llbracket e^B_0b_0 \rrbracket^{(i)} -(e^B_0b_0)^{(i)} \big|
    =\big|\llbracket b_0\rrbracket ^{(i)} -b_0 ^{(i)}\big|<\gamma_2|b_0^{(i)}|.
\end{equation}
For $m=1$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:c17}
    &|\llbracket e^B_1b_0 \rrbracket^{(i)}-(e^B_1b_0 )^{(i)}|
    =\big|\big(\llbracket b_0\rrbracket ^{(i)}+\llbracket 
    b_1\rrbracket^{(i)}\big)(1+\delta_1)-(e^B_1b_0 )^{(i)}\big| \\
\nonumber    &\quad=\big|\llbracket b_0\rrbracket ^{(i)}(1+\delta_1)+\frac{\llbracket Ab_0\rrbracket}{s}^{(i)}\prod_{\nu=1}^3(1+\delta_\nu)^3-(e^B_1b_0 )^{(i)}\big| \overset{\eqref{eq:C10}}{<}\gamma_3|b_0^{(i)}| + \gamma_{\sigma_i+5}\sum_l|B^{(il)}||b_0^{(l)}|,
\end{align}
where $\sigma_i$ is the number of non-zero elements in the $i$th row of $B$.
The general upper bound for arbitrary $m$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c16}
    \big|\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket^{(i)} -(e^B_mb_0 )^{(i)}\big|<\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma_i+2)+m+2}\big(\frac{|B|^k}{k!}|b_0|\big)^i,
\end{equation}
where $|B|,|b_0|$ denote the matrix and the vector with elements $|B^{il}|$, $|b_0^{i}|$, respectively. Defining
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigmapdef}
    \sigma':=\max_i \sigma_i
\end{equation} 
and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:c16}:
\begin{align}
\nonumber    & \norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket -(e^B_mb_0 )}_2=\big|\big||\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket -(e^B_mb_0 )|\big|\big|_2<\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\big|\big|\frac{|B|^k}{k!}|b_0|\big|\big|_2\\
\label{eq:B23} &\quad <\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\frac{\big|\big||B|\big|\big|_2^k}{k!}\big|\big||b_0|\big|\big|_2
 \overset{\eqref{eq:norm_inequality}}{<}\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\frac{\big|\big||B|\big|\big|_1^k}{k!}\big|\big||b_0|\big|\big|_2=\beta||b_0||_2
\end{align}
with $\beta:=\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\frac{||B||_1^k}{k!}$. Here, $\beta||b_0||_2$ is the desired upper bound for the rounding error of $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$. 

\subparagraph{Upper bound for the error $\varepsilon_r$.}
\label{par:upper_bound_eps_r}
The vector $(e^B_m)^sb_0 $ is evaluated by the recursive relation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recursive_relation2}
    c_l=e^B_m c_{l-1},\qquad l=1,2,\cdots,s,
\end{equation}
where $c_0=b_0$. 
I illustrate how to bound the error $\varepsilon_r=\norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^sb_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^sb_0 }_2$ for $s=1,2$, and give the general result subsequently. 

For $s=1$: 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s1}
    \norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-e^B_mb_0 }_2\overset{\eqref{eq:B23}}{<}\beta||b_0||_2=\beta.
\end{equation}
For $s=2$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:C22}
\nonumber    \norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^2b_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^2b_0 }_2 &\leq\norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^2b_0\rrbracket-     
    e^B_m\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket}_2+\norm{e^B_m\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^2b_0}_2\\
   &\, <\norm{\llbracket e^B_m \llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket\rrbracket-     
    e^B_m\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket}_2+\beta\norm{e^B_m}_2,
\end{align} 
where the last step is enabled by Eq.~\eqref{eq:s1}.
The matrix norm in the second term is bounded by
\begin{equation}
    \norm{e^B_m}_2=\norm{e^B-R_m(B)}_2\leq 1+R_m(\norm{B}_2)\overset{\eqref{eq:norm_inequality}}{\leq} 1+R_m(\norm{B}_1)=:\alpha.
\end{equation}
Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:B23} holds for any complex vector $b_0$, the error in Eq.~\eqref{eq:C22} is further bounded by:
\begin{align}
    \norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^2b_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^2b_0 }_2\overset{\eqref{eq:B23}}{<}\beta\norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket}_2+\beta\alpha &=\beta\norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-e^B_mb_0+e^B_mb_0}_2+\beta\alpha\quad\\
    &\overset{\eqref{eq:s1}}{<}\beta(\beta+\alpha)+\beta\alpha.
\end{align}
Generalizing this strategy further yields the expression for the upper bound of $\varepsilon_r$: 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:rounding}
\nonumber    \varepsilon_r=\norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^sb_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^sb_0 }_2 &\leq \sum_{l=0}^{s-1}\norm{(e^B_m)^l \llbracket (e^B_m)^{s-l}b_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^{l+1}\llbracket (e^B_m)^{s-l-1}b_0\rrbracket}_2\\
    &<\beta\sum_{l=0}^{s-1}(\alpha+\beta)^{s-l-1}\alpha^{l}=(\alpha+\beta)^s-\alpha^s.
\end{align}    

Combining the upper bounds for $\varepsilon_r$ and $\varepsilon_t$, I return to Eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilonA} and obtain
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overallupperbound}
\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}< E_A(m,s)=sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\frac{1-\big(sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\big)^s}{1-sR_m(\norm{B}_1)}+(\alpha+\beta)^s-\alpha^s,
\end{equation}
where $\norm{B}_1=\norm{A}_1/s$, $\alpha=\alpha(\norm{A}_1,m,s)$ and $\beta=\beta(\sigma',\norm{A}_1,m,s)$.

\subsubsection{Choosing scaling factor and truncation order}

For a given $A$ when computing state propagation by scaling and squaring discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:scaling-squaring}, the inequality~\eqref{eq:accuracy_inequality} generally has multiple solutions for $m$ and $s$. As shown in the previous section, the evaluation requires $ms$ matrix-vector multiplications, which are observed to dominate the runtime. Since systematic minimization of $ms$ requires significant runtime itself, I instead: (1) select solutions to Eq.~\eqref{eq:accuracy_inequality} with the smallest $s$, which is denoted by $\mathbb{s}$, and (2) among these pairs, choose the one with smallest $m$ (denoted by $\mathbb{m}$). 

Thus, calculating $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ requires
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mudefnition}
    \mu:=\mathbb{m}\mathbb{s}
\end{equation}
matrix-vector multiplications.%
\nomenclature{$\mu$}{Number of matrix-vector multiplications required for propagator-state product evaluation}
As shown in the following, for two concrete examples, this method for evaluating $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ can achieve better runtime performance and accuracy than \texttt{expm\_multiply} function~\cite{al2011computing} implmented in \texttt{Scipy} .

There are two reasons for this tendency. First, rounding errors are not considered in the derivation of the upper bound for the errors~\cite{al2011computing}; second, the truncation of $e^B_m$ [Eq.~\eqref{eq:eBm}] is merely based on a heuristic criterion.

Although \texttt{Scipy}'s implementation spends less time on matrix-vector multiplications, the total runtime of \texttt{Scipy}'s implementation is larger, see Fig.~\ref{fig:sas_numerical_experiments}(b) and~(d). This is due to the larger runtime overhead of \texttt{Scipy}'s implementation for determining $(m,s)$. 

In conclusion, the implementation presented here has better numerical stability and runtime than \texttt{Scipy}'s implementation in these numerical experiments. 

\subsection{Propagator Derivative Acting on a State via the Auxiliary Matrix Method}
\label{subsec:auxiliary-matrix}

Having addressed the challenges associated with numerically evaluating $e^A\dket{\Psi}$, I now turn to the calculation of $\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}$, which is crucial for computing cost function gradients as in Eqs.~\eqref{stgradient} and~\eqref{gategradient}. This evaluation is based on the approximation 
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}\approx \frac{\partial (e^{B_n}_m)^s}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi},
\end{align}
where $B_n={-iH_n\Delta t}/{s}$. I proceed by using the auxiliary matrix method based on the identity~\cite{auxigoodwin2015auxiliary,najfeld1995derivatives}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:auxiliary}
    \begin{aligned}
    (e^{\mathcal{A}_n/s}_m)^s
    \binom{0}{|\Psi\rangle}&=
    \binom{
    \frac{\partial (e^{B_{n}}_m)^s}{\partial a_n}  \dket{\Psi}}{{(e^{B_n}_m)^s|\Psi\rangle}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal{A}_n$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_n:=
{\biggl(\begin{matrix}
-iH_n \Delta t& -ih_c\Delta t \\
0 & -iH_n\Delta t
\end{matrix}\biggr)},
\end{equation}
which is a $2\times2$ matrix of operators each acting on the Hilbert space with dimension $d$. Once a basis is chosen, $\mathcal{A}_n$ can be represented as a $2d\times2d$ matrix of complex numbers. Numerical evaluation of the left-hand side of the identity~\eqref{eq:auxiliary} gives a $2d$-dimensional vector. The first $d$ entries of the vector correspond to $\partial (e^{B_n}_m)^s / \partial a_n \dket{\Psi}$, which is an approximation of $\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}$.

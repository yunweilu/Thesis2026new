\chapter{Stark-Induced Sweet Spots for Suppressing Cavity Dephasing}
\label{chap:stark-sweet-spots}

Superconducting microwave cavities are a promising platform for quantum information processing because they exhibit long coherence times~\cite{Milul2023,longcoherence2,Romanenko2020,Reagor2016,longcoherence5}.  Hardware-efficient bosonic quantum error-correction schemes further strengthen this platform by protecting encoded information against dominant errors~\cite{breakeven1,breakeven2,Ni2023,breakeven4,CampagneIbarcq2020,breakeven7,aqec1,aqec2,Cai2021}. In particular, cat and dual-rail encodings exploit the strong photon-loss bias in cavities, which underlies their error-correction advantage~\cite{teoh2023dual,putterman2025hardware}.

Universal control of bosonic qubits generally requires coupling to a nonlinear element~\cite{Ma2021QuantumControl,cavitycontrol1,cavitycontrol2,cavitycontrol3}. A family of flux-tunable devices, such as the Superconducting Nonlinear Asymmetric Inductive eLement (SNAIL)~\cite{SNAIL}, the Flux-Tunable Transmon (FTT)~\cite{squid}, and the Linear INductive Coupler (LINC)~\cite{linc}, enables single-mode control and entangling operations between modes. Certain control protocols~\cite{SNAIL,linc,Copetudo2026DirectPhase} require biasing these devices at flux-sensitive operating points away from the sweet spot. One example is the use of FTT to realize a beam-splitting interaction between two cavity modes, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:analyticalcontrol}. At these operating points, flux noise induces fluctuations in the nonlinear-element frequency. Through coupling to the nonlinear element, the cavity modes inherit these frequency fluctuations and consequently undergo dephasing. This inherited dephasing is especially harmful because it directly competes with the favorable photon-loss bias.

A commonly used strategy to mitigate this dephasing is to idle the nonlinear element at a DC flux sweet spot that is insensitive to flux noise, and tune it away from the sweet spot only when an operation requires it. However, this scheme is challenging in two respects: (1) in three-dimensional cavities, ensuring both fast flux tuning and a high-coherence cavity requires extra hardware complexity~\cite{magnetichose1,magnetichose2,li2025,valadares2026}; (2) tuning flux carries the risk of inducing unwanted transitions~\cite{Sung2021CZiSWAPleakage,Yan2018TunableCouplingleakage,Stehlik2021TunableCouplingleakage}.

This work introduces an alternative approach to suppress inherited cavity dephasing without changing the DC flux bias: the Stark-induced sweet spot (SISS). In this scheme, a weak off-resonant drive applied to the nonlinear mode induces an AC Stark shift of the cavity transition frequency. With suitable drive parameters, the shifted cavity frequency is first-order insensitive with respect to flux noise, thereby creating a dynamical sweet spot~\cite{Huang2021DynamicalSweetSpots,PhysRevApplied.12.054015dss2}. For a cavity coupled to a flux-tunable transmon with a representative set of parameters, Monte Carlo simulations show a \(\sim 20\times\) improvement in the cavity dephasing time.

This chapter is organized as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:flux-noise-dephasing}, we describe cavity dephasing inherited from a nonlinear device. In Section~\ref{sec:creating-stark-sweet-spots}, we introduce the mechanism for suppressing cavity dephasing using a drive and show how to select the corresponding drive parameters. In Section~\ref{sec:residual-dephasing}, we derive the drive-induced dephasing rates and compare them with numerical simulations. In Section~\ref{sec:bosonic-qec}, we apply the SISS scheme to binomial and dual-rail encodings, highlighting its ability to suppress dephasing simultaneously across multiple pairs of Fock states and multiple modes.

\section{Flux-Noise-Induced Cavity Dephasing}
\label{sec:flux-noise-dephasing}

We now analyze flux-noise-induced cavity dephasing in the dispersive regime. When the nonlinear element is operated at a flux-sensitive bias point, flux noise causes fluctuations in its transition frequency. Through the dispersive coupling, these fluctuations are transferred to the cavity mode as effective frequency noise, leading to cavity dephasing.

To illustrate this mechanism, we consider a cavity coupled to a nonlinear device, specifically a flux-tunable transmon (FTT). The full Hamiltonian of the system shown in Fig.\ \ref{fig:stark_device}(a) is
\begin{align}
\label{fullhamiltonian}
H_{\text{full}}(t)=\;&4E_C (n-n_g)^2
-E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}\!\left(\Phi+\delta\Phi(t)\right)
\cos\!\left[{\varphi}-{\varphi_0\!\left(\Phi+\delta\Phi(t)\right)}\right] \notag\\
&+\omega_c c^{\dagger}c
+g' n (c^{\dagger}+c)
+2\Omega'_0\cos(\omega_d t)\,n .
\end{align}
Here, \(E_C\) is the charging energy, \(n\) is the charge operator, and \(n_g\) is the offset charge. \(b\) (\(c\)) annihilates a photon in the transmon (cavity) mode of frequency \(\omega_b\) (\(\omega_c\)). The capacitive coupling strength is \(g'\), and the final term describes a charge drive applied to the FTT with amplitude \(\Omega'_0\) and frequency \(\omega_d\).

Both the effective Josephson energy \(E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}\) and the phase offset \(\varphi_0\) depend on flux:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}\!\left(\Phi+\delta\Phi(t)\right)
    &= (E_{J1}+E_{J2}) \cos\!\left[\frac{\pi\left(\Phi+\delta\Phi(t)\right)}{\Phi_0}\right],\\
    \varphi_0
    &= \arctan\!\left[
    d\tan\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    \right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, \(E_{J1}\) and \(E_{J2}\) are the Josephson energies of the two junctions, \(\Phi_0=h/2e\) is the superconducting flux quantum, and \(d \equiv (E_{J2}-E_{J1})/({E_{J1}+E_{J2}})\) is the SQUID-loop asymmetry.

Flux noise in these systems is typically observed to exhibit a \(1/f\) spectrum. Accordingly, the power spectral density of the Gaussian flux fluctuations is
\begin{equation}
    S_{1/f}(\omega) = \int_0^{t} \langle \delta \Phi(0)\, \delta\Phi(\tau) \rangle e^{i\omega \tau}\, d\tau
    = \frac{A^2_0}{|\omega / 2 \pi|},
\end{equation}
where \(A_0\) is the flux-noise spectral-density amplitude.

For small flux fluctuations \(\delta\Phi(t)\ll\Phi_0\), a Taylor expansion of \(H_{\text{full}}\) gives
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{full}}\!\left(\Phi+\delta\Phi(t)\right)
\approx H_{\text{sys}}(\Phi) + \underbrace{\delta\Phi(t)\frac{\partial H_{\text{sys}}}{\partial \Phi}}_{H_{\text{noise}}'} 
+ \mathcal{O}\big((\delta \Phi)^2\big),
\end{equation}
where \(H_{\text{sys}}\) is evaluated at the static bias point \(\Phi\):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\label{system hamiltonian}
H_{\text{sys}}(\Phi) =\; & 4E_C (n-n_g)^2
- E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}(\Phi)
\cos\!\left[{\varphi}-{\varphi_0(\Phi)}\right]  + \omega_c c^{\dagger}c
+ g' n (c^{\dagger}+c)
+ 2\Omega'_0 \cos(\omega_d t)\, n.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Transmon limit.}
In the transmon regime, \(E_C \ll E_{J,\text{eff}}\), the wavefunction is localized near the bottom of the cosine potential well; see Sec.\ \ref{sec:transmon}. The phase and charge operators can then be written in terms of ladder operators as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \varphi - \varphi_0 &= \varphi_{\text{ZPF}} \left( b + b^\dagger \right), \quad n = \frac{-i \left( b - b^\dagger \right)}{2 \varphi_{\text{ZPF}}}, \quad\varphi_{\text{ZPF}}(\Phi) = \left( \frac{2 E_C}{E_{J,\text{eff}} (\Phi)} \right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Expanding the cosine potential to fourth order and applying the rotating-wave approximation (RWA) yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    H_\text{sys}(t) \approx \; & \omega_{b} b^{\dagger} b + \frac{K}{2} b^{\dagger 2} b^{2} + \omega_c c^\dagger c + g (b + b^{\dagger})(c + c^{\dagger}) + 2\Omega_0\cos(\omega_{d}t)(b + b^{\dagger}),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where the FTT frequency, anharmonicity, renormalized drive amplitude, and coupling strength are given by \(\omega_{b} = \sqrt{8E_{C}E_{J,\text{eff}} } - E_{C}\), \(K = -E_{C}\), \(\Omega_0 = \Omega_0'/2\varphi_{\text{ZPF}}\), and \(g = g'/2\varphi_{\text{ZPF}}\), respectively. Here, \(\omega_b\), \(g\), and \(\Omega_0\) are flux dependent, but this dependence is dropped below for simplicity.

The noise Hamiltonian in the oscillator basis defined at the static flux bias \(\Phi\) is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
H_{\text{noise}}'(t) 
&= \delta\Phi(t) \frac{\partial H_{\text{sys}}}{\partial \Phi} \\
&= -\delta\Phi(t)\biggl[ 
\frac{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Phi} \cos(\varphi - \varphi_0) + E_{J,\mathrm{eff}} \frac{\partial \varphi_0}{\partial\Phi} \sin(\varphi - \varphi_0) 
\biggr] \\
&\approx -\delta\Phi(t)\biggl[
\frac{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Phi}
\left(1-\frac{(\varphi-\varphi_0)^2}{2}\right)+ E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}\frac{\partial \varphi_0}{\partial\Phi}(\varphi-\varphi_0)
+ \mathcal{O}\big((\varphi-\varphi_0)^3\big)
\biggr] \\
&\approx -\delta\Phi(t)\biggl[
\frac{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Phi}
\left(1-\frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{ZPF}}^2}{2}(b+b^\dagger)^2\right) + E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}\frac{\partial \varphi_0}{\partial\Phi}\,
\varphi_{\mathrm{ZPF}}(b+b^\dagger)
\biggr].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This expression contains a term linear in \(b+b^\dagger\) and a quadratic term proportional to \((b+b^\dagger)^2\). The linear term drives transitions near the oscillator frequency \(\omega_b\), with rates governed by \(S_{1/f}(\omega_b)\). Expanding \((b+b^\dagger)^2 = 2b^\dagger b + 1 + b^2 + b^{\dagger 2}\), we obtain the squeezing terms, \(b^2\) and \(b^{\dagger 2}\), with drive transitions near \(2\omega_b\), and their rates proportional to \(S_{1/f}(2\omega_b)\). Because \(S_{1/f}(\omega)\) is strongly suppressed at these high frequencies, \(\omega_b/2\pi\sim\mathrm{GHz}\), both the linear and squeezing-induced transition channels are neglected. Retaining only the number-conserving terms proportional to \(b^\dagger b\)gives
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{noise}}'(t)\approx
\frac{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Phi}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{ZPF}}^2\,
\delta\Phi(t)\; b^\dagger b ,
\end{equation}
up to an additive constant. Using
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\omega_b}{\partial\Phi}
=
\frac{\partial\omega_b}{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}\frac{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Phi}
=
\varphi_{\mathrm{ZPF}}^2\,\frac{\partial E_{J,\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Phi},
\label{eq:sensitivity_deriv}
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{noise}}'(t)\approx
\frac{\partial\omega_b}{\partial\Phi}\,\delta\Phi(t)\; b^\dagger b .
\end{equation}

In the transmon rigime, the full Hamiltonian in Eq.\ \eqref{fullhamiltonian} can be approximated as
\begin{align}
H(t) &= H_{\mathrm{sys}} + H_{\mathrm{noise}}(t),\label{H_sys}\\
H_{\mathrm{sys}} &= H_{\mathrm{static}}+ 2\Omega_0 \cos(\omega_d t)\,(b + b^\dagger),\\
H_{\mathrm{static}} &=
\omega_b\, b^\dagger b
+ \frac{K}{2}\, b^{\dagger 2} b^{2}
+ \omega_c\, c^\dagger c
+ g\,(b+b^\dagger)(c+c^\dagger), \label{Hstatic}\\
H_{\mathrm{noise}}(t) &= \delta\omega_b(t)\, b^\dagger b .
\end{align}
Here, \(\delta\omega_b(t)=(\partial\omega_b/\partial\Phi)\delta\Phi(t)\), and \(H_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)\) models fluctuations in the FTT frequency induced by flux noise.

\paragraph{Dispersive regime.}
In the regime \(|g| \ll |\omega_c-\omega_b|\ll|\omega_c+\omega_b|\), counter-rotating terms in \(H_\text{sys}\), Eq.\ \eqref{H_sys}, can be neglected, giving
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\label{approximated system hamiltonian}
     H_{\mathrm{sys}}(t) \approx \,& \omega_b b^\dagger b + \omega_c c^\dagger c + \frac{K}{2} b^{\dagger 2} b^2 + g\,(bc^\dagger + b^\dagger c) + 2\Omega_0 \cos(\omega_d t)\,(b + b^\dagger).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The static Hamiltonian \(H_{\text{static}}\) consists of the first four terms. To remove the interaction term \(g(bc^\dagger + b^\dagger c)\), we apply a second-order Schrieffer--Wolff transformation with \(U_{\text{disp}} = e^{D_{\text{disp}}}\).

The generator \(D_{\text{disp}}\) is chosen to eliminate the off-diagonal terms in $H_\text{static}$ to first order:
\begin{equation}
\label{Ddisp}
    D_{\text{disp}} = \sum_n \sqrt{n+1}\,
    \frac{g}{\omega_b + nK - \omega_c}\,
    |n+1\rangle \langle n|\, c - \mathrm{h.c.}
\end{equation}
The transmon--cavity detuning \(\Delta_{\text{bc}}=\omega_b-\omega_c\) is taken to be large compared with the transmon anharmonicity, so that the dispersive Hamiltonian is far from the straddling regime~\cite{Koch2007}. Expanding in the small parameter \(n|K|/|\Delta_{\text{bc}}|\) yields
\begin{equation}
    D_{\text{disp}} \approx
    \frac{g}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}} b^\dagger c
    - \frac{gK}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}^2} (b^\dagger b - 1)\, b^\dagger c
    + \mathcal{O}\!\left[\left(nK/\Delta_{\text{bc}}\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}
Applying the same transformation to the approximate system Hamiltonian gives
\begin{align}
\label{Hdispprime}
    H'_{\text{disp}}
    &= e^{-D_{\text{disp}}} H_\text{sys}(t)\, e^{D_{\text{disp}}}
    \nonumber\\
    &\approx H_{\text{disp}}
    + 2\Omega_0 \cos(\omega_d t)\,
    \Bigl[e^{-D_{\text{disp}}} (b + b^\dagger) e^{D_{\text{disp}}}\Bigr]
    \nonumber \\
    &\approx H_{\text{disp}}
    + 2\Omega_0 \cos(\omega_d t)\,
    \Bigl[b^\dagger + [D_{\text{disp}}, b] + \mathrm{h.c.}\Bigr],
\end{align}
where the dispersive Hamiltonian takes the form
\begin{align}
\label{Hdisp}
H_\text{disp} =
\bar{\omega}_b\, b^\dagger b
+ \frac{K}{2}\, b^{\dagger 2} b^{2}
+ \bar{\omega}_c\, c^\dagger c
+ \chi\, b^\dagger b\, c^\dagger c.
\end{align}
Here \(\chi\) is the dispersive shift, while \(\bar{\omega}_b\) and \(\bar{\omega}_c\) are the Lamb-shifted FTT and cavity frequencies, respectively:
\begin{align}
\chi &\approx 2\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}^2}K,\quad
\bar{\omega}_b \approx \omega_b + \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}}, \quad
\bar{\omega}_c \approx \omega_c - \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}}.
\end{align} 
We approximate the commutation in Eq.\ \eqref{Hdispprime}
\begin{equation}
[D_{\text{disp}}, b] \approx
-\frac{g}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}}\, c
-\frac{Kg}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}^2}\, b\, c^\dagger
+\frac{2Kg}{\Delta_{\text{bc}}^2}\, b^\dagger b\, c .
\end{equation}
These terms rotate at frequencies set by the transmon--cavity detuning and the cavity frequency. Because \(\omega_d\) is far detuned from these transitions, the rotating-wave approximation is applied, leaving
\begin{equation}
H'_{\text{sys}} \approx H_{\text{disp}} + 2\Omega_0 \cos(\omega_d t)\,(b + b^\dagger).
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Cavity dephasing inherited from the FTT}The noise Hamiltonian \(H_{\mathrm{noise}}(t)\) primarily induces dephasing between eigenstates of \(H_\text{disp}\) (see discussion later in Sec.\ \ref{subsec:effective-master}). In particular, because \(\Delta_{bc}\) depends on \(\omega_b\), fluctuations in the FTT frequency \(\delta\omega_b(t)\) lead to fluctuations in the cavity frequency 
\begin{align}
\delta\bar{\omega}_c(t)
= \frac{\partial \bar{\omega}_c}{\partial \omega_b}\,\delta\omega_b(t)
\approx \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\,\delta\omega_b(t),
\end{align}
as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:stark_device}(b). Such fluctuations produce cavity dephasing.

\section{Creating Stark-Induced Sweet Spots}
\label{sec:creating-stark-sweet-spots}

FTT-frequency fluctuations \(\delta\omega_b(t)\) lead to cavity-frequency fluctuations \(\delta\bar{\omega}_c(t)\) through the Lamb shift, thereby causing cavity dephasing. This dephasing channel can be suppressed by driving the FTT [Fig.~\ref{fig:stark_device}(a)]. The drive induces an AC Stark shift that can be tuned to create a \emph{dynamical sweet spot}. At such a sweet spot, the transition frequencies between the relevant Floquet states become first-order insensitive to flux fluctuations at a fixed flux-bias point~\cite{Huang2021DynamicalSweetSpots,PhysRevApplied.12.054015dss2}.


In the weak, off-resonant regime considered here, the quasienergies of the Floquet states are well approximated by the eigenenergies of an effective time-independent Hamiltonian, as justified in App.~\ref{app:floquet}. In the frame rotating at the drive frequency \(\omega_d\), the driven dispersive Hamiltonian after the rotating-wave approximation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:H_R_floquet}
H_R =
\Delta_{bd}\, b^\dagger b
+ \frac{K}{2}\, b^{\dagger 2} b^{2}
+ \overline{\omega}_c\, c^\dagger c
+ \chi\, b^\dagger b\, c^\dagger c
+ \Omega_0\, ( b + b^\dagger ),
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta_{bd}=\bar{\omega}_b-\omega_d\) is the FTT--drive detuning. For a fixed cavity photon number \(m\), the drive hybridizes the states \(|g,m\rangle\) and \(|e,m\rangle\), producing level repulsion and shifting the energy of \(|g,m\rangle\). The sign and magnitude of this shift are controlled by the photon-number-dependent FTT--drive detuning,
\[
\Delta_m \equiv \Delta_{bd}+m\chi ,
\]
which is shifted by the dispersive interaction with the cavity. Consequently, the drive-induced AC Stark shift of \(|g,m\rangle\) depends on \(m\), as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:stark_device}(c). This photon-number-dependent Stark shift modifies the transition frequency between neighboring cavity Fock states. By tuning the drive amplitude and detuning, this transition frequency can be made first-order insensitive to flux noise, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:stark_device}(d).

An analytic expression for the AC Stark shifts can be derived in the weak-drive regime. The perturbative analysis relies on two assumptions: (i) \(\Omega_0 \ll \min_m |\Delta_m|\) for the relevant photon numbers, so that the drive does not strongly hybridize \(|g,m\rangle\) and \(|e,m\rangle\); and (ii) \(|\Omega_0| \ll |K|\), so that virtual transitions involving higher transmon levels remain perturbative.

Starting from the rotating-frame Hamiltonian with a three-level truncation of the FTT,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:HR_start}
\begin{aligned}
H_R \approx\;&
\Delta_{bd}\,|e\rangle\langle e|
+\bigl(2\Delta_{bd} + K\bigr)\,|f\rangle\langle f|
+\bar\omega_c\,c^\dagger c
+\chi\bigl(|e\rangle\langle e|+2|f\rangle\langle f|\bigr)c^\dagger c \\
&+\Omega_0\Bigl(|g\rangle\langle e|+
\sqrt{2}\,|e\rangle\langle f|+
\mathrm{h.c.}\Bigr),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
we decompose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:H0_plus_V}
H_R = H_0 + V,
\end{equation}
where \(H_0\) is the \(\Omega_0\)-independent part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:HR_start}, and \(V\) is the drive term.

A Schrieffer--Wolff transformation with an anti-Hermitian generator \(D_d=-D_d^\dagger\) gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:SWT_def}
H_d' = e^{-D_d}H_R e^{D_d},
\end{equation}
which is diagonal in the bare basis. To first order in \(\Omega_0\), the generator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Dd_first_order}
\begin{aligned}
D_d=
\sum_{m}\Biggl[
-\frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m}\,|e,m\rangle\langle g,m|
-\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\Omega_0}{\Delta_m+K}\,|f,m\rangle\langle e,m|
-\mathrm{h.c.}
\Biggr] .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For \(|\Delta_m|\ll |K|\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Dd_first_order_approx}
D_d\approx
\sum_{m}\Biggl[
-\frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m}\,|e,m\rangle\langle g,m|
-\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\Omega_0}{K}\,|f,m\rangle\langle e,m|
-\mathrm{h.c.}
\Biggr],
\end{equation}
where we have kept the zeroth-order term in an expansion in \(\Delta_m/K\).

Keeping terms up to second order in the Schrieffer--Wolff transformation gives
\begin{align}
H_d' \approx\;& H_0
+ \sum_m \Omega_0^2\Biggl[
-\frac{1}{\Delta_m}\,|g,m\rangle\langle g,m|
+\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_m}-\frac{2}{K}\right) |e,m\rangle\langle e,m|
+\frac{2}{K}\,|f,m\rangle\langle f,m|
\Biggr].
\label{eq:Hd_second_order_explicit}
\end{align}
Here the terms proportional to \(1/\Delta_m\) arise from the \(|g,m\rangle\leftrightarrow |e,m\rangle\) coupling, while the terms proportional to \(1/K\) arise from virtual coupling to \(|f,m\rangle\).

Because the FTT is initialized in \(|g\rangle\) and the transmon obeys the nearest-neighbor selection rule, the population of \(|f\rangle\) remains negligible throughout the dynamics, including in the presence of noise. Projecting onto the \(\{|g,m\rangle,|e,m\rangle\}\) subspace gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hd_two_level}
\begin{aligned}
H_d=
\widetilde{\Delta}_{bd}\sigma^+\sigma^-
+\bar\omega_c\,c^\dagger c
-\sum_m \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_m}\,|m\rangle\langle m|  +\sigma^+\sigma^-\!\Biggl[
\chi\,c^\dagger c
+\sum_m \frac{2\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_m}\,|m\rangle\langle m|
\Biggr],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the drive-renormalized FTT detuning is
\[
\widetilde{\Delta}_{bd}\equiv \Delta_{bd}-\frac{2\Omega_0^2}{K}.
\]
The drive therefore produces both an \(m\)-dependent shift of the cavity transition frequency and an additional drive-induced correction to the dispersive shift. 

To quantify the resulting fluctuations in the cavity transition frequency, we define the drive-renormalized transition frequency between adjacent cavity Fock states as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:omega_c_tilde_m}
\widetilde{\omega}_{c,m\rightarrow m+1}
=
\omega_c
- \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}}
- \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{m+1}}
+ \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_m}.
\end{equation}
The tilde denotes a drive-renormalized transition frequency. An FTT-frequency fluctuation \(\delta\omega_b(t)\) then induces
\begin{equation}
\delta\widetilde{\omega}_{c,m\rightarrow m+1}(t)
=
D_{m\rightarrow m+1}\,\delta\omega_b(t),
\end{equation}
where
\[
D_{m\rightarrow m+1}
\equiv
\frac{\partial \widetilde{\omega}_{c,m\rightarrow m+1}}{\partial \omega_b}
\]
is the susceptibility of the net cavity transition frequency to FTT-frequency fluctuations. A sweet spot occurs when \(D_{m\rightarrow m+1}=0\). Depending on the drive parameters, this cancellation can occur either for a specific cavity transition, giving a selective sweet spot, or over several neighboring transitions, giving an unselective sweet spot.

The numerical results are obtained based on full Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{system hamiltonian} and the system parameters are \(E_{J1}=E_{J2}=30.19\,\mathrm{GHz}\), \(E_C=0.10\,\mathrm{GHz}\), \(\omega_c/2\pi=5.226\,\mathrm{GHz}\), \(g'/2\pi=0.050\,\mathrm{GHz}\), and \(\Phi=0.20\,\Phi_0\). For these values, the corresponding effective parameters in Eq.~\eqref{fullhamiltonian} are \(\omega_b/2\pi=6.16\,\mathrm{GHz}\), \(K/2\pi=-0.10\,\mathrm{GHz}\), and \(g/2\pi=0.10\,\mathrm{GHz}\).}
\label{fig:drive_sweet_spots_compare}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Unselective Regime.}
In the unselective regime, the drive detuning satisfies \(|\Delta_{bd}|\gg|\chi|\). The drive therefore couples the transitions \(\ket{g,m}\leftrightarrow\ket{e,m}\) across different photon numbers. Expanding
\[
\Delta_m^{-1}=(\Delta_{bd}+m\chi)^{-1}
\]
to leading order in \(|\chi/\Delta_{bd}|\) yields the approximate effective Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hd_far_detuned}
H_d \approx
\widetilde{\Delta}_{bd}\,\sigma^+\sigma^-
+\widetilde{\omega}_{c}\,c^\dagger c
+\chi\left(1-\frac{2\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{bd}^2}\right)\sigma^+\sigma^-\,c^\dagger c ,
\end{equation}
up to constant energy offsets, where
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\omega}_{c,0\to1}
=
\bar{\omega}_c+\frac{\Omega_0^2\chi}{\Delta_{bd}^2}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding susceptibility is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D01_unselective}
D_{0\to1} = \frac{\partial \widetilde{\omega}_{c,0\to1}}{\partial \omega_b}
\approx \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}
\left(1-\frac{4\Omega_0^2K}{\Delta_{bd}^3}\right).
\end{equation}
where we have used the leading dispersive relation \(\chi\simeq 2g^2K/\Delta_{bc}^2\). Setting \(D=0\) gives the unselective sweet-spot condition
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ss_unselective}
\Delta_{bd}=(4\Omega_0^2K)^{1/3},
\end{equation}
where the real cube root is understood. For the transmon-like case \(K<0\), this sweet spot occurs at negative detuning, \(\Delta_{bd}<0\), or equivalently at \(\omega_d>\bar{\omega}_b\).

To verify the analytical expression, we compare the perturbative susceptibility with a Floquet calculation using the full system Hamiltonian in Eq.\ \eqref{system hamiltonian}. The result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:drive_sweet_spots_compare}(a). For \(\Omega_0/2\pi=10~\mathrm{MHz}\), \(D_{0 \rightarrow 1}\) approaches the bare Lamb-shift value \(g^2/\Delta_{bc}^2\approx0.01\) when the drive is far detuned from the FTT transition. As \(\omega_d\) is tuned toward \(\bar{\omega}_b\), a single sweet spot appears for \(\omega_d>\bar{\omega}_b\), consistent with the negative-detuning solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ss_unselective}. The characteristic detuning is also consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ss_unselective}, which predicts \(\Delta_{bd}/2\pi\sim -34~\mathrm{MHz}\) for \(K/2\pi\approx-0.1~\mathrm{GHz}\). The residual offset between the analytical and numerical sweet-spot positions arises because higher-order corrections to Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hd_two_level} become non-negligible at this drive strength. Closer to resonance, \(D_{0 \rightarrow 1}\) increases again because the drive-induced Stark shift dominates the susceptibility.

\paragraph{Selective Regime.}
In the selective regime, the drive is closest to resonance with a particular photon number \(m^\ast\), so that \(|\Delta_{m^\ast}|\ll|\chi|\). In this case, the dominant Stark shift comes from hybridization between \(\ket{g,m^\ast}\) and \(\ket{e,m^\ast}\). The transition frequency between \(\ket{g,m^\ast}\) and \(\ket{g,m^\ast+1}\) is then approximately
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\omega}_{c,m^\ast\rightarrow m^\ast+1}
\approx
\bar{\omega}_c+\frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{m^\ast}}.
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to \(\omega_b\) gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Dm_selective}
D_{m^\ast\rightarrow m^\ast+1}
\approx
\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}
-\frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{m^\ast}^2}.
\end{equation}
The selective sweet spot therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weak_drive_sweetspot}
\Delta_{m^\ast}
=
\pm \left|\frac{\Delta_{bc}}{g}\Omega_0\right|
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Delta_{bd}
=
\pm \left|\frac{\Delta_{bc}}{g}\Omega_0\right|-m^\ast\chi .
\end{equation}

This prediction is confirmed by Fig.~\ref{fig:drive_sweet_spots_compare}(b), where two sweet spots appear on either side of the effective resonance \(\Delta_{m^\ast}=0\). Their characteristic detuning agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:weak_drive_sweetspot}, which predicts
\[
|\Delta_{bd}|
\sim
\left|\frac{\Delta_{bc}}{g}\right|\Omega_0
\approx 0.5~\mathrm{MHz}
\]
for the parameters used here, where \(g/|\Delta_{bc}|\simeq 0.1\) and \(\Omega_0/2\pi=0.05~\mathrm{MHz}\). Thus, in the selective regime, a low-power drive can cancel the bare Lamb-shift susceptibility for a chosen cavity transition.

\paragraph{Sweet-Spot Map.}
Figure~\ref{fig:drive_sweet_spots_compare}(c) shows \(D_{0\rightarrow 1}\) as a function of drive amplitude \(\Omega_0\) and detuning \(\Delta_{bd}\).  At small \(\Omega_0\), the selective-regime condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:weak_drive_sweetspot} gives the linear scaling \(|\Delta_{bd}|\propto\Omega_0\), visible as two sweet spots on either side of the effective resonance. As \(\Omega_0\) increases, the near-resonant region eventually violates the perturbative condition \(\Omega_0\ll|\Delta_m|\), and the weak-drive selective approximation ceases to apply to the left line. The right line evolves continuously into the unselective regime. For \(K<0\), the sweet spots lies at negative detuning and approaches the cubic scaling predicted by Eq.~\eqref{eq:ss_unselective}. At still larger \(\Omega_0\), the region of reduced susceptibility broadens, making the sweet spots more robust to choice of the drive frequency, although quantitative agreement then requires higher-order corrections beyond the second-order perturbative treatment.




\section{Residual Cavity Dephasing at the Stark-Induced Sweet Spot}
\label{sec:residual-dephasing}

The stark-induced sweet spot developed in the preceding chapter suppresses dephasing due to flux noise. However, the drive also opens new dephasing channels when FTT relaxation is present. This chapter quantifies these residual channels and evaluates the total cavity dephasing rate.

The open quantum system is modeled with a stochastic master equation. For a given flux-noise realization $\delta\Phi(t)$, the density matrix $\rho(t)$ obeys
\begin{align}
\dot{\rho}
&= -i\left[\,H_{\mathrm{sys}} + H_{\mathrm{noise}},\ \rho\,\right]
+ \gamma_{b\downarrow}\,\mathcal{D}[b]\rho,
\label{eq:Linblad_ch8}\end{align}
Here $\mathcal{D}[b]\rho = b\rho b^\dagger - \tfrac{1}{2}\{b^\dagger b, \rho\}$ describes FTT relaxation at rate $\gamma_{b\downarrow}$.

\subsection{Effective Master Equation in the Driven Frame}
\label{subsec:effective-master}
To analyze the decoherence between the eigenstates of $H_d$, we transform the operators in the Lindblad master equation from the lab frame to the frame of $H_d$. This procedure involves three transformations, as detailed below.

\section*{Transformation of $H_{\text{noise}}$.}
\label{subsec:Hnoise_transfer}
We consider the flux-noise Hamiltonian
$H_{\text{noise}} = \frac{\partial \omega_b}{\partial \Phi} \delta \Phi(t) b^\dagger b$.
After applying the dispersive transformation $U_{\text{disp}} = e^{D_{\text{disp}}}$, the number operator transforms as
\begin{align}
    e^{-D_{\text{disp}}} b^\dagger b e^{D_{\text{disp}}}
    &= e^{-D_{\text{disp}}} b^\dagger e^{D_{\text{disp}}} e^{-D_{\text{disp}}} b e^{D_{\text{disp}}} \nonumber \approx \left( b^\dagger + [D_{\text{disp}}, b^\dagger] \right) \left( b + [D_{\text{disp}}, b] \right) \nonumber \\
    &\approx b^\dagger b + [D_{\text{disp}}, b^\dagger b] + [D_{\text{disp}}, b^\dagger][D_{\text{disp}}, b] \nonumber \\
    &\approx b^\dagger b + \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} c^\dagger c - \frac{4Kg^2}{\Delta_{bc}^3} b^\dagger b c^\dagger c \nonumber+ \left( -\frac{g}{\Delta_{bc}} b c^\dagger + \frac{Kg}{\Delta_{bc}^2} b^\dagger b c^\dagger + \text{h.c.} \right).
\end{align}

The non-number-conserving terms (e.g., those proportional to $b c^\dagger$ and $b^\dagger b\, c^\dagger$, together with their Hermitian conjugates) drive transitions at frequencies near $\Delta_{bc}/2\pi \sim \mathrm{GHz}$. For $1/f$ flux noise, $S_{1/f}(\omega)$ is negligible at such frequencies, so these rapidly oscillating terms are discarded. The resulting noise Hamiltonian after the dispersive transformation is
\begin{equation}
    H_{\text{noise}}^{\text{disp}} \approx \frac{\partial \omega_b}{\partial \Phi} \delta \Phi \left( b^\dagger b + \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} c^\dagger c - \frac{4Kg^2}{\Delta_{bc}^3} b^\dagger b c^\dagger c \right).
\end{equation}

In the rotating frame defined by $U_R$, the remaining terms are diagonal in the relevant number operators, so its form is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\text{noise}}^R = H_{\text{noise}}^{\text{disp}}.
\end{equation}
Under the drive transformation $U_d = e^{D_d}$, we expand the noise Hamiltonian to second order:
\begin{align}
    {H_{\text{noise}}^d}' &= U_d^\dagger H_{\text{noise}}^R U_d \nonumber \approx H_{\text{noise}}^R + [D_d, H_{\text{noise}}^R] + \frac{1}{2} [D_d, [D_d, H_{\text{noise}}^R]].
\end{align}
Here we truncate the FTT Hilbert space to three levels in the regime of interest, $|\Omega_0/K| \ll 1$, so that
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    H_{\text{noise}}^R \approx \frac{\partial \omega_b}{\partial \Phi} \delta \Phi \bigg( & |e\rangle\langle e| + 2|f\rangle\langle f| + \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} c^\dagger c- \frac{4Kg^2}{\Delta_{bc}^3} \left( |e\rangle\langle e| + 2|f\rangle\langle f| \right) c^\dagger c \bigg).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Evaluating the required commutators, we obtain
\begin{align}
    \left[ D_d, \, b^\dagger b \right] &= -\sum_m \bigg[ \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m} |g,m\rangle \langle e,m| + \frac{\sqrt{2}\Omega_0}{K} |e,m\rangle \langle f,m| + \text{h.c.} \bigg], \nonumber \\
    \left[ D_d, \left[ D_d, b^\dagger b \right] \right] &= \sum_m \Bigg[ 2 \left( \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m} \right)^2 |g,m\rangle\langle g,m| - 2 \left( \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m} \right)^2 |e,m\rangle\langle e,m| \nonumber - \frac{4\Omega_0^2}{K^2} |f,m\rangle\langle f,m| \Bigg], \nonumber \\
    [D_d, c^\dagger c] &= 0,
\end{align}
which gives
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:noise_Hamiltoniand}
    {H_{\text{noise}}^d}' \approx \frac{\partial \omega_b}{\partial \Phi} \delta \Phi \sum_m\Bigg( \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} c^\dagger c + \left(\frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m}\right)^2 |m\rangle\langle m|  - \\\sigma^+ \sigma^- \left( \frac{4Kg^2}{\Delta_{bc}^3} c^\dagger c + 2\left(\frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m}\right)^2 |m\rangle\langle m| \right)
    -  \left[ \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m} \sigma^- |m\rangle\langle m| + \text{h.c.} \right]\Bigg).
\end{multline}
Here we truncate the FTT to two levels, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hd_two_level}.
\section*{Transformation of the Lindbladian.}
\label{subsec:Lindblad_transfer}

Following Ref.~\cite{cqedreview2021}, the reduced dynamics in the dispersive regime are governed by the Lindblad master equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Linblad_disp}
    \dot{\rho}_{\text{disp}} = -i[H_{\text{disp}}, \rho_{\text{disp}}] + \gamma_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}[b]\rho_{\text{disp}} + \gamma_{c\downarrow} \mathcal{D}[c]\rho_{\text{disp}}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\gamma_{b\downarrow}$ is the FTT relaxation rate, and $\gamma_{c\downarrow} = \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\gamma_{b\downarrow}$ is the Purcell-induced cavity decay rate. We assume that the cavity relaxation is Purcell limited in state-of-the-art hardware designs~\cite{longcoherence5}.

In the rotating frame defined by $U_R$, the master equation retains the same Lindblad form:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:LinbladR}
    \dot{\rho}_R = -i[H_R, \rho_R] + \gamma_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}[b]\rho_R + \gamma_{c\downarrow} \mathcal{D}[c]\rho_R,
\end{equation}
where $\rho_R = U_R^\dagger \rho_{\text{disp}} U_R$.

The effective dissipation in the driven frame is obtained by transforming the jump operators with $U_d$. We first truncate the FTT Hilbert space to the three lowest levels so that $b \approx |g\rangle\langle e| + \sqrt{2}|e\rangle\langle f|$, and then apply the drive transformation $U_d = e^{D_d}$. Projecting the transformed operator onto the two-level FTT subspace yields
\begin{align}
\label{eq:transformb}
    e^{-D_d} b e^{D_d}
    &\approx b + [D_d, b] + \frac{1}{2}[D_d, [D_d, b]] \nonumber \\
    &\approx \sigma^- - \sum_m \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m} |m\rangle\langle m| + \sum_m \Omega_0 \left( \frac{2}{\Delta_m} - \frac{2}{K} \right) \sigma^+ \sigma^- |m\rangle\langle m| \nonumber \\
    &\quad + \sum_m \Omega_0^2 \left[ \left( \frac{1}{K \Delta_m} - \frac{1}{\Delta_m^2} \right) \sigma^+ + \left( \frac{2}{K \Delta_m} - \frac{1}{\Delta_m^2} \right) \sigma^- \right] |m\rangle\langle m|.
\end{align}

Similarly, the cavity operator $c$ transforms under the drive as
\begin{align}
\label{eq:transformc}
    e^{-D_d} c e^{D_d}
    &\approx c + \sum_m \frac{\sqrt{m+1}\,\Omega_0 \chi}{\Delta_m \Delta_{m+1}} (\sigma^+ - \sigma^-) |m\rangle\langle m+1| \nonumber \\
    &\quad + \sum_m \sqrt{m+1}\,\Omega_0^2 \left( \frac{1}{\Delta_{m+1}^2} - \frac{1}{\Delta_m^2} \right) |m\rangle\langle m+1| \nonumber \\
    &\quad - \sum_m \sqrt{m+1}\,\frac{\Omega_0^2 (\chi^2 + \Delta_m \chi)}{(\Delta_m \Delta_{m+1})^2} \sigma^+ \sigma^- |m\rangle\langle m+1|.
\end{align}

In this driven frame, the Lindblad master equation becomes
\begin{align}
\label{eq:Linblad_driven}
    \dot{\rho}_d
    &= -i[H_d, \rho_d] + \gamma_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[e^{-D_d} b e^{D_d}\right] \rho_d + \gamma_{c\downarrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[e^{-D_d} c e^{D_d}\right] \rho_d \nonumber \\
    &\approx -i[H_d, \rho_d] + \gamma_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[ \sigma^- + \sum_m \Omega_0^2 \left( \frac{2}{K \Delta_m} - \frac{1}{\Delta_m^2} \right) \sigma^- |m\rangle\langle m| \right] \rho_d \nonumber \\
    &\quad + \gamma_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[ -\sum_m \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_m} |m\rangle\langle m| \right] \rho_d + \gamma_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[ \sum_m \Omega_0^2 \left( \frac{1}{K \Delta_m} - \frac{1}{\Delta_m^2} \right) \sigma^+ |m\rangle\langle m| \right] \rho_d \nonumber \\
    &\quad + \sum_m \gamma_{c\downarrow, m} \mathcal{D}[|m\rangle\langle m+1|] \rho_d + \mathcal{O}\!\left( \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} \frac{(m+1)\Omega_0^2 \chi^2}{(\Delta_m \Delta_{m+1})^2} \right),
\end{align}
where $\rho_d = U_d^\dagger \rho_R U_d$. The effective cavity decay rate $\gamma_{c\downarrow, m}$ is modified perturbatively by the drive:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_{c\downarrow, m} \approx (m+1) \left[ 1 +2 \Omega_0^2 \left( \frac{1}{\Delta_{m+1}^2} - \frac{1}{\Delta_m^2} \right) \right] \gamma_{c\downarrow}.
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:Linblad_driven} is obtained using the secular approximation: in the interaction picture, the transformed jump operator decomposes into spectral components $\sum_k L_k$, each oscillating at a distinct frequency $\omega_k$. When $\gamma \ll |\omega_j - \omega_k|$, the rapidly oscillating cross terms can be neglected, so the dissipation channels decouple:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma \mathcal{D}\!\left[ \sum_k L_k \right] \rho \approx \sum_k \gamma \mathcal{D}[L_k] \rho.
\end{equation}
For the present system , the relevant frequency splittings are set by $\widetilde{\Delta}_{bd}$ and $\omega_c$. The approximation is valid when $\widetilde{\Delta}_{bd}, \omega_c \gg \gamma_{b\downarrow}$.


We have thus transformed the Lindbladian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Linblad_ch8} into the driven frame, yielding the dissipators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Linblad_driven} and the noise Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:noise_Hamiltoniand}. Below, we derive simplified expressions for the Lindbladian and $H_{\text{noise}}^d$ in both the unselective and selective regimes.

\paragraph{Unselective regime}
As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:creating-stark-sweet-spots}, the unselective regime requires $|\Delta_{bd}|\gg|\chi|$. Retaining terms through $\mathcal{O}[(\chi/\Delta_{bd})^2]$, the master equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Linblad_driven} is approximated by
\begin{align}
\label{unselectivemasterequation1}
\dot{\rho}_d
&= -i [H_d + H_{\text{noise}}^d(t), \rho_d]
+ \widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[ \sigma^- (1 - \lambda_\downarrow c^\dagger c) \right] \rho_d
+ 2\gamma_{c\phi} \mathcal{D}\!\left[ c^\dagger c \right] \rho_d \nonumber \\
&\quad
+ \gamma_{b\uparrow} \mathcal{D}\!\left[ \sigma^+ (1 - \lambda_\uparrow c^\dagger c) \right] \rho_d
+ \widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow} \mathcal{D}[c] \rho_d .
\end{align} 
The noise Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:noise_Hamiltoniand} is approximated by
\begin{align}
\label{unselectivenoisehamiltonian}
H_{\text{noise}}^d(t)
&= \delta\omega_b(t) \Bigg[
\left( \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} - \frac{2 \Omega_0^2 \chi}{\Delta_{bd}^3} \right)c^\dagger c
+ \left( 1 - 2 \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{bd}^2} \right)\sigma^+ \sigma^- \nonumber \\
&\quad
+ \left( \frac{4 \Omega_0^2 \chi}{\Delta_{bd}^3}
- \frac{4 K g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^3} \right)
\sigma^+ \sigma^- c^\dagger c
- \frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_{bd}} \sigma_x
+ \frac{\Omega_0 \chi}{\Delta_{bd}^2} \sigma_x c^\dagger c
\Bigg],
\end{align}
where $\delta\omega_b(t)$ denotes the fluctuation of the FTT frequency induced by flux noise. The renormalized FTT relaxation rate, pure cavity-dephasing rate, FTT excitation rate, and renormalized cavity-decay rate are, respectively,
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}
&\approx \gamma_{b\downarrow}
\left[
1 + 2\left(
\frac{2\Omega_0^2}{K\Delta_{bd}}
- \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{bd}^2}
\right)
\right]
+ \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\Omega_0^4}{\Delta_{bd}^4}\right), \nonumber \\
\gamma_{c\phi}
&= \frac{\gamma_{b\downarrow}}{2}
\left( \frac{\Omega_0 \chi}{\Delta_{bd}^2} \right)^2, \nonumber \\
\gamma_{b\uparrow}
&= \gamma_{b\downarrow}
\left[
\Omega_0^2
\left(
\frac{1}{K\Delta_{bd}}
- \frac{1}{\Delta_{bd}^2}
\right)
\right]^2, \nonumber \\
\widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow}
&\approx \gamma_{c\downarrow}
\left[
1 - \frac{4\chi \Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{bd}^3}
+ \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\chi^2}{\Delta_{bd}^2}\right)
\right].
\end{align}
The photon-number-dependent corrections in Eq.\ \eqref{unselectivemasterequation1} are 
\begin{align}
\lambda_\downarrow
\approx
\frac{2\chi}{\Delta_{bd}}\Omega_0^2
\left(
\frac{1}{K\Delta_{bd}}
- \frac{1}{\Delta_{bd}^2}
\right)
+ \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\Omega_0^4}{\Delta_{bd}^4}\right),\quad
\lambda_\uparrow=
\frac{\chi}{\Delta_{bd}}
\left(
\frac{2K-\Delta_{bd}}{K-\Delta_{bd}}
\right).
\end{align}
More photons in cavity suppress the FTT heating rate and make the relaxation rate toward $\widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}$. Because for higher cavity photons, the detuning $|\Delta_m|$ increases due to dispersive shift, and thus the corresponding states $|g,m\rangle,|e,m\rangle$ has less hybridization.

Neglecting this photon-number dependence due to smallness of $|\Delta_{bd}|/\chi$, and using sweet spot condition in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:ss_unselective} gives the simplified master equation
\begin{align}
\label{eq:Ldiss_unselective}
\dot{\rho}_d
&= -i \bigl[ H_d + H_{\text{noise}}^d, \rho_d \bigr]
+ \widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}} \mathcal{D}[\sigma^-]\rho_d
+ \gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{un}} \mathcal{D}[\sigma^+]\rho_d 
+ 2\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{un}} \mathcal{D}[c^\dagger c]\rho_d
+ \widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}} \mathcal{D}[c]\rho_d .
\end{align}
The effective rates $\widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}}$, $\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{un}}$, $\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{un}}$, and $\widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}}$ are
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}}
&= \gamma_{b\downarrow}
\left(1-\frac{\Delta_{bd}}{K}\right), \quad\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{un}}
= \frac{\gamma_{b\downarrow}}{16}
\left(\frac{\Delta_{bd}}{K}\right)^2
\left(1-\frac{2\Delta_{bd}}{K}\right), \nonumber \\
\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{un}}
&= \frac{1}{2}\gamma_{b\downarrow}
\left(\frac{g}{\Delta_{bc}}\right)^4
\frac{K}{\Delta_{bd}}, \quad
\widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}}
= \gamma_{c\downarrow}
\left(1+\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\right).
\end{align}
Here, $H_d$ is the effective driven Hamiltonian given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hd_far_detuned}, and $H_{\text{noise}}^d$ is the noise Hamiltonian in the effective driven frame. In the unselective limit, the latter takes the approximate form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:Hnoise_unselective}
H_{\text{noise}}^d
&\approx \delta\omega_b \Bigg[
\left(1 - \frac{\Delta_{bd}}{2K}\right)\sigma^+\sigma^-
+ \frac{2g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}
\left(1 - \frac{2K}{\Delta_{bc}}\right)
\sigma^+\sigma^- c^\dagger c
- \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{bd}}{K}}\,\sigma_x
\Bigg].
\end{align}
Although the sweet-spot condition cancels the leading direct cavity-dephasing contribution in Eq.\ \eqref{unselectivenoisehamiltonian} it does not eliminate all noise channels in the effective driven frame. These remaining channels are discussed in next section.

\paragraph{Selective regime.}
In the selective regime, the condition $|\Delta_{m^\star}/\chi|\ll 1$ holds. The hierarchy of energy scales is
\begin{equation}
|\Omega_0| \ll |\Delta_{m^\star}| \ll |\chi| \ll |K|.
\end{equation}
To leading order of $\Omega_0/\Delta_{m^\star}$, and zeroth order of $\Omega_0/|K|$, $\Omega_0/|\chi|$, the master equation in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:Linblad_driven} reduces to
\begin{align}
\label{selectivelinbladmasterequation}
&\dot{\rho} \approx -i\,[H_d + H_{\mathrm{noise}}^{d},\,\rho] + \gamma_{b\downarrow}\,\mathcal{D}\!\left[\sigma^- - \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Delta_{m^\star}^2}\,\sigma^- |m^\star\rangle\langle m^\star|\right]\rho
+ 2\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{sel}}\,\mathcal{D}\!\left[|m^\star\rangle\langle m^\star|\right]\rho \nonumber\\
&+ \gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{sel}}\,\mathcal{D}\!\left[\sigma^+ |m^\star\rangle\langle m^\star|\right]\rho
+ \sum_m \widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow,m}^{\mathrm{sel}}\,\mathcal{D}\!\left[|m\rangle\langle m+1|\right]\rho ,
\end{align}
and the noise Hamiltonian in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:noise_Hamiltoniand} is
\begin{align}
\label{selectivenoisehamiltonian}
H_{\mathrm{noise}}^{d}
\approx \delta\omega_b(t)\Bigg[
&\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\,c^\dagger c
+ \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\,|m^\star\rangle\langle m^\star| + \sigma^+\sigma^-\!\left(\frac{4Kg^2}{\Delta_{bc}^3}\,c^\dagger c
+ 2\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\,|m^\star\rangle\langle m^\star|\right) \nonumber\\
&- \frac{g}{\Delta_{bc}}\,\sigma^+ |m^\star\rangle\langle m^\star|
+ \mathrm{h.c.}
\Bigg].
\end{align}
The FTT excitation, renormalized cavity relaxation and cavity pure dephasing rates are 
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{sel}} = \left(\frac{g^4}{\Delta_{bc}^4}\right)\gamma_{b\downarrow}, \qquad
\gamma_{c\downarrow,m}^{\mathrm{sel}} = \gamma_{c\downarrow}^{\mathrm{sel}}\left(1 - 2\delta_{m^*,m}\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\right), \qquad
\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{sel}} = \left(\frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2}\right)\frac{\gamma_{b\downarrow}^{\mathrm{sel}}}{2}.
\end{equation}
To obtain these expressions, the sweet spot condition in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:weak_drive_sweetspot} is used here.

\subsection{Analytical Estimate of the Dephasing Rate}
\label{subsec:analytical-dephasing-rate}

With the effective master equation established in the preceding section, we now extract analytical expressions for the cavity dephasing rate in the two operating regimes.

\section*{Unselective regime.}
\label{subsec:unselective_analytical}

The dissipators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ldiss_unselective} describe (i) drive-renormalized FTT relaxation, $\widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}}\mathcal{D}[\sigma^-]$; (ii) drive-induced FTT excitation, $\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{un}}\mathcal{D}[\sigma^+]$, which gives rise to cavity photon-shot-noise dephasing through the dispersive coupling \cite{shotnoise,PhysRevA.75.042302Ashphotonshot}; (iii) cavity pure dephasing, $2\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{un}}\mathcal{D}[c^\dagger c]$ (the prefactor $2$ is conventional); and (iv) renormalized cavity decay, $\widetilde{\gamma}_{c\downarrow}^{\mathrm{un}}\mathcal{D}[c]$.

The noise Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hnoise_unselective} also contains several contributions. First, it includes dispersive-shift-induced cavity-frequency fluctuations. We neglect this contribution because the FTT remains close to its ground state when it is initialized there. Second, the residual dephasing is dominated by a transverse component proportional to $\sigma_x$, which induces FTT depolarization under $1/f$ flux noise. The corresponding transverse-noise-induced excitation rate at the sweet spot is
\begin{equation}
\label{unselective1/f}
\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{1/f,\mathrm{un}}=\left(\frac{\Omega_0}{\Delta_{bd}}\frac{\partial\omega_b}{\partial\Phi}\right)^2\frac{S_0^2}{|\Delta_{bd}/2\pi|}
\overset{\eqref{eq:ss_unselective}}{=}\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial\omega_b}{\partial\Phi}\right)^2\frac{S_0^2}{|K/2\pi|}.
\end{equation}

In the unselective regime, the total cavity-dephasing rate is the sum of the photon-shot-noise contribution and the pure-dephasing contribution. We approximate the photon-shot-noise contribution by the FTT heating rate in the limit $\chi \gg \widetilde{\gamma}_{b\downarrow}$ \cite{serge}, because a single photon event is sufficient to completely destroy the cavity phase coherence before the photon decays. The total rate is therefore
\begin{align}
\label{eq:Gamma_phi_total_unselective}
\Gamma_{\phi,\mathrm{total}}
=\left(\frac{\partial\omega_b}{\partial\Phi}\right)^2\frac{S_0^2}{|4K/2\pi|}
+\frac{\gamma_{b\downarrow}}{16}\left(\frac{\Delta_{bd}}{K}\right)^2\bigg(1-\frac{2\Delta_{bd}}{K}\bigg)
+\frac{\gamma_{b\downarrow}}{2}\left(\frac{g}{\Delta_{bc}}\right)^4\frac{K}{\Delta_{bd}}.
\end{align}



\section*{Selective regime.}
\label{subsec:selective_analytical}

The dissipators in Eq.~\eqref{selectivelinbladmasterequation} can be interpreted as follows: (i) FTT relaxation with a cavity-state-dependent correction, together with cavity decay at the renormalized rate $\gamma_{c\downarrow,m}^{\mathrm{sel}}$; (ii) FTT heating at rate $\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{sel}}$ when the cavity occupies $|m^\star\rangle$; and (iii) pure dephasing between $|m^\star\rangle$ and $|m^\star+1\rangle$ at rate $\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{sel}}$.

At the sweet spot, $H_{\mathrm{noise}}^{d}$ does not induce dephasing between $|g,m^\star\rangle$ and $|g,m^\star+1\rangle$. Residual cavity dephasing from dispersive-shift fluctuations is negligible for the dynamics of interest, because the FTT remains close to its ground state when initialized there. Instead, the transverse component induces depolarization. Within a Markovian approximation, the corresponding excitation rate due to $1/f$ noise is
\begin{equation}
\label{selective1/f}
\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{1/f,\mathrm{sel}}
= \left( \frac{\partial \omega_b}{\partial \Phi} \right)^2
\left( \frac{g}{\Delta_{bc}} \right)^2
S_{1/f}(\Delta_{bd}) .
\end{equation}

Dephasing between $|g,m^\star\rangle$ and $|g,m^\star+1\rangle$ is therefore limited by cavity pure dephasing at rate $\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{sel}} \propto g^2/\Delta_{bc}^2$ and by $1/f$-noise-induced FTT excitation at rate $\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{1/f,\mathrm{sel}}$. The total dephasing rate is thus
\begin{align}
\label{eq:Gamma_phi_total_selective}
\Gamma^{\mathrm{sel}}_{\phi,\mathrm{total}}
= \left( \frac{\partial \omega_b}{\partial \Phi} \right)^2
\left( \frac{g}{\Delta_{bc}} \right)^2
\frac{S_0^2}{|\Delta_{bd}/2\pi|}
+ \left( \frac{g^2}{\Delta_{bc}^2} \right)\frac{\gamma_{b\downarrow}^{\mathrm{sel}}}{2}.
\end{align}

\subsection{Numerical Validation}
\label{subsec:mc-validation}

Full-system simulations are used to extract the cavity dephasing rate under drive; the
numerical procedure and fitting protocol are summarized in
App.~\ref{Numerical details of obtaining the cavity dephasing rates}. The noise amplitude $S_0$ and the
detuning $\Delta_{bd}$ are sampled over the parameter range of interest. For each
$\Delta_{bd}$, the drive amplitude is chosen to satisfy the sweet-spot condition, and
the corresponding total dephasing rate $\Gamma_{\phi,\mathrm{total}}$ is extracted.

Figure~\ref{fig:total_rate}(a) compares the numerical (solid lines) and analytical results (dashed lines). For $S_0/\Phi_0=10^{-5}$, the
photon-shot noise due to FTT excitation at rate $\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{1/f}$ dominates the dephasing rate over the scanned detuning range. At large detuning,
$\lvert\Delta_{bd}\rvert/2\pi>\lvert\chi\rvert/2\pi\simeq 2~\mathrm{MHz}$, the total
rate is well described by $\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{1/f,\mathrm{un}}$ in Eq.\ \eqref{unselective1/f}, which is independent
of detuning. At smaller detuning, for example
$-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi=1~\mathrm{MHz}<\lvert\chi\rvert/2\pi$, the rate increases as the
dynamics enters the selective regime, consistent with the expected
$1/\lvert\Delta_{bd}\rvert$ scaling in Eq.\ \eqref{selective1/f}.

For a smaller noise amplitude, such as $S_0/\Phi_0=10^{-6}$, the $S_0$-independent contributions $\gamma_{b\uparrow}$ and
$\gamma_{c\phi}$ dominate $\Gamma_{\phi,\mathrm{total}}$. At large
detuning, for example at $-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi=19~\mathrm{MHz}$,
$\gamma_{b\uparrow}^{\mathrm{un}}\propto(\Delta_{bd}/K)^2$ dominates, while
$\gamma_{c\phi}^{\mathrm{un}}\propto \lvert K/\Delta_{bd}\rvert$ is suppressed. The
opposite hierarchy holds at small detuning, for example at
$-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi=1~\mathrm{MHz}$. Consequently,
$-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi=6~\mathrm{MHz}$ yields the minimum
$\Gamma_{\phi,\mathrm{total}}$ within the scanned range. In the undriven case, the
dephasing time increases from $\sim 0.1~\mathrm{ms}$ to $\sim 1~\mathrm{ms}$ as
$S_0/\Phi_0$ decreases from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$. Under sweet-spot driving at
$-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi\simeq 6~\mathrm{MHz}$, the dephasing time is enhanced by up to
$\sim 20\times$ across the considered $S_0$ range, confirming the overall suppression
of cavity dephasing.

We perform time-domain simulations
that include relaxation and stochastic flux noise with $S_0/\Phi_0=10^{-5}$. The
initial state is
$\ket{\psi_0}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\ket{g,0}+\ket{g,1}\big)$. In the driven case,
the state is adiabatically mapped onto the target Floquet states using a shaped drive
pulse,
\begin{equation}
2\Omega(t)\cos\!\big(\omega t+\phi(t)\big).
\end{equation}
In the rotating frame, this gives the $I$ and $Q$ quadratures
\begin{align}
\Omega_I(t) &= \Omega(t)\cos\phi(t), \\
\Omega_Q(t) &= \Omega(t)\sin\phi(t).
\end{align}
We implement a DRAG-like correction,
\begin{equation}
\Omega_Q(t) = -\frac{\dot{\Omega}_I(t)}{\Delta_{bd}},
\end{equation}
which accelerates the adiabatic transfer relative to ramps that use only
$\Omega_I(t)$; see App.~\ref{app:adiabatic-prep}. A ramp with
$\tau=75~\mathrm{ns}$ is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:total_rate}(b).

The dephasing is quantified by the coherence operator
\begin{align}
X(t) &= \ket{\phi_{g,1}(t)}\!\bra{\phi_{g,0}(t)} + \mathrm{H.c.}, \\
P_X(t) &= \mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho(t)X(t)\right],
\end{align}
where $\ket{\phi_{g,1}(t)}$ and $\ket{\phi_{g,0}(t)}$ are the Floquet
states that are adiabatically transferred from the bare states $\ket{g,1}$ and $\ket{g,0}$,
respectively. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:total_rate}(c), pure dephasing from $1/f$ flux noise dominates the decay of $P_X(t)$, leading to sub-Gaussian decay. With the sweet-spot drive, the decay of $P_X(t)$ receives comparable contributions from relaxation and pure dephasing. The decay is exponential because the residual channel is Markovian and arises from FTT depolarization. The extracted pure-dephasing time at the sweet spot reaches $\sim 2~\mathrm{ms}$, compared with $\sim 100~\mu\mathrm{s}$ in the undriven case.


\section{Application to Bosonic Quantum Error Correction}
\label{sec:bosonic-qec}
In the previous sections, we focused on the \(\ket{g,0}\) and \(\ket{g,1}\) states and showed that operating at a sweet spot can enhance the cavity dephasing time by more than an order of magnitude. More generally, this mechanism is not restricted to a single pair of Fock states. In the unselective regime, the drive can simultaneously suppress dephasing for multiple pairs of Fock states. This makes the scheme particularly well suited to the binomial code. It can likewise be extended to multi-cavity architectures, which we illustrate using the dual-rail code.

\subsection{Binomial encoding}
\label{subsec:binomial-codes}

As a concrete example, consider the minimal binomial code~\cite{PhysRevX.6.031006Binonmial}
\begin{equation}
|0_L\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|4\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad |1_L\rangle=|2\rangle,
\end{equation}
which corrects a single photon-loss event. In this encoding, dephasing is not associated with a single pair of Fock states. Rather, the logical information, the error space after photon loss, and the intermediate states populated during recovery are supported by several bare-state coherences. We consider an FTT--cavity system in which the FTT serves as the ancilla. A drive applied to the FTT can suppress the transition-frequency fluctuations that dominate dephasing of several pair of Fock states. We now identify these pairs explicitly.

Within the code space, the logical basis state \( |0_L\rangle \propto |0\rangle + |4\rangle \) is sensitive to the fluctuation \( \delta\widetilde{\omega}_{c,0\rightarrow 4}(t) \), which induces dephasing between \( |0\rangle \) and \( |4\rangle \) and therefore distorts \( |0_L\rangle \). More generally, because this code does not protect against pure dephasing, the coherence of an arbitrary logical superposition \( \alpha|0_L\rangle + \beta|1_L\rangle \) is carried by the bare-state coherences \( |0\rangle\!\leftrightarrow\!|2\rangle \) and \( |2\rangle\!\leftrightarrow\!|4\rangle \). Suppressing the corresponding dephasing rates \( \gamma_{\phi,0\rightarrow 2} \) and \( \gamma_{\phi,2\rightarrow 4} \) therefore directly improves the logical coherence time.

After a single photon loss, the code space maps as \( |0_L\rangle \rightarrow |3\rangle \) and \( |1_L\rangle \rightarrow |1\rangle \), so the logical information temporarily resides in the coherence \( |1\rangle\!\leftrightarrow\!|3\rangle \) before recovery is applied. Suppressing \( \gamma_{\phi,1\rightarrow 3} \) is therefore important for high-fidelity error correction. During the recovery itself, intermediate superpositions populate the \( \{|0\rangle,|3\rangle,|4\rangle\} \) and \( \{|1\rangle,|2\rangle\} \) subspaces, making the associated dephasing channels---for example, \( \gamma_{\phi,0\rightarrow 3} \), \( \gamma_{\phi,3\rightarrow 4} \), and \( \gamma_{\phi,1\rightarrow 2} \)---additional targets for suppression

To quantify the improvement, we sweep the detuning \(\Delta_{bd}\) at fixed \(\Omega_0/2\pi = 10\,\mathrm{MHz}\) and evaluate the dephasing rate due to \(1/f\) flux noise,
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{\phi,n\rightarrow m}
=
S_0
\bigg|
\frac{\partial \widetilde{\omega}_{c,n\rightarrow m}}{\partial \Phi}
\bigg|
\sqrt{
2\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{ir}}t}\right)
},
\label{eq:qec_dephasing_rate}
\end{equation}
where \(S_0\) is the flux-noise amplitude, \(\omega_{\mathrm{ir}}\) is the infrared cutoff of the \(1/f\) noise spectrum, and \(t\) is the experimental timescale. Equivalently, for \(\omega_{\mathrm{ir}}t<1\), the logarithmic factor is \( |\ln(\omega_{\mathrm{ir}}t)| \). An earlier experiment used \( |\ln(\omega_{\mathrm{ir}}t)|\approx 4 \) \cite{PhysRevX.7.031037factor4}, and we adopt this value in the following numerics.

The results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qec_bosonic_driven}(a). The minima of several dephasing rates overlap near \(-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi \simeq 30\,\mathrm{MHz}\), consistent with the unselective regime \(|\Delta_{bd}|\gg |\chi|\) considered here, where \(|\chi|/2\pi \sim 0.5\,\mathrm{MHz}\). Choosing \(\Delta_{bd}/2\pi = -30\,\mathrm{MHz}\) yields a worst-case dephasing-rate reduction of \(\sim 13\times\) across the relevant transitions.

This improvement is  evident in the Wigner tomography in Fig.~\ref{fig:qec_bosonic_driven}(b). For the initial logical superposition \(\ket{+_L} = (\ket{0_L}+\ket{1_L})/\sqrt{2}\), the undriven Wigner function becomes nearly diagonal in the Fock basis after \(300\,\mu\mathrm{s}\), indicating substantial loss of logical coherence. In contrast, the driven case largely retains the characteristic interference fringes, showing that the drive preserves the photon-number coherences that define the logical state.

This behavior is also reflected quantitatively in the decoherence-induced infidelity. We use
\begin{equation}
I=1-\sqrt{\bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}},
\label{eq:qec_infidelity}
\end{equation}
with \(\ket{\psi}=\ket{+_L}\) the initial state and \(\rho\) the realized density matrix at time \(t\). After \(300\,\mu\mathrm{s}\), the infidelity decreases from \(0.3\) in the undriven case to \(0.043\) in the driven case, with the loss arising solely from the modeled decoherence channels.
\subsection{Dual-rail encoding}
\label{subsec:dual-rail}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{chapter4/figures/dualrailillustration.pdf}
    \caption[Schematic of two dual-rail qubits coupled through a central FTT.]{Schematic of two dual-rail qubits coupled through a central FTT. Flux noise \(\delta\Phi(t)\) shifts the FTT frequency and, through the dispersive Lamb shift, induces inherited frequency fluctuations in the coupled cavities.}
    \label{fig:dualrail_illustration}
\end{figure}

Dual-rail encoding is widely used because the dominant single-photon-loss error maps out of the logical subspace into a detectable erasure \cite{dualrailcavity1,teoh2023dual}. A dual-rail qubit is encoded in two cavities with logical states \(\ket{0_L}=\ket{01}\) and \(\ket{1_L}=\ket{10}\). The fault-tolerance advantage of this encoding relies on an error hierarchy in which single-photon loss dominates over dephasing. However, inherited cavity dephasing from a flux-tunable coupler can degrade this bias. To implement two-qubit gates, one approach is to couple the relevant rails to an FTT and exploit its three-wave-mixing interaction. A representative architecture is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dualrail_illustration}, where a central FTT mediates interactions between two dual-rail qubits while remaining sensitive to flux noise \(\delta\Phi(t)\).

In this architecture, the FTT couples to cavities labeled by \(i=1,2\). Flux fluctuations \(\delta\Phi(t)\) modulate the FTT frequency and, in the dispersive limit, this modulation is inherited by each cavity through its Lamb shift, producing cavity-frequency fluctuations
\begin{equation}
\delta \widetilde{\omega}^{(i)}_{c}(t)
=
\biggl(\frac{g^{(i)}}{\Delta^{(i)}_{bc}}\biggr)^2
\delta\omega_b(t),
\end{equation}
where \(g^{(i)}\) and \(\Delta^{(i)}_{bc}\) are the coupling strength and detuning between the FTT and cavity \(i\), respectively. Only the differential component of the rail-frequency noise produces logical dephasing in a dual-rail qubit. Reducing the inherited dephasing rates of the individual cavities therefore suppresses this differential-noise contribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:qec_bosonic_driven}(c) shows the numerically computed dephasing rate \(\gamma_{\phi,0\rightarrow 1}^{(i)}\) for each coupled cavity as \(\Delta_{bd}\) is swept. Choosing \(-\Delta_{bd}/2\pi\simeq 29\,\mathrm{MHz}\) yields \(\sim 10\times\) lower dephasing rates for both cavities than in the undriven case.

We next consider two dual-rail qubits initialized in the Bell state
\begin{equation}
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{Bell}}}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\left(\ket{00_L}+\ket{11_L}\right),
\end{equation}
and plot the state tomography within the logical subspace in Fig.~\ref{fig:qec_bosonic_driven}(d). The dominant effect of the driven sweet spot is the preservation of the off-diagonal coherence between \(\ket{00_L}\) and \(\ket{11_L}\), consistent with the enhanced dephasing time. By contrast, the reduced heights of the diagonal elements mainly reflect photon-loss-induced leakage out of the logical subspace. This leakage remains nearly unchanged between the driven and undriven cases, indicating that the sweet-spot drive has negligible effect on the erasure error because it only weakly changes the cavity photon-loss rates. Quantitatively, using the same infidelity definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:qec_infidelity}, the decoherence-induced infidelity is reduced from \(0.18\) in the undriven case to \(0.011\) in the driven case.

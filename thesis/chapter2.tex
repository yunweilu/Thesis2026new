\chapter{Bosonic control with ancilla and coupler}
\label{preliminary}

This chapter develops the control framework for bosonic modes in superconducting circuits. Throughout this chapter, we set $\hbar=1$, so energies and angular frequencies are used interchangeably unless stated otherwise. The discussion begins with the quantized LC oscillator, which provides the basic description of a cavity mode and clarifies the limitations of purely linear dynamics. It then introduces the transmon as a weakly anharmonic ancilla and shows how its dispersive coupling to the cavity enables conditional control, including ECD gates and arbitrary single-cavity unitaries. Finally, it introduces flux-mediated three-wave mixing, which provides beam-splitter and two-mode-squeezing interactions for entangling multiple bosonic modes.

\section{Oscillator Circuit Hamiltonian}
\label{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian}

The simplest circuit is the lossless LC oscillator, which serves as the foundational circuit element of superconducting quantum circuits. Its importance is twofold. First, it provides a direct correspondence between circuit variables and canonical variables in quantum mechanics. Second, it illustrates a fundamental limitation: a purely linear oscillator, even when driven by classical fields, cannot generate non-Gaussian states such as Fock states from the vacuum. This limitation motivates the introduction of a nonlinear element in the next section.

\subsection{Quantization of the LC oscillator}

Consider an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ connected in parallel. The natural generalized coordinate is the node flux
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} V(t')\,dt',
\end{equation}
where $V(t)$ is the node voltage. The conjugate variable is the node charge $Q$, which is associated with the capacitor branch. In terms of the flux coordinate, the classical Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\Phi,\dot{\Phi}) = \frac{C}{2}\dot{\Phi}^{2} - \frac{1}{2L}\Phi^{2}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the electrostatic energy stored in the capacitor, while the second term is the magnetic energy stored in the inductor.

The canonical momentum conjugate to $\Phi$ is
\begin{equation}
    Q \equiv \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\Phi}} = C\dot{\Phi}.
\end{equation}
This is precisely the capacitor charge. Performing the Legendre transform gives the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H = Q\dot{\Phi} - \mathcal{L}
      = \frac{Q^{2}}{2C} + \frac{\Phi^{2}}{2L}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the LC circuit is formally identical to a mechanical harmonic oscillator, with the correspondence
\begin{equation}
    \Phi \leftrightarrow x,
    \qquad
    Q \leftrightarrow p,
    \qquad
    C \leftrightarrow m,
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{L} \leftrightarrow m\omega^{2}.
\end{equation}
The resonance frequency is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\end{equation}

Canonical quantization promotes $\Phi$ and $Q$ to operators satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q}] = i.
\end{equation}
The quantum Hamiltonian is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{0} = \frac{\hat{Q}^{2}}{2C} + \frac{\hat{\Phi}^{2}}{2L}.
\end{equation}

It is convenient to introduce the characteristic impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}},
\end{equation}
and the zero-point fluctuations
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \sqrt{\frac{Z}{2}},
    \qquad
    Q_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2Z}}.
\end{equation}
The annihilation and creation operators are then defined through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Phi} = \Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}),
    \qquad
    \hat{Q} = -iQ_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}),
\end{equation}
with $[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}] = 1$. Substituting these expressions into $\hat{H}_{0}$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{0}
    =
    \omega_{0}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is the standard quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian.

The eigenstates of this Hamiltonian are the Fock states $\{\ket{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$, defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\ket{n} = n\ket{n}.
\end{equation}
These states have equally spaced energies,
\begin{equation}
    E_{n} = \omega_{0}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Because the level spacing is uniform, the linear oscillator does not distinguish one transition from another. This spectral degeneracy is the central obstacle to selective state preparation using only linear control.

\subsection{Classical voltage drive of a linear oscillator}

A classical voltage source couples linearly to a linear LC oscillator. In the circuit description, a drive voltage $V_d(t)$ applied through a capacitive coupling modifies the electrostatic energy and therefore adds a term linear in the node charge operator $\hat Q$. After quantization, $\hat Q$ is proportional to a linear combination of $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$, so the driven oscillator Hamiltonian can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \hat H(t)
    =
    \omega_0 \hat a^\dagger \hat a
    +
    f(t)\hat a
    +
    f^*(t)\hat a^\dagger,
\end{equation}
up to a constant energy offset, which shifts all levels equally and can be absorbed into the zero of energy. The precise form of $f(t)$ depends on the drive amplitude and coupling geometry.

The dynamics generated by this Hamiltonian can be treated exactly by moving to a displaced frame. Define
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\tilde \psi(t)}
    =
    \hat D^\dagger[\alpha(t)] \ket{\psi(t)},
    \qquad
    \hat D(\alpha)
    =
    \exp\!\left(\alpha \hat a^\dagger - \alpha^* \hat a\right).
\end{equation}
Substituting this transformation into the Schr\"odinger equation and requiring that the terms linear in $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$ cancel yields the equation of motion
\begin{equation}
    \dot \alpha(t)
    =
    -i\omega_0 \alpha(t) - i f^*(t).
\end{equation}
With this choice of $\alpha(t)$, the transformed Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm disp}(t)
    =
    \omega_0 \hat a^\dagger \hat a
    +
    E(t),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E(t)
    =
    \omega_0 |\alpha(t)|^2
    +
    f(t)\alpha(t)
    +
    f^*(t)\alpha^*(t)
    -
    \frac{i}{2}
    \bigl(\alpha^*(t)\dot\alpha(t) - \dot\alpha^*(t)\alpha(t)\bigr)
\end{equation}
is a real $c$-number. Being a $c$-number, it contributes only a global phase and can be ignored. The driven evolution is therefore exactly equivalent to the usual harmonic evolution generated by $\omega_0 \hat a^\dagger \hat a$, which produces phase-space rotation at angular frequency $\omega_0$, together with a displacement set by $\alpha(t)$.

In particular, if the oscillator starts in the vacuum state and $\alpha(0)=0$, then classical voltage drive maps vacuum only to a coherent state
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi(t)}
    =
    e^{-i\phi(t)} \ket{\alpha(t)}.
\end{equation}

This immediately explains why excited Fock states cannot be prepared from $\ket{0}$ by classical driving alone. In a perfectly harmonic oscillator, every adjacent transition has the same energy $\omega_0$, so a resonant linear drive cannot isolate only the $\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}$ transition without also coupling $\ket{1}\leftrightarrow\ket{2}$, $\ket{2}\leftrightarrow\ket{3}$, and so on. As a result, the drive produces phase-space motion rather than deterministic preparation of a specific number state.

This limitation motivates the introduction of anharmonicity. Once the level spacings become nonuniform, individual transitions can be spectrally resolved, and nonclassical states such as Fock states become accessible.

\section{Transmon as a Weakly Anharmonic System}
\label{sec:transmon}

The linear LC oscillator provides a clean starting point for circuit quantization, but its equally spaced spectrum prevents selective preparation of non-Gaussian states. A superconducting qubit requires a circuit element that breaks exact harmonicity. This role is played by the Josephson junction. When a junction is shunted by a large capacitance, the resulting device---the transmon---forms a weakly anharmonic oscillator whose lowest two levels can be isolated and used as an effective qubit.
\subsection{Josephson Hamiltonian}

A Josephson junction consists of two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier through which Cooper pairs tunnel coherently. Unlike a linear inductor, whose energy is quadratic in current, the tunneling energy depends periodically on the superconducting phase difference across the barrier. This nonlinear phase dependence is the origin of the anharmonicity that distinguishes a Josephson circuit from an LC oscillator. The critical current $I_c$ sets the maximum dissipationless current that the junction can support.

The junction is characterized by the nonlinear current--phase relation
\begin{equation}
    I = I_c \sin \varphi,
\end{equation}
and by the Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi},
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ is the superconducting phase difference across the junction and $\Phi_0 = h/2e$ is the flux quantum. The superconducting phase $\varphi$ and the Cooper-pair number $n$ are conjugate variables, analogous to position and momentum, reflecting the number--phase uncertainty of the condensate. Upon quantization they become operators satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\hat{\varphi},\hat{n}] = i.
\end{equation}
The charging energy is
\begin{equation}
    E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}.
\end{equation}
In general the circuit Hamiltonian includes an offset charge $n_g$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    4E_C (\hat n - n_g)^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
\end{equation}
Here $n_g$ accounts for stray static charges that shift the effective island charge. In the transmon regime $E_J \gg E_C$, the resulting charge dispersion is exponentially suppressed, so to leading order one may neglect $n_g$ and write
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
    \label{eq:JJ_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This Hamiltonian already shows the essential difference from the LC oscillator. The charging term is quadratic, but the Josephson potential is cosine rather than quadratic, and therefore introduces the nonlinearity required for anharmonic energy levels. The transmon is the most widely used realization of this circuit, obtained by operating in the regime where the Josephson energy greatly exceeds the charging energy.

\subsection{Transmon limit and weak anharmonicity}

The transmon operates in the regime
\begin{equation}
    E_J \gg E_C,
\end{equation}
where phase fluctuations are small and the cosine potential may be expanded around one of its minima, typically $\varphi = 0$:
\begin{equation}
    -E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}
    \approx
    -E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\hat{\varphi}^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\hat{\varphi}^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\hat{\varphi}^6).
\end{equation}
Substituting this into Eq.~\eqref{eq:JJ_hamiltonian} gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    \approx
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    +
    \frac{E_J}{2}\hat{\varphi}^2
    -
    \frac{E_J}{24}\hat{\varphi}^4
    -
    E_J.
\end{equation}
The first two terms define a harmonic oscillator, while the quartic correction produces a weak anharmonicity. To make this explicit, we introduce ladder operators through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\varphi} = \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger),
    \qquad
    \hat{n} = i n_{\mathrm{zpf}}(\hat{b}^\dagger-\hat{b}),
\end{equation}
where the zero-point fluctuations are fixed by requiring the quadratic Hamiltonian to take the standard harmonic-oscillator form. This gives
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{2E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/4},
    \qquad
    n_{\mathrm{zpf}} = \left(\frac{E_J}{32E_C}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
The quadratic part then becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{harm}}
    =
    \omega_p
    \left(
        \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{b} + \frac{1}{2}
    \right)
    -
    E_J,
\end{equation}
with plasma frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \sqrt{8E_JE_C}.
\end{equation}
This is the Josephson analogue of the LC resonance frequency $1/\sqrt{LC}$, with the identification
\begin{equation}
    L_J = \frac{(\Phi_0/2\pi)^2}{E_J},
    \qquad
    C = C_\Sigma.
\end{equation}
The Josephson inductance $L_J$ is the effective inductance obtained by linearizing the junction about $\varphi=0$; unlike a geometric inductance, it is set by the junction parameters and can be tuned in flux-controlled circuits.
\subsection{Flux-tunable transmon}

Replacing a single Josephson junction by a SQUID loop makes the effective Josephson energy tunable by external magnetic flux. A SQUID consists of two Josephson junctions connected in parallel around a superconducting loop. An external flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$ threading the loop imposes a phase bias between the two junctions, so the total Josephson tunneling amplitude is the coherent sum of two paths. As a result, the effective Josephson energy varies periodically with flux through constructive or destructive interference.

For junction energies $E_{J1}$ and $E_{J2}$, the SQUID behaves as a single junction with flux-dependent effective Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    =
    E_{J\Sigma}
    \sqrt{
        \cos^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
        +
        d^2
        \sin^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    },
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_{J\Sigma} = E_{J1}+E_{J2},
    \qquad
    d = \frac{E_{J1}-E_{J2}}{E_{J1}+E_{J2}}.
\end{equation}
The parameter $d$ measures the junction asymmetry. For $d=0$, the SQUID is symmetric and the tunability is maximal. For $d>0$, the effective Josephson energy no longer vanishes at half a flux quantum, so the tuning range is reduced but the spectrum retains a nonzero minimum frequency.

The transmon transition frequency therefore acquires flux dependence,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_q(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_C E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}})}
    -
    E_C.
\end{equation}
At $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$, the Josephson energy is maximal and has zero slope with respect to flux, so the qubit frequency is first-order insensitive to small flux fluctuations. This operating point is the flux sweet spot and minimizes dephasing from low-frequency flux noise. Away from the sweet spot, the qubit becomes increasingly flux sensitive, which enables tunability but also increases susceptibility to noise.

In practice, flux tuning allows the qubit frequency to be varied over a range of several GHz, while the anharmonicity remains approximately $\alpha \approx -E_C$ because $E_C$ is set primarily by the circuit capacitance and is largely independent of flux. External flux therefore provides direct control of the transmon spectrum: it can be used to bring the qubit into and out of resonance with other circuit elements, adjust detunings, and selectively activate interactions that are otherwise off resonant.

This flux tunability is also the basis of parametric control. By modulating $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}$ in time, one can generate sideband interactions between the transmon and coupled cavity modes, including beam-splitter, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:three_wave_mixing_two_mode}.

\section{ECD Scheme for Arbitrary Single-Cavity Unitary}
\label{sec:ecd_single_cavity}

Section~\ref{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian} showed that a single linear cavity driven only by classical microwave fields remains confined to Gaussian dynamics. In particular, starting from vacuum, such control cannot produce Fock states or implement general non-Gaussian unitaries on the cavity mode. The transmon introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transmon} provides the missing nonlinearity. When the cavity is dispersively coupled to the transmon, the qubit can mediate conditional phase-space operations on the cavity, and these operations promote the cavity from a Gaussian system to a controllable bosonic mode. To formulate this control scheme correctly, however, one should begin from the full weakly anharmonic transmon ladder and only then reduce to an effective two-level ancilla.

\subsection{Dispersive cavity--transmon coupling}
\label{sec:ecd_dispersive_coupling}

The transmon is not fundamentally a two-level system, but a weakly anharmonic oscillator with eigenstates
\(\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0}^\infty\), obtained by diagonalizing the Josephson Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{T}}
    =
    4E_C \hat{n}^2
    -
    E_J \cos \hat{\varphi}.
\end{equation}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:transmon}, its spectrum forms a ladder of levels with transition frequencies
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{j+1,j}
    =
    E_{j+1}-E_j
    \approx
    \omega_{01} + j\alpha,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha<0\) is the transmon anharmonicity. Because \(|\alpha|\) is small compared with \(\omega_{01}\), the transmon remains close to a harmonic oscillator, but its level spacings are not exactly equal. This weak anharmonicity is sufficient to isolate the lowest transition under appropriate control conditions.

Now couple the transmon capacitively to a single cavity mode of frequency \(\omega_c\), described by the annihilation operator \(\hat{a}\). In the transmon eigenbasis, the uncoupled Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_0
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty \omega_j \ket{j}\bra{j},
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_j = E_j\). The capacitive interaction arises through the transmon charge operator \(\hat{n}\), whose dominant matrix elements connect neighboring transmon levels. The coupled cavity--transmon system is therefore described by the generalized Jaynes--Cummings Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty \omega_j \ket{j}\bra{j}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty g_j
    \left(
        \hat{a}^\dagger \ket{j}\bra{j+1}
        +
        \hat{a}\ket{j+1}\bra{j}
    \right),
    \label{eq:multilevel_JC}
\end{equation}
where \(g_j\) is the cavity coupling matrix element for the \(\ket{j}\leftrightarrow\ket{j+1}\) transition. In the transmon regime, these couplings approximately scale as
\begin{equation}
    g_j \approx \sqrt{j+1}\,g_0,
\end{equation}
reflecting the near-oscillator character of the device.

The ECD protocol operates in the dispersive regime, where each relevant transmon transition is far detuned from the cavity,
\begin{equation}
    |\Delta_j|
    =
    |\omega_{j+1,j}-\omega_c|
    \gg
    g_j\sqrt{n+1},
\end{equation}
for all significantly occupied photon numbers \(n\). In this limit, real exchange of excitations is suppressed, and the interaction can be treated perturbatively. To second order, the energy shift of the bare state \(\ket{j,n}\) is
\begin{equation}
    \delta E_{j,n}^{(2)}
    =
    \left[
        \frac{(n+1)g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}}
        -
        \frac{n g_j^2}{\Delta_j}
    \right],
\end{equation}
with the convention \(g_{-1}=0\). Reorganizing these shifts yields the multilevel dispersive Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(\mathrm{multi})}
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty
    \left(\omega_j+\Lambda_j\right)\ket{j}\bra{j}
    +
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty
    \chi_j \ket{j}\bra{j},
    \label{eq:multilevel_disp}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_j = \frac{g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}},
    \qquad
    \chi_j
    =
    \frac{g_{j-1}^2}{\Delta_{j-1}}
    -
    \frac{g_j^2}{\Delta_j}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:multilevel_disp} shows that each transmon level pulls the cavity by a different amount. The cavity therefore acquires a transmon-state-dependent frequency shift, while the transmon levels acquire Lamb shifts.

At this stage the ancilla is still a multilevel system. The reduction to an effective qubit is an approximation that must be justified. In the present setting, that approximation is valid for three reasons. First, the control pulses are designed to act resonantly on the \(\ket{g}\equiv\ket{0}\) to \(\ket{e}\equiv\ket{1}\) transition, while the next transition is detuned by the anharmonicity \(|\alpha|\). Second, the qubit-drive Rabi rate and spectral bandwidth are chosen small enough that off-resonant excitation of \(\ket{f}\equiv\ket{2}\) and higher states remains negligible. Third, the cavity population is kept below the critical photon number associated with the dispersive approximation, so that the cavity does not strongly hybridize with higher transmon levels. Under these conditions, population leakage out of \(\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}\) is weak, and the transmon can be treated as an effective two-level ancilla for the purpose of gate synthesis.

Projecting Eq.~\eqref{eq:multilevel_disp} onto the lowest two transmon levels gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(ge)}
    =
    \omega_c \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    (\omega_g+\Lambda_g)\ket{g}\bra{g}
    +
    (\omega_e+\Lambda_e)\ket{e}\bra{e}
    +
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    \left(
        \chi_g \ket{g}\bra{g}
        +
        \chi_e \ket{e}\bra{e}
    \right).
\end{equation}
Introducing
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}_z = \ket{e}\bra{e} - \ket{g}\bra{g},
\end{equation}
together with the dressed qubit frequency
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\omega}_q
    =
    (\omega_e+\Lambda_e)-(\omega_g+\Lambda_g),
\end{equation}
the mean cavity pull
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\chi} = \frac{\chi_e+\chi_g}{2},
\end{equation}
and the differential cavity pull
\begin{equation}
    \chi = \chi_e-\chi_g,
\end{equation}
one obtains
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{disp}}^{(ge)}
    =
    (\omega_c+\bar{\chi})\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \frac{\tilde{\omega}_q}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z.
    \label{eq:two_level_disp_from_multilevel}
\end{equation}
The first term simply renormalizes the cavity frequency and can be absorbed into a redefinition of \(\omega_c\). The last term is the effective dispersive interaction used throughout the ECD protocol.

For a weakly anharmonic transmon, Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_level_disp_from_multilevel} reduces to the familiar transmon dispersive shift. Using \(g_1 \approx \sqrt{2}\,g_0\) and \(\Delta_1=\Delta_0+\alpha\), one finds
\begin{equation}
    \chi
    \approx
    2g_0^2
    \left(
        \frac{1}{\Delta_0}
        -
        \frac{1}{\Delta_0+\alpha}
    \right)
    =
    \frac{2g_0^2 \alpha}{\Delta_0(\Delta_0+\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the standard qubit--cavity dispersive Hamiltonian is not imposed by assumption, but emerges as the low-energy reduction of the full multilevel cavity--transmon system.

The classical cavity and qubit drives used for bosonic control are then added as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
    =
    \epsilon^*(t)\hat{a} + \epsilon(t)\hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right],
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon(t)\) is the cavity-drive envelope and \(\Omega(t)\) is the qubit-drive envelope. In a frame rotating at the dressed cavity and qubit frequencies, the controlled dynamics reduce to
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}
    =
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \epsilon^*(t)\hat{a}
    +
    \epsilon(t)\hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Echoed conditional displacement}

The basic idea of ECD is to use the dispersive interaction to convert an ordinary cavity drive into a qubit-state-dependent displacement. Introduce a time-dependent displacement operator
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}[\alpha(t)] = \exp\!\left[\alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger-\alpha^*(t)\hat{a}\right],
\end{equation}
and choose \(\alpha(t)\) so that the unconditional cavity drive is absorbed into the displaced frame. Using
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\hat{a}\hat{D}(\alpha) = \hat{a}+\alpha,
\end{equation}
the dispersive term transforms as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\,
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,
    \hat{D}(\alpha)
    =
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \alpha \hat{a}^\dagger
    +
    \alpha^* \hat{a}
    +
    |\alpha|^2.
\end{equation}
The displaced-frame Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{H}
    =
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}
    \left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger + \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right)\hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{\chi}{2}|\alpha(t)|^2 \hat{\sigma}_z
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        \Omega^*(t)\hat{\sigma}_-
        +
        \Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_+
    \right].
    \label{eq:ecd_displaced}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ecd_displaced} contains three distinct qubit-dependent terms. The first term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_z,
\end{equation}
is an unwanted conditional phase rotation of the cavity. The third term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}|\alpha(t)|^2 \hat{\sigma}_z,
\end{equation}
is an unwanted qubit \(Z\) rotation. The middle term,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\chi}{2}
    \left(
        \alpha(t)\hat{a}^\dagger + \alpha^*(t)\hat{a}
    \right)\hat{\sigma}_z,
    \label{eq:desired_conditional_drive}
\end{equation}
is the desired conditional linear drive on the cavity.

An echo sequence composed of two displacement segments separated by a qubit \(\pi\) pulse cancels the unwanted dispersive phase rotation and the qubit \(Z\) rotation while preserving the conditional linear drive. The resulting unitary is the echoed conditional displacement gate,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        \frac{1}{2}
        \left(
            \beta \hat{a}^{\dagger}
            -
            \beta^{*}\hat{a}
        \right)\hat{\sigma}_z
    \right],
\end{equation}
where the complex amplitude \(\beta\) is determined by the integrated drive envelope. This gate displaces the cavity in opposite directions depending on the state of the ancilla and is therefore the basic non-Gaussian entangling primitive used for bosonic control.

\subsection{Universal gate set for the joint qubit--cavity system}

The ECD gate is combined with arbitrary single-qubit rotations
\begin{equation}
    R(\theta,\phi)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        -i\frac{\theta}{2}
        \left(
            \cos\phi\,\hat{\sigma}_x
            +
            \sin\phi\,\hat{\sigma}_y
        \right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:single_qubit_rotation}
\end{equation}
A generic control sequence then has the form
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_N
    =
    R(\theta_N,\phi_N)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_N)
    \cdots
    R(\theta_2,\phi_2)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_2)\,
    R(\theta_1,\phi_1)\,
    \mathrm{ECD}(\beta_1).
    \label{eq:ecd_circuit}
\end{equation}
The pair
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}
    =
    \left\{
        \mathrm{ECD}(\beta),\,
        R(\theta,\phi)
    \right\}
\end{equation}
forms a universal gate set for the joint cavity--qubit Hilbert space, in the sense that arbitrary unitaries on a finite-dimensional truncation of the cavity together with the qubit can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy.

The physical reason is clear. The qubit rotations provide arbitrary control within the ancilla two-level system, while ECD supplies a non-Gaussian entangling operation between the ancilla and the bosonic mode. Once these two ingredients are available, repeated commutation and composition generate a sufficiently rich Lie algebra to span arbitrary operations on the truncated joint space. In contrast with the Gaussian controls of Section~\ref{sec:oscillator_hamiltonian}, the qubit-mediated ECD interaction is not restricted to affine transformations of phase space.

\subsection{Synthesis of arbitrary cavity unitaries}

To implement a target cavity unitary $\hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}$, one works in a truncated Fock space
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_d = \mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\dots,\ket{d-1}\},
\end{equation}
chosen large enough to contain the support of the states involved in the protocol. The control sequence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ecd_circuit} is then optimized so that, for every cavity state $\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_d$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_N
    \bigl(
        \ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{g}
    \bigr)
    \approx
    \bigl(
        \hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}}\ket{\psi}
    \bigr)
    \otimes
    \ket{g}.
    \label{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ecd_synthesis_condition} expresses two requirements simultaneously: the cavity undergoes the desired unitary transformation, and the qubit is returned to its initial state so that no residual entanglement remains.

In practice, the parameters
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
        \beta_k,\theta_k,\phi_k
    \right\}_{k=1}^N
\end{equation}
are found by numerical optimization on the truncated Hilbert space. The optimization minimizes a cost function based on the overlap between the synthesized unitary $\hat{U}_N$ and the target operation $\hat{U}_{\mathrm{cav}} \otimes \mathbb{I}_q$, or between the corresponding state-transfer maps when only a state-preparation task is required. Because the gate set is universal, increasing the circuit depth $N$ allows one, in principle, to approximate any desired single-cavity unitary to arbitrary accuracy on $\mathcal{H}_d$.

This synthesis strategy provides a concrete route from a small set of experimentally natural primitives to general bosonic control. Fock-state preparation, cat-state generation, logical-state encoding, and more general non-Gaussian cavity gates all arise as special cases of the same framework. The essential point is that the cavity is not controlled directly through a native cavity nonlinearity; rather, the nonlinearity is borrowed from the transmon and converted, through the dispersive interaction and echoed conditional displacements, into programmable phase-space control.

The remainder of this chapter uses this viewpoint throughout. Once the cavity--qubit system is equipped with the gate set $\{\mathrm{ECD}(\beta),R(\theta,\phi)\}$, single-mode bosonic control reduces to the problem of compiling the desired operation into an experimentally feasible sequence of echoed conditional displacements and qubit rotations.

\section{3-Wave Mixing and Beam-Splitting Interactions for Two-Mode Entanglement}
\label{sec:three_wave_mixing_two_mode}

The control framework developed so far addresses a single cavity mode. That is sufficient for arbitrary single-mode bosonic unitaries, but it is not sufficient for genuinely multimode tasks such as state transfer, nonlocal interference, and, most importantly, entanglement generation between spatially distinct cavity modes. Reaching that regime requires going beyond single-mode control and engineering a coherent interaction between two bosonic modes. In superconducting circuits, this is naturally achieved by using a flux-tunable nonlinear element as a parametric coupler.

Consider therefore two cavity modes, \(\hat{a}\) and \(\hat{b}\), coupled through a flux-tunable transmon or related Josephson nonlinear element represented by a third mode \(\hat{c}\). Under flux modulation, the Josephson nonlinearity acts as a three-wave mixer and mediates coherent exchange between the two cavity modes. The essential point is that the external pump does not merely drive the system; it selects which interaction channel becomes resonant.

\subsection{Three-wave mixing from a flux-tunable nonlinear element}

The uncoupled Hamiltonian of the three modes is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_0
    =
    \omega_a \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}
    +
    \omega_b \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{b}
    +
    \omega_c \hat{c}^\dagger \hat{c}.
\end{equation}
Because the coupler is nonlinear and flux tunable, its Josephson energy becomes time dependent,
\begin{equation}
    E_J\bigl[\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)\bigr],
    \qquad
    \Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
    =
    \Phi_{\mathrm{dc}}
    +
    \Phi_p \cos(\omega_p t + \phi_p),
\end{equation}
so that the normal modes inherit parametrically activated mixing terms. To leading order, the resulting nonlinear interaction can be written as a cubic Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{3\mathrm{wm}}
    =
    g_3
    (\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)
    (\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger)
    (\hat{c}+\hat{c}^\dagger),
    \label{eq:full_3wm}
\end{equation}
where \(g_3\) is the effective three-wave-mixing strength set by the circuit parameters and the flux bias point.

Expanding Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_3wm} generates several operator products, including terms such as
\begin{equation}
    g_3
    \left(
        \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger \hat{c}
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b}\hat{c}^\dagger
    \right),
\end{equation}
which describe coherent conversion between the two cavity modes mediated by the nonlinear mode, as well as terms such as
\begin{equation}
    g_3
    \left(
        \hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{c}^\dagger
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b}^\dagger \hat{c}
    \right),
\end{equation}
which describe pair creation and annihilation processes.

To activate a controlled interaction between \(\hat{a}\) and \(\hat{b}\), the nonlinear mode \(\hat{c}\) is driven strongly and treated in the stiff-pump approximation as a classical field,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{c}
    \;\rightarrow\;
    \xi_p e^{-i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)}
    +
    \xi_p^* e^{i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)},
\end{equation}
where \(\xi_p\) is the complex pump amplitude. Substituting this into Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_3wm} yields an effective bilinear interaction between the two cavity modes,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
    =
    G
    \bigl(
        e^{-i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)}
        +
        e^{i(\omega_p t+\phi_p)}
    \bigr)
    (\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)
    (\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger),
    \label{eq:effective_bilinear}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    G = g_3 |\xi_p|.
\end{equation}
Thus, the strong pump converts the native three-mode nonlinearity into a tunable two-mode coupling.

\subsection{Pump selection and rotating-wave approximation}

To determine which interaction is realized, move to the interaction picture with respect to \(\hat{H}_0\). Using
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}(t)=\hat{a}e^{-i\omega_a t},
    \qquad
    \hat{b}(t)=\hat{b}e^{-i\omega_b t},
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_bilinear} becomes
\begin{align}
    \hat{H}_I
    =
    G \Big[
        &
        \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
        e^{-i(\omega_a-\omega_b-\omega_p)t-i\phi_p}
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}
        e^{+i(\omega_a-\omega_b-\omega_p)t+i\phi_p}
        \nonumber\\
        &
        +
        \hat{a}\hat{b}
        e^{-i(\omega_a+\omega_b-\omega_p)t-i\phi_p}
        +
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}^\dagger
        e^{+i(\omega_a+\omega_b-\omega_p)t+i\phi_p}
    \Big]
    +
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{fast}},
    \label{eq:interaction_picture_3wm}
\end{align}
where \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{fast}}\) collects the counter-rotating terms oscillating at frequencies \(\omega_a \pm \omega_b + \omega_p\). When these fast terms oscillate much faster than the induced coupling strength \(G\), they average to zero under the rotating-wave approximation.

The interaction selected by the pump is therefore determined by which exponential in Eq.~\eqref{eq:interaction_picture_3wm} is made stationary.

\subsection{Beam-splitter interaction}

If the pump is chosen at the mode-difference frequency,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = |\omega_b-\omega_a|,
\end{equation}
the near-resonant terms are
\begin{equation}
    \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
    \qquad \text{and} \qquad
    \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b},
\end{equation}
while all others rotate rapidly and are discarded under the RWA. Assuming \(\omega_b > \omega_a\) for definiteness, so that \(\omega_p=\omega_b-\omega_a\), the effective Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{BS}}
    =
    G
    \left(
        e^{-i\phi_p}\hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
        +
        e^{i\phi_p}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{b}
    \right).
    \label{eq:beam_splitter_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This is the beam-splitter, or frequency-conversion, interaction. It conserves the total excitation number,
\begin{equation}
    \left[
        \hat{H}_{\mathrm{BS}},
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b}
    \right]
    = 0,
\end{equation}
and coherently swaps excitations between the two cavity modes. Its unitary evolution is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{U}_{\mathrm{BS}}(t)
    =
    \exp\!\left[
        -itG
        \left(
            e^{-i\phi_p}\hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger
            +
            e^{i\phi_p}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}
        \right)
    \right].
\end{equation}
Physically, Eq.~\eqref{eq:beam_splitter_hamiltonian} hybridizes the two modes exactly as an optical beam splitter mixes two traveling fields. It is therefore the natural interaction for state transfer, interference, and mode conversion.

By itself, however, the beam-splitter interaction does not create entanglement from the two-mode vacuum, since
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\mathrm{BS}}\ket{0,0}=0.
\end{equation}
Its role in entanglement generation is instead indirect but essential: when one mode is first prepared in a nonclassical state, or when the beam-splitter is combined with single-mode bosonic control from Section~\ref{sec:ecd_single_cavity}, the mixing operation converts local nonclassicality into nonlocal correlations between the two cavities.


\subsection{Control perspective and relevance for the rest of the chapter}

The key advantage of three-wave mixing is that the same nonlinear hardware element can realize different effective interactions simply by changing the pump frequency. A pump at the difference frequency activates the beam-splitter channel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:beam_splitter_hamiltonian}; a pump at the sum frequency activates the two-mode-squeezing channel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tms_hamiltonian}. The pump amplitude sets the interaction strength \(G\), while the pump phase \(\phi_p\) sets the interaction phase. In this way, the flux-tunable transmon functions as a programmable nonlinear mixer for bosonic modes.

This flexibility is precisely what is needed for multimode bosonic control. The beam-splitter interaction enables coherent transfer and interference between cavities, while the two-mode-squeezing interaction provides a direct entangling resource. Together with the single-mode cavity control developed earlier, these parametrically activated interactions furnish the basic toolbox for preparing and manipulating two-mode entangled bosonic states in circuit QED.

\chapter{Superconducting Qubits: Coherence and Control}
\label{chap:sc-qubits}

This chapter introduces basic superconducting circuits, decoherence, and control techniques. The discussion in Sec.~\ref{sec:sc-qubits} is organized around three hardware paradigms: transmons, fluxoniums, and bosonic qubits. A detailed discussion of decoherence mechanisms and coherence metrics is deferred to Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}. Section~\ref{sec:analyticalcontrol} then presents the analytical and numerical techniques used to control these systems.

\section{Superconducting Qubits}
\label{sec:sc-qubits}

All superconducting qubits rely on Josephson junctions, which render the circuit spectrum anharmonic. This anharmonicity allows individual transitions to be spectrally resolved and the two lowest levels to be used as an effective qubit. Qubit design must achieve two main goals: sufficient anharmonicity to suppress leakage into noncomputational states, and reduced sensitivity to parameter fluctuations to improve robustness against noise. Different qubit modalities achieve these goals through their circuit topology, parameter regime, and operating point. The following subsections introduce two widely used nonlinear platforms, the transmon and the fluxonium. We then discuss a complementary platform, bosonic qubits, which encode quantum information in linear cavities.

\subsection{The Transmon}
\label{sec:transmon}

The transmon is a superconducting circuit composed of a Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitance, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:transmon}(a). The Josephson junction provides the nonlinearity that renders the spectrum anharmonic, while the capacitive shunt reduces sensitivity to offset charge. These features can be seen by analyzing the circuit Hamiltonian.

\subsubsection{Transmon Hamiltonian}

A single Josephson junction shunted by a capacitor [Fig.~\ref{fig:transmon}(a)] is described by the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C (n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos \varphi,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma},
    \qquad
    E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(C_\Sigma\) is the total capacitance of the circuit, including the junction capacitance, any added shunt capacitance, and parasitic capacitances; \(\Phi_0 = h/2e\) is the flux quantum; and \(I_c\) is the critical current. Unless otherwise stated, this thesis uses units in which \(\hbar = 1\). 

The charge degree of freedom is described by the operator \(n\), whose integer eigenvalues represent the net number of Cooper pairs on the island. The parameter \(n_g\) is an offset charge, in units of the Cooper-pair charge, arising from capacitive coupling to external voltages as well as from background charges in the device environment. The charging term \(4E_C (n-n_g)^2\) therefore gives the electrostatic energy of the island and determines the energy cost of changing its charge state.

The phase degree of freedom is described by the operators \(e^{\pm i\varphi}\), which transfer a single Cooper pair across the junction and thus shift the charge by one:
\begin{equation}
    [n, e^{\pm i\varphi}] = \pm e^{\pm i\varphi},
\end{equation}
or equivalently,
\begin{equation}
    e^{\pm i\varphi}\ket{n} = \ket{n \pm 1}.
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the Josephson term
\begin{equation}
    -E_J \cos\varphi
    = -\frac{E_J}{2}\left(e^{i\varphi}+e^{-i\varphi}\right) = -E_J\big(|n\rangle\langle n+1| + \text{h.c.}\big)
\end{equation}
represents coherent Cooper-pair tunneling across the barrier. In contrast to the quadratic potential of a linear inductor, this contribution is periodic in \(\varphi\). The resulting cosine potential is nonlinear and gives rise to the anharmonicity required for qubit operation.

\subsubsection{Transmon limit and weak anharmonicity}

The transmon regime is defined by
\begin{equation}
    E_J \gg E_C .
\end{equation}
In this limit, the low-lying states are localized near \(\varphi \approx 0\), a minimum of the Josephson potential. The spectrum is therefore determined mainly by the local shape of the cosine potential, which can be expanded about this minimum as
\begin{equation}
    -E_J\cos\varphi
    \approx
    -E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6).
\end{equation}
The low-energy states and eigenvalues are then described by the approximate Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{app}}
    =
    4E_C(n-n_g)^2
    - E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6),
\end{equation}
with the boundary condition
\begin{equation}
    \psi(\varphi)\to 0
    \qquad \text{as } |\varphi|\to\infty .
\end{equation}

With this boundary condition, the offset charge can be eliminated by the unitary transformation
\begin{equation}
    U(n_g)=e^{-in_g\varphi},
\end{equation}
which transforms the operators as
\begin{equation}
    U n U^\dagger = n+n_g,
    \qquad
    U\varphi U^\dagger=\varphi,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    U H_{\mathrm{app}} U^\dagger
    =
    4E_C n^2
    - E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6).
\end{equation}
The low-energy spectrum is therefore approximately independent of \(n_g\).

The validity of this approximation is controlled by the ratio \(E_C/E_J\). When \(E_C/E_J\) is small, the charge dispersion is exponentially suppressed \cite{Koch2007}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:transmon}(c), increasing \(E_J/E_C\) from 5 to 50 makes the energy levels nearly independent of \(n_g\). In the transmon limit \(E_J/E_C \gg 1\), one may therefore set \(n_g=0\) at leading order and obtain
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{app}}
    \approx
    4E_C n^2
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    - E_J .
\end{equation}

The quadratic terms define a harmonic oscillator with frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \sqrt{8E_JE_C}.
\end{equation}
To express the Hamiltonian in the oscillator basis, we introduce the ladder operators
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(b+b^\dagger),
    \qquad
    n = i\,n_{\mathrm{zpf}}(b^\dagger-b),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\left(\frac{2E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/4},
    \qquad
    n_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\left(\frac{E_J}{32E_C}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
The harmonic part becomes
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{harm}}
    =
    \omega_p\left(b^\dagger b+\frac12\right)-E_J.
\end{equation}
The quartic term gives the leading deviation from equal level spacing and yields the anharmonicity
\begin{equation}
    \alpha \approx -E_C,
\end{equation}
and the lowest transition frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{01}\approx \sqrt{8E_JE_C}-E_C.
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|\alpha|}{\omega_{01}}
    \sim
    \left(\frac{E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/2}\ll 1,
\end{equation}
the transmon is weakly anharmonic.

\subsubsection{Flux-tunable transmon}
\label{FTT}
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{chapter2/FTT.pdf}
    \caption[Flux-tunable transmon.]{Flux-tunable transmon. (a)~Lumped-element circuit: a SQUID-based transmon in which two Josephson junctions with energies \(E_{J1}\) and \(E_{J2}\) are shunted by a capacitance with charging energy \(E_C\), with the loop threaded by an external flux \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\). (b)~Flux dependence of the transmon transition frequency \(\omega_b/2\pi\). The parameters are \(E_{J1}/h = 12.0~\mathrm{GHz}\), \(E_{J2}/h = 8.0~\mathrm{GHz}\), and \(E_C/h = 0.25~\mathrm{GHz}\). The frequency is maximal at integer values of \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}/\Phi_0\) and remains finite at half-integer values due to the junction asymmetry.}
    \label{fig:flux_tunable_transmon}
\end{figure}

Replacing the single Josephson junction with a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) makes the effective Josephson energy tunable by an external magnetic flux. A SQUID consists of two Josephson junctions connected in parallel to form a superconducting loop, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:flux_tunable_transmon}. The applied flux \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) sets the phase difference between the two junctions, allowing the SQUID to be described as an effective single junction with a flux-dependent Josephson energy.

For a SQUID consisting of two Josephson junctions with Josephson energies \(E_{J1}\) and \(E_{J2}\), the Josephson potential is
\begin{equation}
    U_J = -E_{J1}\cos\varphi_1 - E_{J2}\cos\varphi_2,
\end{equation}
where \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) are the phase differences across the two junctions. In the limit of negligible loop inductance, the phases satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_1 - \varphi_2 = 2\pi \frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}.
\end{equation}
Introducing the average phase
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \frac{\varphi_1+\varphi_2}{2},
\end{equation}
the Josephson potential becomes
\begin{equation}
    U_J
    =
    -E_{J1}\cos\!\left(\varphi+\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    -E_{J2}\cos\!\left(\varphi-\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right).
\end{equation}
This can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    U_J = -E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})
    =
    E_{J\Sigma}
    \sqrt{
        \cos^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
        +
        d^2 \sin^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    },
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    E_{J\Sigma}=E_{J1}+E_{J2},
    \qquad
    d=\frac{E_{J1}-E_{J2}}{E_{J1}+E_{J2}},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_0 = \arctan\!\left( -d\tan\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right) \right).
\end{equation}
The parameter \(d\) quantifies the junction asymmetry. For a symmetric SQUID (\(d=0\)), the tunability is maximal, and \(E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})\) vanishes at \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}=\Phi_0/2\). For \(d\neq 0\), the effective Josephson energy remains finite at \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}=\Phi_0/2\), which reduces the tuning range and leaves the qubit with a nonzero minimum frequency. The transmon transition frequency therefore becomes flux dependent:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_b(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_C\,E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})} - E_C.
\end{equation}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:flux_tunable_transmon}(b), \(\omega_b\) is periodic in \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) with period \(\Phi_0\). It reaches its maximum at integer values of \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}/\Phi_0\) and decreases toward half-integer values, where the reduction in \(E_J\) lowers the qubit frequency.

\endgroup

\subsection{The Fluxonium}
\label{sec:fluxonium}
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{chapter2/fluxonium.pdf}
    \caption[Fluxonium qubit.]{Fluxonium qubit.  (a)~Lumped-element circuit of a fluxonium qubit: a Josephson junction with Josephson energy $E_J$, shunted by a capacitance characterized by charging energy $E_C$ and a superinductance characterized by inductive energy $E_L$, with the loop threaded by an external flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}$. (b)~Energy spectrum of the three lowest energy levels as a function of $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}/\Phi_0$. (c,d)~Potential energy landscape (gray) and probability densities of the ground state (green) and first excited state (orange) at $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0.4\,\Phi_0$ (c) and at the half-flux sweet spot, $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0.5\,\Phi_0$ (d). Parameters: $E_J/2\pi = 5.0\;\text{GHz}$, $E_C/2\pi = 1.0\;\text{GHz}$, and $E_L/2\pi = 1.0\;\text{GHz}$}
    \label{fig:fluxonium}
\end{figure}

The transmon suppresses charge sensitivity by operating in the regime \(E_C/E_J \ll 1\), but this same limit leaves the spectrum only weakly anharmonic. The fluxonium~\cite{Manucharyan2009} addresses this trade-off by modifying the circuit topology: it incorporates a large linear inductance. This modification opens access to a qualitatively different regime of superconducting qubit design.

The Hamiltonian of the fluxonium circuit, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(a), is
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C (n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos\varphi + \frac{1}{2} E_L (\varphi + \phi_e)^2,
    \label{eq:fluxonium_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where \(E_C\) is the charging energy, \(E_J\) is the Josephson energy, and
\begin{equation}
    E_L = \left(\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\right)^2 \frac{1}{L}
\end{equation}
is the inductive energy associated with the linear inductance \(L\). The reduced external flux
\begin{equation}
    \phi_e = \frac{2\pi \Phi_e}{\Phi_0}
\end{equation}
biases the loop formed by the junction and the inductor. 

The inductive shunt changes the circuit boundary conditions. In a Cooper-pair box or transmon, the two superconducting islands are connected only through the junction, so charge is transferred in discrete units and the phase is a periodic variable. In fluxonium, the superinductor galvanically connects the two sides of the junction and removes this isolated-island structure. As a result, \(\varphi\) can be treated as an ordinary unbounded operator, and \(n\) as its continuous conjugate variable, satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\varphi,n] = i.
\end{equation}
This is one of the defining advantages of fluxonium compared to the transmon: fluxonium is intrinsically insensitive to offset charge without requiring the large-\(E_J/E_C\) limit.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fluxonium_hamiltonian} contains three energy scales---\(E_C\), \(E_J\), and \(E_L\)---and the external flux \(\phi_e\). Different ratios of these parameters give rise to qualitatively distinct regimes, all described by the same Hamiltonian but exhibiting different spectra and operating characteristics. These include heavy fluxonium \cite{Earnest2018,Zhang2021,Ficheux2021}, standard fluxonium \cite{Nguyen2019,Somoroff2023,Bao2022,Ding2023}, the inductively shunted transmon \cite{Hassani2023,Zobrist2026}, and integer fluxonium \cite{Mencia2024}. All of these qubits correspond to different parameter regimes of a common circuit model.

For concreteness, we focus on the standard fluxonium. Away from half flux, the double-well potential is tilted, and the two lowest states are localized predominantly in one of the two wells [Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(c)]. The matrix elements \(\langle g|\varphi|e\rangle\) and \(\langle g|n|e\rangle\) are strongly suppressed because the two states have little spatial overlap.

At half flux, the potential
\begin{equation}
    V(\varphi)= -E_J \cos\varphi + \frac{1}{2} E_L (\varphi + \pi)^2
\end{equation}
becomes a symmetric double-well potential [Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(d)]. The two lowest eigenstates then form a tunnel-split doublet, so the \(\ket{g}\leftrightarrow\ket{e}\) transition is governed by interwell tunneling. At this bias point, the transition frequency is first-order insensitive to flux,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \omega_{ge}}{\partial \phi_e}=0,
\end{equation}
while the anharmonicity remains large, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(b). This bias point is called a sweet spot and suppresses dephasing due to flux noise, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:sweet-spots}. 

The combination of protection against charge noise and strong spectral anharmonicity is a defining feature of fluxonium.

\subsection{Bosonic Qubits}
\label{sec:bosonic_qubits}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{chapter2/qec.pdf}
    \caption[Bosonic quantum error correction.]{Bosonic quantum error correction. (a)~Dual-rail encoding: the logical subspace is spanned by $|1,0\rangle$ and $|0,1\rangle$ across two bosonic modes. A single-photon loss maps both logical states to the vacuum state $|0,0\rangle$, a leakage state outside the code space, making the error detectable. (b)~Binomial code: the code space (left, blue) is spanned by logical states built from even-photon-number Fock states; their Wigner functions are shown at the vertices of a Bloch-sphere representation. A single-photon loss maps the code space into a distinguishable error space (right, purple) that retains the logical amplitudes. Parity monitoring detects the error, followed by a recovery operation. Panels (a) and (b) are adapted from Refs.~\cite{teoh2023dual} and \cite{Ni2023}, respectively.}
    \label{fig:bosonic_qec}
\end{figure}

Transmon and fluxonium qubits encode quantum information in the lowest two levels of an anharmonic circuit. Bosonic qubits follow a different strategy: they use the oscillator Hilbert space to encode a logical qubit with redundancy, so that errors are detectable and, in some encodings, correctable. The need for such redundancy is clear from a comparison with a two-level qubit. If a qubit is measured in $|0\rangle$, the measurement outcome alone does not reveal whether the computation actually produced $|0\rangle$ or whether the qubit was in $|1\rangle$ and relaxed before readout. The error acts within the computational subspace and leaves no direct flag.

The simplest bosonic example is the dual-rail code [Fig.~\ref{fig:bosonic_qec}(a)],
\begin{equation}
    |0_L\rangle = |1,0\rangle, \qquad |1_L\rangle = |0,1\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the logical qubit is stored across two bosonic modes. A single-photon loss maps both logical basis states to the vacuum state $|0,0\rangle$, which lies outside the code space. Photon loss therefore appears as a detectable leakage event: monitoring the total excitation number reveals that an error has occurred. The dual-rail code illustrates the essential principle of bosonic error detection: redundancy makes photon loss visible rather than leaving it hidden within the logical subspace.

To achieve correction rather than mere detection, the encoding must preserve the logical amplitudes after the error. A simple example is the binomial code [Fig.~\ref{fig:bosonic_qec}(b)],
\begin{equation}
    |0_L\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |4\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad |1_L\rangle = |2\rangle.
\end{equation}
These logical states occupy only even-photon-number Fock states. A single-photon loss maps the code space into the odd-parity sector,
\begin{equation}
    a|0_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\,|3\rangle, \qquad a|1_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\,|1\rangle,
\end{equation}
so the loss event is identified by a change in photon-number parity. Unlike in the dual-rail code, the error states remain distinct and retain the encoded amplitudes. For an encoded state $|\psi_L\rangle = \alpha|0_L\rangle + \beta|1_L\rangle$, photon loss induces
\begin{equation}
    a|\psi_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\left(\alpha|3\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, the coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are preserved. Once the error is detected, a recovery operation can restore the state $|\psi_L\rangle$. The binomial code therefore illustrates how a bosonic encoding can extend the qubit lifetime.

Other common bosonic encodings include cat codes~\cite{Mirrahimi2014,Grimm2020}, which use superpositions of coherent states, and Gottesman--Kitaev--Preskill (GKP) states~\cite{Gottesman2001,CampagneIbarcq2020}, which encode information in a grid-like structure in phase space. 

In the absence of nonlinearity, a linearly driven cavity can only produce coherent states. Preparing the non-Gaussian states needed for bosonic encodings, such as cat states and GKP states, therefore requires coupling the cavity to a nonlinear ancilla or coupler, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:analyticalcontrol}.

\section{Decoherence in Superconducting Qubits}
\label{sec:decoherence}

Superconducting qubits undergo decoherence due to unavoidable coupling to the environment. This coupling has two main effects: first, it induces transitions between qubit states, including relaxation \(\ket{1}\rightarrow\ket{0}\) and thermal excitation \(\ket{0}\rightarrow\ket{1}\); second, it causes fluctuations in the qubit frequency, leading to random phase accumulation and hence pure dephasing. This section introduces a basic description of decoherence, defines the relevant coherence metrics, discusses representative noise channels, and explains how sweet spots suppress dephasing by reducing first-order sensitivity to noise.

\subsection{Relaxation and Pure Dephasing: \texorpdfstring{$T_1$ and $T_{\phi}$}{T1 and Tphi}}
\label{subsec:t1-tphi}
To model the effect of environmental coupling, we allow a control parameter in the Hamiltonian to fluctuate in time,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(t) = \lambda_0 + \delta\lambda(t),
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_0\) is the operating point and \(\delta\lambda(t)\) denotes the fluctuation induced by noise. Expanding the Hamiltonian around \(\lambda_0\) gives
\begin{equation}
    H(t) \approx H_0 + B_\lambda\,\delta\lambda(t) + \mathcal{O}\big((\delta\lambda)^2\big),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    H_0 = H(\lambda_0),
    \qquad
    B_\lambda = \left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial \lambda}\right|_{\lambda_0}.
\end{equation}
The noise is characterized by the spectral density
\begin{equation}
    S_\lambda(\omega)
    =
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dt\, e^{i\omega t}
    \langle \delta\lambda(t)\delta\lambda(0)\rangle,
\end{equation}
which is the Fourier transform of the noise correlation function. This fluctuating coupling affects both populations and phases and therefore causes both depolarization and pure dephasing.

\noindent\textbf{Depolarization and \(T_1\).}
Depolarization corresponds to transitions between the qubit states and includes both relaxation and thermal excitation. To leading order in the noisy coupling, the corresponding rates are \cite{RevModPhys.82.1155}
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\downarrow}
    =
    \left|\langle 0|B_\lambda|1\rangle\right|^2
    S_\lambda(\omega_q),
    \qquad
    \Gamma_{\uparrow}
    =
    \left|\langle 0|B_\lambda|1\rangle\right|^2
    S_\lambda(-\omega_q),
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_q\) is the qubit frequency. For classical noise, \(S_\lambda(\omega)=S_\lambda(-\omega)\), so the two rates are equal. For a quantized environment, however, the positive- and negative-frequency components generally differ, and in thermal equilibrium the rates satisfy the detailed-balance relation \cite{breuer2002quantummaster}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Gamma_{\uparrow}}{\Gamma_{\downarrow}}
    =
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{\omega_q}{k_B T}\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, when \(k_B T \ll \omega_q\), thermal excitation is negligible and relaxation dominates. The depolarization time \(T_1\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{T_1} = \Gamma_{\downarrow} + \Gamma_{\uparrow}.
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Pure dephasing and \(T_\phi\).}
Pure dephasing arises from fluctuations of the qubit frequency itself. For the same noisy coupling, the pure-dephasing rate in the Markovian limit is \cite{RevModPhys.82.1155}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pure-dephasing}
    \Gamma_{\phi}
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left(
    \langle 0|B_\lambda|0\rangle
    -
    \langle 1|B_\lambda|1\rangle
    \right)^2
    S_\lambda(0)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left(
    \frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \lambda}
    \right)^2
    S_\lambda(0),
\end{equation}
where the second equality follows from \(B_\lambda = \partial H/\partial \lambda\). The pure-dephasing time is
\begin{equation}
    T_\phi = \frac{1}{\Gamma_\phi}.
\end{equation}
In superconducting qubits, however, the \(1/f\) flux-noise spectrum diverges near zero frequency. The resulting pure dephasing must therefore be treated more carefully than in the simple Markovian limit of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pure-dephasing} \cite{groszkowski2018coherence}, as discussed in Sec.\ \ref{subsec:noise-channels}.

Although \(T_\phi\) describes pure dephasing, experiments usually measure the decay of the total transverse coherence. Preparing the qubit in
\[
|+\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},
\]
and monitoring \(\langle \sigma_x\rangle\) defines the coherence time \(T_2\), which contains contributions from both energy relaxation and pure dephasing:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{T_2}=\frac{1}{2T_1}+\frac{1}{T_\phi}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Examples of Important Noise Channels}
\label{subsec:noise-channels}

Decoherence is determined by two ingredients: the system operator through which the environment couples to the circuit and the spectral density of the corresponding fluctuations. We now discuss several important noise channels in superconducting circuits.

\paragraph{Dielectric loss.}
Dielectric loss arises from dielectric materials that contribute to the circuit capacitance, such as the substrate, native oxides, and material interfaces \cite{martinis2005decoherence}. It couples to the charge degree of freedom, so the system operator is
\[
B_\lambda = 2e\,n.
\]
A common model for the corresponding noise spectral density is given by \cite{Smith2020,Nguyen2019}
\[
S(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{|\omega|\,C_J\,Q_{\rm cap}(\omega)}
\left(1+\coth\frac{|\omega|}{2k_B T}\right),
\]
where \(C_J\) is the relevant capacitance and \(Q_{\rm cap}(\omega)\) is the dielectric quality factor. This noise causes relaxation and, at finite temperature, thermal excitation. Dielectric loss is often a major contributor to \(T_1\) in superconducting qubits \cite{Murray2021dielec}.

\paragraph{Inductive loss.}
Inductive loss arises from dissipation associated with an inductance or superinductance. It couples to the flux degree of freedom, so the system operator is
\[
B_\lambda=\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\,\phi.
\]
A common model for the corresponding spectral density is given by \cite{Smith2020,Nguyen2019}
\[
S(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{|\omega|\,L_J\,Q_{\rm ind}(\omega)}
\left(1+\coth\frac{|\omega|}{2k_B T}\right),
\]
where \(L_J\) is the relevant inductance and \(Q_{\rm ind}(\omega)\) is the inductive quality factor. Like dielectric loss, this noise causes depolarization. In fluxonium, for example, superinductor loss can limit \(T_1\) \cite{Hazard2019inductor}.

\paragraph{\texorpdfstring{$1/f$}{1/f} flux noise.}
A major dephasing channel in superconducting circuits is low-frequency \(1/f\) noise. If a circuit parameter \(\lambda\) fluctuates slowly, the qubit frequency \(\omega_q\) also fluctuates. A superposition state then accumulates a random relative phase. For \(1/f\) noise, the spectral density is commonly written as
\[
S_{\lambda}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi A_{\lambda}^2}{|\omega|},
\]
where \(A_{\lambda}\) sets the noise amplitude. Because this spectrum is concentrated at low frequencies, its main effect is usually pure dephasing rather than energy relaxation.

Flux noise has a \(1/f\) spectrum, with the external flux \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) as the fluctuating parameter. This noise is especially relevant in flux-tunable circuits because fluctuations in \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) directly modulate the qubit frequency, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:sc-qubits}. The corresponding system operator is
\[
B_\lambda=\frac{\partial H}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}.
\]
The spectral density is
\[
S_{\Phi}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi A_{\Phi}^2}{|\omega|}.
\]
Away from bias points at which the qubit is first-order insensitive to flux, the dephasing time is approximately given by \cite{groszkowski2018coherence}
\[
\frac{1}{T_{\phi}}
=
\sqrt{2}\,A_{\Phi}
\left|\frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right|
\sqrt{\bigl|\ln(\omega_{\rm low} t_{\rm exp})\bigr|},
\]
where \(\omega_{\rm low}\) is a low-frequency cutoff and \(t_{\rm exp}\) is the experimental timescale. The key factor is the flux sensitivity \(\partial \omega_q/\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\): stronger flux dependence gives faster dephasing.
\subsection{Improving Dephasing Time via Sweet Spots: First-Order Noise Insensitivity}
\label{subsec:sweet-spots}

The above result shows that dephasing from \(1/f\) flux noise is set by the flux sensitivity \(\partial \omega_q/\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\). This suggests a simple strategy: bias the circuit at an operating point where the qubit frequency is first-order insensitive to the external flux,
\[
\frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}=0.
\]
Such points are called sweet spots. At a sweet spot, the linear response to flux noise vanishes. The leading contribution to dephasing is then shifted to higher order.

\paragraph{Floquet states in driven systems.}\label{sec:floquet}
The same idea applies to a periodically driven qubit \cite{Huang2021DynamicalSweetSpots,Valery2022DynamicalSweetSpot}. When
\[
H(t+T)=H(t),
\]
the natural basis states are Floquet states,
\[
|\psi_\alpha(t)\rangle
=
e^{-i\epsilon_\alpha t}
|\phi_\alpha(t)\rangle,
\qquad
|\phi_\alpha(t+T)\rangle = |\phi_\alpha(t)\rangle,
\]
where \(\epsilon_\alpha\) is the quasienergy and \(|\phi_\alpha(t)\rangle\) is a time-periodic Floquet mode. In the driven system, these dressed states play the role of the computational states. The relevant dressed qubit frequency is therefore
\[
\omega_q^{(\mathrm{F})}=\epsilon_1-\epsilon_0.
\]

If the external flux fluctuates slowly, it modulates this dressed qubit frequency and thereby causes dephasing between the two Floquet states. A dynamical sweet spot is reached when
\[
\frac{\partial \omega_q^{(\mathrm{F})}}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}=0.
\]
At this point, the drive renders the Floquet qubit frequency first-order insensitive to flux noise and suppresses the leading-order contribution of \(1/f\) flux noise to pure dephasing.

\section{Control of Superconducting Qubits}
\label{sec:analyticalcontrol}

Fault-tolerant quantum computers require high-fidelity operations. To achieve this goal, we must engineer drive parameters that implement target gates with minimal errors. We begin with two examples in which the control parameters can be optimized analytically, and then turn to numerical optimization when analytical approaches are no longer sufficient.
\section*{DRAG}
In the rotating frame of the drive and within the rotating-wave approximation, the transmon Hamiltonian truncated to the lowest three levels is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:H_drag_start}
H(t)=
\frac{\Omega_x(t)}{2}\bigl(\sigma_x^{01}+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_x^{12}\bigr)
+
\frac{\Omega_y(t)}{2}\bigl(\sigma_y^{01}+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_y^{12}\bigr)
+
\delta_1(t)\Pi_1
+
\bigl(\alpha+2\delta_1(t)\bigr)\Pi_2,
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega_x(t)\) and \(\Omega_y(t)\) are the two quadrature envelopes of the microwave drive, \(\delta_1(t)\) is the detuning of the \(\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}\) transition from the drive frequency, \(\alpha=\omega_{12}-\omega_{01}\) is the anharmonicity, and \(\Pi_j=\ket{j}\bra{j}\). We assume the drive is sufficiently weak that levels above \(\ket{2}\) remain negligibly populated.

To isolate the dominant leakage channel, we first set \(\Omega_y=\delta_1=0\) and focus on the off-resonant \(\{\ket{1},\ket{2}\}\) subspace. Up to an irrelevant identity term, its Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:H12_drag}
H_{12}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{2}\,\Omega_x(t)\sigma_x^{12}-\alpha \sigma_z^{12}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_z^{12}=\ket{1}\bra{1}-\ket{2}\bra{2}\). Defining the instantaneous mixing angle by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:theta_drag}
\tan\theta(t)=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\Omega_x(t)}{\alpha},
\end{equation}
the corresponding counter-diabatic correction~\cite{Berry2009,GueryOdelin2019} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hcd_drag}
H_{\mathrm{cd}}(t)
=
-\frac{\dot{\theta}(t)}{2}\sigma_y^{12}
=
-\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\alpha\,\dot{\Omega}_x(t)}
{2\left[\alpha^2+2\Omega_x^2(t)\right]}\sigma_y^{12}.
\end{equation}
In the weak-drive limit \(\abs{\Omega_x}\ll \abs{\alpha}\), this reduces to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hcd_drag_weak}
H_{\mathrm{cd}}(t)\approx -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\alpha}\dot{\Omega}_x(t)\sigma_y^{12}.
\end{equation}
To realize this Hamiltonian, we can use \(y\)-quadrature control
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag_y_only}
\Omega_y(t)=-\frac{\dot{\Omega}_x(t)}{\alpha},
\end{equation}
which is the leading-order DRAG condition.
Thus, a derivative-shaped \(y\)-quadrature suppresses leakage through the off-resonant \(\ket{1}\leftrightarrow\ket{2}\) transition.

This condition alone does not guarantee resonant Rabi oscillation between \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\), because the same \(y\)-quadrature also acts within the computational subspace. To analyze dynamics in the qubit subspace, we decouple the subspace from \(\ket{2}\) perturbatively using a second-order Schrieffer--Wolff transformation. We define the subspace projector and complex drive
\[
P=\Pi_0+\Pi_1,
\qquad
\Omega=\Omega_x+i\Omega_y.
\]
Then we rewrite the Hamiltonian as
\[
H=H_{\mathrm{bd}}+V_{\mathrm{od}},
\]
with
\[
H_{\mathrm{bd}}
=
\frac{\Omega_x}{2}\sigma_x^{01}
+
\frac{\Omega_y}{2}\sigma_y^{01}
+
\delta_1\Pi_1
+
(\alpha+2\delta_1)\Pi_2,
\]
and
\[
V_{\mathrm{od}}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\left(
\Omega^* \ket{1}\bra{2}
+
\Omega \ket{2}\bra{1}
\right),
\]
the first-order generator \(S^{(1)}\) is chosen to satisfy
\[
[S^{(1)},H_0]=-V_{\mathrm{od}},
\qquad
H_0=\delta_1\Pi_1+(\alpha+2\delta_1)\Pi_2.
\]
This gives
\[
S^{(1)}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(\alpha+\delta_1)}
\left(
\Omega \ket{2}\bra{1}
-
\Omega^* \ket{1}\bra{2}
\right)
\approx
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\alpha}
\left(
\Omega \ket{2}\bra{1}
-
\Omega^* \ket{1}\bra{2}
\right),
\]
where the last step keeps only the leading term in \(\delta_1/\alpha\). Projecting the block-diagonalized Hamiltonian onto the computational subspace yields
\[
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(01)}
=
P\left(H_{\mathrm{bd}}+\frac12[S^{(1)},V_{\mathrm{od}}]\right)P
\approx
\frac{\Omega_x}{2}\sigma_x^{01}
+
\frac{\Omega_y}{2}\sigma_y^{01}
+
\left(
\delta_1-\frac{|\Omega|^2}{2\alpha}
\right)\Pi_1.
\]

Rewriting the effective Hamiltonian in terms of Pauli operators, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hq_drag}
H_q
=
\frac{\Omega_x}{2}\sigma_x^{01}
+
\frac{\Omega_y}{2}\sigma_y^{01}
-
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\delta_1-\frac{\Omega_x^2}{2\alpha}
\right)\sigma_z^{01}.
\end{equation}
The \(y\)-quadrature control also contributes an effective detuning. To see this, we perform a second near-identity transformation within the qubit subspace,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Uq_drag}
U(t)=e^{-iS(t)},
\qquad
S(t)=\frac{\Omega_x(t)}{2\alpha}\sigma_y^{01}.
\end{equation}
To leading order in \(\Omega_x/\alpha\), the transformed Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hqprime_expand_drag}
H_q'
=
U H_q U^\dagger + i \dot U U^\dagger
\approx
H_q-i[S,H_q]+\dot S.
\end{equation}
Keeping terms to the same order gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Hqprime_drag}
H_q'
\approx
\frac{\Omega_x}{2}\sigma_x^{01}
+
\frac{1}{2}\left(\Omega_y+\frac{\dot\Omega_x}{\alpha}\right)\sigma_y^{01}
-
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\delta_1+\frac{\Omega_x^2}{2\alpha}
\right)\sigma_z^{01},
\end{equation}
up to an irrelevant overall phase and higher-order corrections.

The residual \(y\)-quadrature control vanishes under the leakage-suppression condition. Equation~\eqref{eq:Hqprime_drag} shows that, in this frame, a pure \(x\)-rotation requires the effective detuning to vanish. This gives the standard leading-order detuning condition,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag_final}
\delta_1(t)=-\frac{\Omega_x^2(t)}{2\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Under these conditions, the transformed qubit Hamiltonian reduces to
\[
H_q'\approx \frac{\Omega_x(t)}{2}\sigma_x^{01}.
\]
The conditions in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:drag_y_only} and~\eqref{eq:drag_final} ensure the intended \(\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}\) rotation while suppressing leakage to \(\ket{2}\).

\section*{Parametric drive }Parametric driving can selectively activate interactions between modes and thereby enable two-qubit gates. As an illustration, we consider an FTT capacitively coupled to two cavities, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:twomode}. The Hamiltonian can be written as~\cite{Petrescu2023}
\begin{equation}
    H = H^{(0)} + H_{\rm nl} + H^{(1)}(t),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
    H^{(0)}
    &=
    \omega_a a^\dagger a
    + \omega_b b^\dagger b
    + \omega_c c^\dagger c
    + g_{ab}(a^\dagger b + a b^\dagger)
    + g_{bc}(c^\dagger b + c b^\dagger), \\
    H_{\rm nl}
    &=
    -\frac{K_b}{2} b^{\dagger 2} b^2, \\
    H^{(1)}(t)
    &=
    \delta\omega_b \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)\, b^\dagger b.
\end{align}
Here \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are the annihilation operators of the three bare modes, with bare frequencies \(\omega_a\), \(\omega_b\), and \(\omega_c\), respectively, and \(g_{ab}\) and \(g_{bc}\) are the static capacitive coupling strengths. The nonlinear term \(H_{\rm nl}\) describes the anharmonicity \(K_b\) of the transmon mode \(b\). As discussed in Sec.~\ref{FTT}, the transmon frequency depends on the external flux, so \(H^{(1)}(t)\) represents the parametric flux drive, where \(\delta\omega_b\) is the amplitude of the induced frequency modulation and \(\omega_d\) and \(\phi_d\) are the drive frequency and phase, respectively.

Diagonalizing the linear part \(H^{(0)}\) gives
\begin{equation}
    H^{(0)}
    =
    \sum_{\nu=a,b,c} \tilde{\omega}_\nu d_\nu^\dagger d_\nu,
    \label{eq:H0_diag_compact}
\end{equation}
where \(d_\nu\) are the dressed normal-mode operators. The dressed frequencies \(\tilde{\omega}_\nu\) and the mode compositions depend on the bare frequencies \(\omega_{a,b,c}\) and the static couplings \(g_{ab}\) and \(g_{bc}\). In the dispersive regime, two dressed modes remain predominantly cavity-like, and one remains predominantly transmon-like.

Since we are interested in the beam-splitting interactions, we skip rewriting the Kerr terms generated by \(H_{\rm nl}\) explicitly. Expressing the bare transmon operator as
\begin{equation}
    b = \sum_{\nu=a,b,c} u_{b\nu} d_\nu,
\end{equation}
the modulation term becomes
\begin{equation}
    H^{(1)}(t)
    =
    \delta\omega_b \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)
    \sum_{\mu,\nu=a,b,c}
    \eta_{\mu\nu} d_\mu^\dagger d_\nu,
    \qquad
    \eta_{\mu\nu}=u_{b\mu}^*u_{b\nu},
    \label{eq:H1_normalmode_compact}
\end{equation}
which contains both diagonal frequency shifts and off-diagonal mode-conversion terms. For the two cavity-like dressed modes, the beam-splitting term is
\begin{equation}
    H_{ac}(t)
    =
    \delta\omega_b \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)
    \left(
        \eta_{ac} d_a^\dagger d_c
        +
        \eta_{ac}^* d_a d_c^\dagger
    \right),
    \qquad
    \eta_{ac}=u_{ba}^*u_{bc}.
\end{equation}

Moving to the interaction picture with respect to \eqref{eq:H0_diag_compact} and choosing \(\omega_d=\tilde{\omega}_c-\tilde{\omega}_a\) makes the beam-splitting term resonant. Under the rotating-wave approximation, the rapidly oscillating terms are dropped, yielding
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{BS}}
    =
    G_{ac}
    \left(
        e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}} d_a^\dagger d_c
        +
        e^{-i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}} d_a d_c^\dagger
    \right),
    \qquad
    G_{ac}=\frac{\delta\omega_b |\eta_{ac}|}{2},
    \quad
    \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=\phi_d+\arg(\eta_{ac}).
\end{equation}
This is the parametrically activated beam-splitter interaction between the two cavity-like dressed modes. 

More generally, the same mechanism underlies parametrically driven two-qubit gates, where modulation selectively activates entangling interactions~\cite{Strand2013,Noguchi2020,Fasciati2024}.

\subsection{Numerical Optimization of Control Parameters}

Analytical optimization is often insufficient once multiple sources of gate infidelity and hardware constraints must be treated simultaneously. Pulse design is therefore frequently recast as a numerical optimization problem~\cite{Koch2022,Song2022,Werninghaus2021}. 

In the strong-driving regime, approximations such as the rotating-wave approximation begin to fail. Counter-rotating terms can then renormalize gate parameters and generate Bloch--Siegert frequency shifts and spurious transitions, all of which contribute to gate errors~\cite{Petrescu2023,Pietikainen2017,Rower2024}. Moreover, the waveform generated by the electronics is generally not identical to the pulse seen by the qubit, because the control line introduces filtering and distortion~\cite{Rol2020,Guo2024,Hyyppa2024}. Incorporating all of these effects analytically within a single framework is difficult, so high-fidelity control is often achieved through numerical optimization~\cite{Koch2022,Song2022,Werninghaus2021}.

To incorporate these effects systematically, pulse design is formulated as an optimization problem for the time-dependent control fields $\{u_k(t)\}$. These fields are chosen so that the realized evolution $U(T)$ approximates a target operation while minimizing a cost functional. In practice, the cost functional may include not only gate infidelity, but also penalties for leakage, pulse amplitude, finite bandwidth, smoothness, robustness, and control-line distortion~\cite{Khaneja2005,Reich2012,Song2022}. Several numerical methods are widely used in this setting. Gradient-based methods such as GRAPE optimize discretized control amplitudes using derivatives of the cost functional with respect to the pulse parameters~\cite{Khaneja2005}. Krotov-type methods construct iterative updates with monotonic improvement under suitable conditions~\cite{Palao2003,Reich2012}. Basis-expansion approaches such as CRAB optimize a reduced set of pulse coefficients in a truncated function basis~\cite{Caneva2011}. 

In superconducting qubits, these methods are particularly useful for designing high-fidelity single- and two-qubit operations in realistic multilevel architectures~\cite{Werninghaus2021,Huang2014,Rol2020,Song2022}. In this thesis, we focus on GRAPE, one of the most widely used quantum optimal-control techniques.

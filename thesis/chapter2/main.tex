This chapter introduces the superconducting circuits and control techniques used throughout the thesis. The discussion in Sec. \ref{sec:sc-qubits} is organized around the three hardware paradigms introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}: the transmon, fluxonium, and bosonic qubits. Detailed discussion of decoherence mechanisms and coherence metrics is deferred to Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}. Sec.~\ref{sec:control} presents the analytical and numerical techniques used to control these systems.
\section{Superconducting Qubits}
\label{sec:sc-qubits}

All superconducting qubits rely on Josephson junctions, which makes the circuit spectrum anharmonic. This anharmonicity allows individual transitions to be spectrally distinguished and the two lowest levels to be used as an effective qubit. Qubit design must achieve two main goals: sufficiently strong anharmonicity to suppress leakage into noncomputational states, and reduced sensitivity to parameter fluctuations in order to improve robustness against noise. Different qubit modalities realize this balance through their circuit topology, parameter regime, and operating point. The following subsections introduce two widely used platforms, the transmon and the fluxonium. We also discuss a complementary platform, bosonic qubits, which encode quantum information in linear cavities.

\label{sec:transmon}

\subsubsection{Transmon Hamiltonian}

A Josephson junction consists of two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier through which Cooper pairs tunnel coherently~\cite{Gu2017junction,Oliver2013junction}. The junction is characterized by the nonlinear current--phase relation \cite{Josephson1962,Golubov2004}.
\begin{equation}
    I = I_c \sin \varphi,
\end{equation}
where $I_c$ is the critical current and $\varphi$ is the superconducting phase difference across the barrier.

The corresponding Josephson energy is
\begin{equation}
    E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi},
\end{equation}
with $\Phi_0 = h/2e$ the flux quantum. Unlike a linear inductor, whose energy is quadratic in current, the Josephson junction contributes an energy that is periodic in $\varphi$. This cosine dependence is the source of the circuit nonlinearity and, ultimately, of the anharmonicity needed for qubit operation.

Because the junction connects two superconducting islands, Cooper pairs tunnel across it in discrete units. The charge operator $n$, which counts the number of transferred Cooper pairs, therefore has integer eigenvalues. Its conjugate variable is the gauge-invariant phase difference $\varphi$. In the charge basis, the most natural phase operators are the exponentials $e^{\pm i\varphi}$, which shift the charge by one Cooper pair and satisfy
\begin{equation}
    [e^{\pm i\varphi},\, n] = \pm e^{\pm i\varphi}.
\end{equation}
The Josephson term can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    \cos \varphi = \frac{e^{i\varphi} + e^{-i\varphi}}{2},
\end{equation}
which is a well-defined operator in the charge basis.

The charging energy associated with the total shunt capacitance $C_\Sigma$ is
\begin{equation}
    E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}.
\end{equation}
Including a possible offset charge $n_g$, the circuit Hamiltonian is [Fig.~\ref{fig:transmon}(a)]
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C (n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos \varphi.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Transmon limit and weak anharmonicity}

The transmon regime is defined by
\begin{equation}
    E_J \gg E_C ,
\end{equation}
or equivalently \(E_C/E_J \ll 1\). In this limit, the low-lying states are localized near a minimum of the Josephson potential, at \(\varphi \approx 0\). The spectrum is therefore determined mainly by the local shape of the cosine potential, which is be expanded about this minimum as
\begin{equation}
    -E_J\cos\varphi
    \approx
    -E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6).
\end{equation}
The low-energy states and eigenvalues are then approximated by the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{app}}
    =
    4E_C(n-n_g)^2
    - E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6),
\end{equation}
with boundary condition
\begin{equation}
    \psi(\varphi)\to 0
    \qquad \text{as } |\varphi|\to\infty .
\end{equation}

Once the periodic boundary condition is no longer imposed, \(\varphi\) can be treated as an ordinary unbounded operator satisfying \([\varphi,n]=i\). In this approximate description, the offset charge can be removed by the unitary transformation
\begin{equation}
    U(n_g)=e^{-in_g\varphi},
\end{equation}
under which
\begin{equation}
    U n U^\dagger = n+n_g,
    \qquad
    U\varphi U^\dagger=\varphi,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    U H_{\mathrm{app}} U^\dagger
    =
    4E_C n^2
    - E_J
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    + \mathcal{O}(\varphi^6).
\end{equation}
The low-energy spectrum is therefore approximately independent of \(n_g\).

The validity of this approximation is controlled by the ratio \(E_C/E_J\). For small \(E_C/E_J\), equivalently large \(E_J/E_C\), the charge dispersion is exponentially suppressed \cite{Koch2007}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:transmon}(c), increasing \(E_J/E_C\) from 5 to 50 makes the energy levels nearly flat as functions of \(n_g\). One may therefore set \(n_g=0\) to leading order and write
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{app}}
    \approx
    4E_C n^2
    + \frac{E_J}{2}\varphi^2
    - \frac{E_J}{24}\varphi^4
    - E_J .
\end{equation}

The quadratic terms define a harmonic oscillator with plasma frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_p = \sqrt{8E_JE_C}.
\end{equation}
To reexpress Hamiltonian under oscillator basis states, we introducing ladder operators,
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}(b+b^\dagger),
    \qquad
    n = i\,n_{\mathrm{zpf}}(b^\dagger-b),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\left(\frac{2E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/4},
    \qquad
    n_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\left(\frac{E_J}{32E_C}\right)^{1/4},
\end{equation}
the harmonic part becomes
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{harm}}
    =
    \omega_p\left(b^\dagger b+\frac12\right)-E_J.
\end{equation}
The quartic term gives the leading deviation from equal level spacing, with anharmonicity
\begin{equation}
    \alpha \approx -E_C,
\end{equation}
and lowest transition frequency
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{01}\approx \sqrt{8E_JE_C}-E_C.
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|\alpha|}{\omega_{01}}
    \sim
    \left(\frac{E_C}{E_J}\right)^{1/2}\ll 1,
\end{equation}
the transmon is only weakly anharmonic.

\subsubsection{Flux-tunable transmon}

Replacing the single Josephson junction by a SQUID loop makes the Josephson energy tunable with an external magnetic flux, as shown in Fig.\ \ref{fig:flux_tunable_transmon}. A SQUID consists of two Josephson junctions connected in parallel around a superconducting loop. The applied flux \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) imposes a phase difference between the two branches, so the total tunneling amplitude is the coherent sum of two Josephson paths. The SQUID therefore behaves as a single effective junction with flux-dependent Josephson energy.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{chapter2/FTT.pdf}
    \caption[Flux dependence of the transmon transition frequency \(\omega_b/2\pi\) for a SQUID-based transmon.]{Flux dependence of the transmon transition frequency \(\omega_b/2\pi\) for a SQUID-based transmon. Parameters: \(E_{J1}/h = 12.0~\mathrm{GHz}\), \(E_{J2}/h = 8.0~\mathrm{GHz}\), and \(E_C/h = 0.25~\mathrm{GHz}\). The frequency is maximal at integer flux bias and remains finite at half-integer flux bias because of the junction asymmetry.}
    \label{fig:flux_tunable_transmon}
\end{figure}

For junction energies \(E_{J1}\) and \(E_{J2}\), and neglecting the loop inductance, the SQUID is equivalent to a single junction with effective Josephson energy
\begin{equation}
    E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})
    =
    E_{J\Sigma}
    \sqrt{
        \cos^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
        +
        d^2 \sin^2\!\left(\pi\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\Phi_0}\right)
    },
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    E_{J\Sigma} = E_{J1}+E_{J2},
    \qquad
    d = \frac{E_{J1}-E_{J2}}{E_{J1}+E_{J2}}.
\end{equation}
The parameter \(d\) quantifies the junction asymmetry. For a symmetric SQUID, \(d=0\), the tunability is maximal. For \(d\neq 0\), the effective Josephson energy does not vanish at \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}=\Phi_0/2\), so the tuning range is reduced and the qubit retains a nonzero minimum frequency.

The transmon transition frequency therefore becomes flux dependent:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_b(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})
    \approx
    \sqrt{8E_C\,E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}})} - E_C.
\end{equation}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:flux_tunable_transmon}(b), \(\omega_b\) is periodic in \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) with period \(\Phi_0\). It reaches its maximum at integer flux bias and decreases toward half-integer flux bias, where the reduction in \(E_J\) lowers the qubit frequency. 

Flux tuning provides direct control over the transmon spectrum. It can be used to adjust qubit detunings, bring the qubit into or out of resonance with other circuit elements, and access interactions that are otherwise off resonant. Since \(E_C\) is set primarily by the shunt capacitance, the anharmonicity remains approximately \(\alpha \approx -E_C\) throughout the tuning range, while the transition frequency can vary by several GHz.

Time-dependent flux control extends this tunability to parametric driving. Modulating \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) generates sidebands and enables controllable interactions between cavity modes, as discussed later in Secs.~\ref{subsec:analytical-control}.

\subsection{The Fluxonium}
\label{sec:fluxonium}

The transmon suppresses charge sensitivity by operating in the regime $E_J/E_C \gg 1$, but this same limit also makes the spectrum only weakly anharmonic. As systems scale up, the resulting small spacing between neighboring transitions increases the risk of leakage and makes selective control more difficult \cite{Koch2007,Sank2016}. Fluxonium addresses this trade-off by modifying the circuit topology itself: instead of shunting the Josephson junction only with a large capacitance, it also includes a large linear inductance. The resulting circuit accesses a qualitatively different regime of superconducting-qubit physics and was first proposed by Manucharyan \emph{et al.}~\cite{Manucharyan2009}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{chapter2/fluxonium.pdf}
    \caption[Fluxonium qubit with $E_J/2\pi = 5.0\;\text{GHz}$, $E_C/2\pi = 1.0\;\text{GHz}$, and $E_L/2\pi = 1.0\;\text{GHz}$.]{Fluxonium qubit with $E_J/2\pi = 5.0\;\text{GHz}$, $E_C/2\pi = 1.0\;\text{GHz}$, and $E_L/2\pi = 1.0\;\text{GHz}$. (a)~Lumped-element circuit: a Josephson junction with energy $E_J$ shunted by a capacitance with charging energy $E_C$ and a superinductance with inductive energy $E_L$, threaded by an external flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}$. (b)~Energy spectrum of the three lowest levels as a function of $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}/\Phi_0$. (c,\,d)~Potential landscape (gray) and probability densities of the ground (green) and first excited (orange) states at $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0.4\,\Phi_0$ (c) and at the half-flux sweet spot $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0.5\,\Phi_0$.}
    \label{fig:fluxonium}
\end{figure}

The fluxonium Hamiltonian is [Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(a)]
\begin{equation}
    H = 4E_C (n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos\varphi + \frac{1}{2} E_L (\varphi + \phi_e)^2,
    \label{eq:fluxonium_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $E_C$ is the charging energy, $E_J$ the Josephson energy, and
\begin{equation}
    E_L = \left(\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\right)^2 \frac{1}{L}
\end{equation}
is the inductive energy associated with the linear inductance $L$. The reduced external flux
\begin{equation}
    \phi_e = \frac{2\pi \Phi_e}{\Phi_0}
\end{equation}
biases the loop formed by the junction and the inductor. Relative to the transmon, the new ingredient is the quadratic inductive term, which competes with the Josephson cosine and qualitatively reshapes the potential landscape.

The inductive shunt also changes the circuit boundary conditions. In a Cooper-pair box or transmon, the two superconducting islands are connected only through the junction, so charge is transferred in discrete units and the phase is a compact variable. In fluxonium, the superinductor galvanically connects the two sides of the junction and removes this isolated-island structure. As a result, $\varphi$ may be treated as an ordinary unbounded operator and $n$ as its continuous conjugate variable, satisfying
\begin{equation}
    [\varphi,n] = i.
\end{equation}
This underlies one of the defining advantages of fluxonium compared to transmon: fluxonium is intrinsically insensitive to offset charge without requiring the large-$E_J/E_C$ limit.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fluxonium_hamiltonian} contains three energy scales---$E_C$, $E_J$, and $E_L$---together with the external flux $\phi_e$. Different ratios of these parameters produce qualitatively distinct regimes, all described by the same Hamiltonian but with different spectra and operating characteristics. Examples include heavy fluxonium \cite{Earnest2018,Zhang2021,Ficheux2021}, standard fluxonium \cite{Nguyen2019,Somoroff2023,Bao2022,Ding2023}, the inductively shunted transmon \cite{Hassani2023,Zobrist2026}, and integer fluxonium \cite{Mencia2024}. Rather than viewing these as unrelated devices, it is useful to regard them as different parameter regimes of a common circuit model.

For pedagogical concreteness, it is useful to focus on a standard fluxonium.
Away from the half-flux sweet spot, \(\phi_e \neq \pi\), the double-well potential is tilted, and the two lowest states are localized predominantly in one well or the other [Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(c)]. In this regime, the transition frequency is strongly flux dependent because the relative depth of the two wells changes directly with \(\phi_e\) [Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(b)]. The matrix elements \(\langle g|\varphi|e\rangle\) and \(\langle g|n|e\rangle\) are strongly suppressed, since the two states have little spatial overlap.

At the sweet spot, \(\phi_e=\pi\), the potential
\begin{equation}
    V(\varphi)= -E_J \cos\varphi + \frac{1}{2} E_L (\varphi + \pi)^2
\end{equation}
becomes a symmetric double well [Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(d)]. The two lowest eigenstates are then approximately symmetric and antisymmetric superpositions of states in the two wells, so the low-energy spectrum is governed by tunneling rather than by weak anharmonic corrections to a single harmonic well, as in the transmon. At this bias point, the transition frequency is first-order insensitive to flux,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \omega_{ge}}{\partial \phi_e}=0,
\end{equation}
while the anharmonicity remains large, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fluxonium}(b). This combination of flux-noise protection and strong spectral nonuniformity is a defining feature of fluxonium.
\subsection{Bosonic Qubits}
\label{sec:bosonic_qubits}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{chapter2/qec.pdf}
    \caption[Bosonic quantum error correction.]{Bosonic quantum error correction. (a)~Dual-rail encoding: the logical subspace is spanned by $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ across two bosonic modes.     \label{fig:bosonic_qec}

Transmon and fluxonium qubits encode quantum information in the lowest two levels of an anharmonic circuit. Bosonic qubits follow a different strategy: they encode a logical qubit in the large Hilbert space of a harmonic oscillator, typically a superconducting microwave cavity. Because the cavity mode contains no Josephson junction, it can be much longer lived than junction-based qubits, particularly in three-dimensional architectures \cite{Paik2011,Reagor2016,Milul2023}. Its dominant error channel is often single-photon loss rather than pure dephasing. This pronounced noise bias makes bosonic modes a natural platform for hardware-efficient quantum error correction, in which the encoding is tailored to the dominant physical error \cite{Cai2021,Copetudo2024}.

The central idea of Bosonic encoding is to use the oscillator Hilbert space to encode a logical qubit redundantly, so that the dominant error produces a detectable and, in favorable cases, correctable signature. The need for such redundancy is clear from comparison with an ordinary two-level qubit. If a qubit is measured in $|0\rangle$, the final result alone does not reveal whether the computation genuinely produced $|0\rangle$ or whether an intended $|1\rangle$ relaxed before readout. The error acts within the computational subspace and leaves no direct flag.

The simplest bosonic example is the dual-rail code [Fig.~\ref{fig:bosonic_qec}(a)],
\begin{equation}
    |0_L\rangle = |1,0\rangle, \qquad |1_L\rangle = |0,1\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the logical qubit is stored across two bosonic modes. A single-photon loss maps both logical basis states to the vacuum state $|0,0\rangle$, which lies outside the code space. Photon loss therefore appears as a detectable leakage event: monitoring the total excitation number reveals that an error has occurred. Although the dual-rail code does not recover the original superposition coefficients, it illustrates the essential principle of bosonic error detection: redundancy is used so that the dominant error becomes visible rather than remaining hidden within the logical subspace.

To achieve correction rather than mere detection, the encoding must preserve the logical amplitudes after the error. A simple example is the binomial code [Fig.~\ref{fig:bosonic_qec}(b)],
\begin{equation}
    |0_L\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |4\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad |1_L\rangle = |2\rangle.
\end{equation}
These logical states occupy only even-photon-number Fock states. A single-photon loss maps the code space into the odd-parity sector,
\begin{equation}
    a|0_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\,|3\rangle, \qquad a|1_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\,|1\rangle,
\end{equation}
so the loss event is identified by a change in photon-number parity. Unlike the dual-rail code, the error states remain distinct and retain the encoded amplitudes. For an encoded state $|\psi_L\rangle = \alpha|0_L\rangle + \beta|1_L\rangle$, photon loss induces
\begin{equation}
    a|\psi_L\rangle = \sqrt{2}\left(\alpha|3\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\right).
\end{equation}
So the coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are preserved. The binomial code therefore illustrates how the oscillator Hilbert space can be engineered so that the dominant physical error is, in principle, correctable.

Other bosonic encodings implement the same idea with different state structures and noise models. Cat codes \cite{Mirrahimi2014,Grimm2020} use superpositions of coherent states and typically extract the error syndrome through photon-number parity, while Gottesman--Kitaev--Preskill (GKP) states \cite{Gottesman2001,CampagneIbarcq2020} encode information in a grid-like structure in phase space. Despite these differences, the common principle is the same: the encoding is matched to the dominant noise channel of the bosonic mode.

Due to absence of non-linearity, linear driving cavity can only produces coherent states. Preparation of the non-Gaussian states required for bosonic encoding, such as cat states and GKP states requires coupling the cavity to a nonlinear ancilla or coupler, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:analytical-control}.

\section{Decoherence in Superconducting Qubits}
\label{sec:decoherence}

Superconducting qubits undergo decoherence because of unavoidable coupling to the environment. This coupling has two main effects. First, it induces transitions between qubit states, including relaxation \(\ket{1}\rightarrow\ket{0}\) and thermal excitation \(\ket{0}\rightarrow\ket{1}\). Second, it causes fluctuations in the qubit frequency, which lead to random phase accumulation and hence pure dephasing. This section introduces the basic description of decoherence, defines the corresponding coherence metrics, discusses representative noise channels, and explains how sweet spots suppress dephasing by reducing first-order sensitivity to noise.

\subsection{Relaxation and Pure Dephasing: \texorpdfstring{$T_1$ and $T_{\phi}$}{T1 and Tphi}}
\label{subsec:t1-tphi}

Decoherence in superconducting qubits originates from unavoidable coupling to the environment. A simple description is to let a control parameter in the Hamiltonian fluctuate in time,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(t) = \lambda_0 + \delta\lambda(t),
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_0\) is the operating point and \(\delta\lambda(t)\) is the noise. Expanding the Hamiltonian around \(\lambda_0\) gives
\begin{equation}
    H(t) \approx H_0 + B_\lambda\,\delta\lambda(t) + \mathcal{O}\big((\delta\lambda)^2\big),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    H_0 = H(\lambda_0),
    \qquad
    B_\lambda = \left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial \lambda}\right|_{\lambda_0}.
\end{equation}
The noise is characterized by the spectral density
\begin{equation}
    S_\lambda(\omega)
    =
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dt\, e^{i\omega t}
    \langle \delta\lambda(t)\delta\lambda(0)\rangle,
\end{equation}
which is the Fourier transform of the noise correlation function. This fluctuating coupling affects both populations and phases, and therefore causes both depolarization and pure dephasing.

\noindent\textbf{Depolarization and \(T_1\).}
Depolarization corresponds to transitions between the qubit states and includes both relaxation and thermal excitation. To first order in the noisy coupling, the corresponding rates are \cite{RevModPhys.82.1155}
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\downarrow}
    =
    \left|\langle 0|B_\lambda|1\rangle\right|^2
    S_\lambda(\omega_q),
    \qquad
    \Gamma_{\uparrow}
    =
    \left|\langle 0|B_\lambda|1\rangle\right|^2
    S_\lambda(-\omega_q),
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_q\) is the qubit frequency. For classical noise, \(S_\lambda(\omega)=S_\lambda(-\omega)\), so the two rates are equal. For a quantized environment, however, the positive- and negative-frequency components generally differ and satisfy the detailed-balance relation in thermal equilibrium \cite{breuer2002quantummaster}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Gamma_{\uparrow}}{\Gamma_{\downarrow}}
    =
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{\omega_q}{k_B T}\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, when \(k_B T \ll \omega_q\), thermal excitation is negligible and relaxation dominates. The depolarization time \(T_1\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{T_1} = \Gamma_{\downarrow} + \Gamma_{\uparrow}.
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Pure dephasing and \(T_\phi\).}
Pure dephasing arises from fluctuations of the qubit frequency itself. For the same noisy coupling, the pure-dephasing rate in the Markovian limit is \cite{RevModPhys.82.1155}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pure-dephasing}
    \Gamma_{\phi}
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left(
    \langle 0|B_\lambda|0\rangle
    -
    \langle 1|B_\lambda|1\rangle
    \right)^2
    S_\lambda(0)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left(
    \frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \lambda}
    \right)^2
    S_\lambda(0),
\end{equation}
where the second equality follows from \(B_\lambda = \partial H/\partial \lambda\). The pure-dephasing time is
\begin{equation}
    T_\phi = \frac{1}{\Gamma_\phi}.
\end{equation}

In superconducting qubits, however, important dephasing channels such as \(1/f\) flux noise are dominated by very low frequencies. Pure dephasing therefore requires more care than the simple Markovian limit of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pure-dephasing} \cite{groszkowski2018coherence}. The \(1/f\) case is discussed below.

\subsection{Examples of Important Noise Channels}
\label{subsec:noise-channels}

Decoherence is determined by two ingredients: the system operator through which the environment couples to the circuit, and the spectrum of the corresponding fluctuation. We now discuss several important noise channels in superconducting circuits.

\paragraph{Dielectric loss.}
Dielectric loss arises from dielectric materials that participate in the circuit capacitance, such as the substrate, native oxides, and material interfaces \cite{martinis2005decoherence}. It couples to the charge degree of freedom, so the system operator is
\[
B_\lambda = 2e\,n.
\]
A common model for the corresponding spectral density is \cite{Smith2020,Nguyen2019}
\[
S(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{|\omega|\,C_J\,Q_{\rm cap}(\omega)}
\left(1+\coth\frac{|\omega|}{2k_B T}\right),
\]
where \(C_J\) is the relevant capacitance and \(Q_{\rm cap}(\omega)\) is the dielectric quality factor. Because this channel has spectral weight at the qubit frequency, it drives transitions between eigenstates. It therefore causes relaxation and, at finite temperature, thermal excitation. Dielectric loss is often a major contributor to \(T_1\) in superconducting qubits \cite{Murray2021dielec}.

\paragraph{Inductive loss.}
Inductive loss arises from dissipation in an inductance or superinductance. It couples to the flux degree of freedom, so the system operator is
\[
B_\lambda=\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\,\phi.
\]
The spectral density may be written as \cite{Smith2020,Nguyen2019}
\[
S(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{|\omega|\,L_J\,Q_{\rm ind}(\omega)}
\left(1+\coth\frac{|\omega|}{2k_B T}\right),
\]
where \(L_J\) is the relevant inductance and \(Q_{\rm ind}(\omega)\) is the inductive quality factor. Like dielectric loss, this channel exchanges energy with the system and therefore causes depolarization. In fluxonium, for example, superinductor loss can limit \(T_1\) \cite{Hazard2019inductor}.

\paragraph{\texorpdfstring{$1/f$}{1/f} flux noise.}
A major dephasing channel in superconducting circuits is low-frequency \(1/f\) noise. If a circuit parameter \(\lambda\) fluctuates slowly, the qubit frequency \(\omega_q\) also fluctuates. A superposition state then accumulates a random relative phase. For \(1/f\) noise, the spectral density is commonly written as
\[
S_{\lambda}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi A_{\lambda}^2}{|\omega|},
\]
where \(A_{\lambda}\) sets the noise amplitude. Because this spectrum is concentrated at low frequencies, its main effect is usually pure dephasing rather than energy relaxation.

An important example is \(1/f\) flux noise, where the fluctuating parameter is the external flux \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\). This noise is especially relevant in flux-tunable circuits because fluctuations of \(\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\) directly modulate the qubit frequency. The corresponding system operator is
\[
B_\lambda=\frac{\partial H}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}},
\]
and the spectral density is
\[
S_{\Phi}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi A_{\Phi}^2}{|\omega|}.
\]
Away from bias points where the qubit is first-order insensitive to flux, the dephasing time is approximately \cite{groszkowski2018coherence}
\[
\frac{1}{T_{\phi}}
=
\sqrt{2}\,A_{\Phi}
\left|\frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right|
\sqrt{\bigl|\ln(\omega_{\rm low} t_{\rm exp})\bigr|},
\]
where \(\omega_{\rm low}\) is a low-frequency cutoff and \(t_{\rm exp}\) is the experimental timescale. The key factor is the flux sensitivity \(\partial \omega_q/\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\): stronger flux dependence gives faster dephasing.

\subsection{Improve Dephasing Time by Sweet Spots: First-Order Noise Insensitivity}
\label{subsec:sweet-spots}

The result above shows that dephasing from \(1/f\) flux noise is set by the flux sensitivity \(\partial \omega_q/\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}\). This suggests a simple strategy: bias the circuit at a point where the qubit frequency is first-order insensitive to flux,
\[
\frac{\partial \omega_q}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}=0.
\]
Such points are called sweet spots. At a sweet spot, the linear response to flux noise vanishes. The leading dephasing is then pushed to higher order.

The same idea applies to a periodically driven qubit \cite{Huang2021DynamicalSweetSpots,Valery2022DynamicalSweetSpot}. When
\[
H(t+T)=H(t),
\]
the natural basis is given by Floquet states,
\[
|\psi_\alpha(t)\rangle
=
e^{-i\epsilon_\alpha t}
|\phi_\alpha(t)\rangle,
\qquad
|\phi_\alpha(t+T)\rangle = |\phi_\alpha(t)\rangle,
\]
where \(\epsilon_\alpha\) is the quasienergy and \(|\phi_\alpha(t)\rangle\) is a time-periodic Floquet mode. In the driven problem, these dressed states play the role of the computational states. The relevant dressed qubit frequency is therefore
\[
\omega_q^{(\mathrm{F})}=\epsilon_1-\epsilon_0,
\]
up to the usual modulo-\(\omega_d\) ambiguity of Floquet theory.

If the external flux fluctuates slowly, it modulates this dressed qubit frequency and causes dephasing between the two Floquet states. A dynamical sweet spot is reached when
\[
\frac{\partial \omega_q^{(\mathrm{F})}}{\partial \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}=0.
\]
At this point, the drive makes the dressed qubit frequency first-order insensitive to flux noise and suppresses the leading contribution of \(1/f\) flux noise to pure dephasing.

\section{Control of Superconducting Qubits}
Having introduced superconducting-qubit platforms and the decoherence mechanisms that constrain their performance, we now turn to their control. The goal is to engineer drive pulses that realize target gates with high fidelity. We begin with two examples of analytical optimization of control, and then move to numerical optimization when analytical approaches are no longer sufficient.
\label{sec:control}

\subsection{Analytical Optimization of Control Parameters}
\label{subsec:analytical-control}
Analytical optimization plays a central role in superconducting-qubit control because it provides pulse-design principles are physically transparent. Two particularly important examples are DRAG for single-qubit control and parametric-drive schemes for two-qubit gates. Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate (DRAG) is designed to suppress leakage to non-computational states in weakly anharmonic systems, whereas parametric modulation enables selective activation of qubit-qubit interactions for entangling operations. These protocols illustrate how control parameters can be chosen from the underlying Hamiltonian structure to obtain high-fidelity gate before resorting to fully numerical methods.

\paragraph{DRAG}

A simple way to understand DRAG is through the counter-diabatic construction~\cite{Berry2009,GueryOdelin2019}. In a three-level truncation and in the rotating frame of the $\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}$ transition, the driven Hamiltonian can be written as
\[
H(t)=\frac{\Omega_x(t)}{2}\left(\sigma_x^{01}+\lambda \sigma_x^{12}\right)
+\frac{\Omega_y(t)}{2}\left(\sigma_y^{01}+\lambda \sigma_y^{12}\right)
+\alpha \ket{2}\bra{2},
\]
where $\alpha=\omega_{12}-\omega_{01}$ is the anharmonicity and $\lambda\simeq\sqrt{2}$ for a transmon. The leading leakage process is governed by the off-resonant $\{\ket{1},\ket{2}\}$ block,
\[
H_{12}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda \Omega_x(t)\sigma_x^{12}+\alpha \sigma_z^{12}\right).
\]
Defining the instantaneous mixing angle by
\[
\tan\theta(t)=\frac{\lambda \Omega_x(t)}{\alpha},
\]
the corresponding counter-diabatic correction \cite{Berry2009,GueryOdelin2019} is
\[
H_{\mathrm{cd}}(t)=-\frac{\dot{\theta}(t)}{2}\sigma_y^{12}
=-\frac{\lambda \alpha\,\dot{\Omega}_x(t)}
{2\left[\alpha^2+\lambda^2\Omega_x^2(t)\right]}\sigma_y^{12}.
\]
In the weak-drive limit $\abs{\Omega_x}\ll \abs{\alpha}$, this reduces to
\[
H_{\mathrm{cd}}(t)\approx -\frac{\lambda}{2\alpha}\dot{\Omega}_x(t)\sigma_y^{12}.
\]
Since the available $y$-quadrature contributes
\[
\frac{\Omega_y(t)}{2}\left(\sigma_y^{01}+\lambda \sigma_y^{12}\right),
\]
matching its $\ket{1}\leftrightarrow\ket{2}$ component to the counter-diabatic term gives the leading-order DRAG condition
\[
\Omega_y(t)\approx -\frac{1}{\alpha}\dot{\Omega}_x(t).
\]
The DRAG-based pulse has been demonstrated in obtaining high-fidelity transmon gate, and the pulse outperforms naive single-quadrature Gaussian pulse~\cite{Hyyppa2024}.

\paragraph{Parametric Drive}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{chapter2/twomode.pdf}
\caption[Parametric-drive model and circuit implementation.]{Parametric-drive model and circuit implementation. (a) Graph representation of the three-mode system, where the bare modes are coupled capacitively (light-gray edges) and mode \(b\) is a flux-tunable transmon whose frequency is modulated by a parametric flux drive. (b) Superconducting-circuit implementation of the model, in which modes \(a\) and \(c\) are cavity modes and mode \(b\) is realized as a flux-tunable transmon coupler.}
\label{fig:twomode}
\end{figure}

Parametric driving is used to activate desired interactions between different qubits. For illustration, we consider an FTT capacitively coupled to two cavities, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:twomode}. The system Hamiltonian is~\cite{Petrescu2023}
\begin{equation}
    H = H^{(0)} + H^{(1)}(t),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    H^{(0)}
    =
    \omega_a a^\dagger a
    +
    \omega_c c^\dagger c
    +
    \omega_b b^\dagger b
    +
    g_{ab}(a^\dagger b + a b^\dagger)
    +
    g_{bc}(c^\dagger b + c b^\dagger),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    H^{(1)}(t)
    =
    -\frac{K_b}{2} b^{\dagger 2} b^2
    +
    \delta\omega_b \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)\, b^\dagger b.
\end{equation}
Here \(\delta\omega_b\) is the flux-induced modulation amplitude of the transmon frequency, while \(\omega_d\) and \(\phi_d\) are the drive frequency and phase. Diagonalizing the linear part \(H^{(0)}\) yields the normal modes of the coupled system,
\begin{equation}
    H^{(0)}
    =
    \tilde{\omega}_a a^\dagger a
    +
    \tilde{\omega}_b b^\dagger b
    +
    \tilde{\omega}_c c^\dagger c.
    \label{eq:H0_diag_compact}
\end{equation}
The dressed frequencies \(\tilde{\omega}_{a,b,c}\) and the mode compositions depend on the bare frequencies \(\omega_{a,b,c}\) and the static couplings \(g_{ab}\) and \(g_{bc}\). In the dispersive regime, two modes remain predominantly cavity-like, while one remains predominantly transmon-like.

Expressing the bare transmon-like operator in the dressed basis as
\begin{equation}
    b = \sum_{\nu=a,b,c} u_{b\nu} d_\nu,
\end{equation}
the modulation term becomes
\begin{equation}
    H^{(1)}(t)
    \approx
    -\frac{\tilde K_b}{2} b^{\dagger 2} b^2
    +
    \delta\omega_b \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)
    \sum_{\mu,\nu=a,b,c}
    \eta_{\mu\nu} d_\mu^\dagger d_\nu,
    \label{eq:H1_normalmode_compact}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mu\nu}=u_{b\mu}^*u_{b\nu}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the flux modulation generates not only diagonal frequency shifts but also off-diagonal mode-conversion terms between the dressed modes. In particular, the interaction between the two cavity-like modes is governed by the coefficient
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{ac}=u_{ba}^*u_{bc},
\end{equation}
which gives
\begin{equation}
    H_{ac}(t)
    =
    \delta\omega_b \cos(\omega_d t+\phi_d)
    \left(
        \eta_{ac} a^\dagger c
        +
        \eta_{ac}^* a c^\dagger
    \right).
\end{equation}

Writing \(\eta_{ac}=|\eta_{ac}|e^{i\theta_{ac}}\), we define
\begin{equation}
    \delta g_{ac}=\delta\omega_b |\eta_{ac}|,
    \qquad
    \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=\phi_d+\theta_{ac}.
\end{equation}
The interaction then becomes
\begin{equation}
    H_{ac}(t)
    =
    \delta g_{ac}\cos(\omega_d t+\phi_{\mathrm{eff}})
    \left(
        a^\dagger c + a c^\dagger
    \right).
\end{equation}
Moving to the interaction picture with respect to the dressed-mode energies and choosing
\begin{equation}
    \omega_d=\tilde{\omega}_c-\tilde{\omega}_a,
\end{equation}
makes the exchange term resonant. Under the rotating-wave approximation, the rapidly oscillating terms are neglected. The effective interaction then reduces to
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{BS}}
    =
    G_{ac}
    \left(
        e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}} a^\dagger c
        +
        e^{-i\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}} a c^\dagger
    \right),
    \qquad
    G_{ac}=\frac{\delta g_{ac}}{2}.
\end{equation}
This is the parametrically activated beam-splitter interaction between the two cavity-like modes. It originates from the static hybridization with the transmon-like mode, while the drive frequency selects which dressed-mode transition is activated resonantly. More generally, the same mechanism underlies parametrically driven two-qubit gates, where modulation selectively turns on entangling interactions~\cite{Strand2013,Noguchi2020,Fasciati2024}.


\subsection{Numerical Optimization of Control Parameters}

Analytical optimization is often insufficient once multiple sources of gate infidelity and hardware constraints must be treated simultaneously. Pulse design is therefore frequently recast as a numerical optimization problem~\cite{Koch2022,Song2022,Werninghaus2021}. In the strong-driving regime, approximations such as the rotating-wave approximation begin to fail. Counter-rotating terms can then renormalize gate parameters and generate Bloch--Siegert frequency shifts and spurious transitions, all of which contribute to gate errors~\cite{Petrescu2023,Pietikainen2017,Rower2024}. Moreover, the waveform generated by the electronics is generally not identical to the pulse seen by the qubit, because the control line introduces filtering and distortion that must often be characterized on-chip and corrected by predistortion~\cite{Rol2020,Guo2024,Hyyppa2024}. Since it is difficult to incorporate all of these effects analytically within a single framework, high-fidelity control is often achieved through numerical optimization based on a realistic device model~\cite{Koch2022,Song2022,Werninghaus2021}.

To incorporate these effects systematically, pulse design is formulated as an optimization problem for the time-dependent control fields $\{u_k(t)\}$. These fields are chosen so that the realized evolution $U(T)$ approximates a target operation while minimizing a cost functional. In practice, the cost functional may include not only gate infidelity, but also penalties for leakage, pulse amplitude, finite bandwidth, smoothness, robustness, and control-line distortion~\cite{Khaneja2005,Reich2012,Song2022}. Several numerical methods are widely used in this setting. Gradient-based methods such as GRAPE optimize discretized control amplitudes using derivatives of the cost functional with respect to the pulse parameters~\cite{Khaneja2005}. Krotov-type methods construct iterative updates with monotonic improvement under suitable conditions~\cite{Palao2003,Reich2012}. Basis-expansion approaches such as CRAB optimize a reduced set of pulse coefficients in a truncated function basis~\cite{Caneva2011}. 

In superconducting-qubit platforms, these methods are particularly useful for suppressing leakage in fast gates, compensating for control-line distortion, and designing high-fidelity single- and two-qubit operations in realistic multilevel architectures~\cite{Werninghaus2021,Huang2014,Rol2020,Song2022}. In practice, numerical optimization is often most effective when combined with analytically motivated pulse ans\"atze or prior physical insight~\cite{Song2022}. In this thesis, we focus on GRAPE which is one of the most commonly used quantum optimal control technique.

\chapter{GRAPE: Theory, Gradients, and Implementation}
\label{chap:grape}

One of the prominent techniques for implementing quantum optimal control is Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering (GRAPE). The central goal of optimal control is to adjust control parameters in such a way that the infidelity of the desired state transfer or gate operation is minimized. In GRAPE, the minimization is based on gradient descent and thus generally requires the evaluation of gradients. Automatic differentiation (AD) \cite{baydin2018automatic} is a convenient tool that has also made an impact on quantum optimal control with GRAPE \cite{pacleung2017speedup,pacqoc,abdelhafez2019gradient,grapead}.
Internally, AD builds a computational graph and applies the chain rule to automatically compute the gradient of a given function. 

However, the convenience of AD comes at the cost of memory required for storing the full computational graph. The use of semi-automatic differentiation (semi-AD) \cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum} partially reduces the memory overhead compared to full automatic differentiation (full-AD). This reduction can still be insufficient to tackle optimal control for quantum systems of rapidly growing size \cite{arute2019quantum,gong2021quantum}. This motivates revisiting the original GRAPE scheme \cite{Khaneja2005}, which is based on hard-coded gradients (HG) and has the smallest possible memory cost. We perform a scaling analysis of memory usage and runtime and thereby develops a decision tree guiding the optimal choice of strategy among HG, semi-AD, and full-AD. The scaling behavior is exemplified with benchmarks for concrete optimization tasks.

The outline of this chapter is as follows. Sec.\ \ref{sec:grape-theory} briefly reviews GRAPE and introduces necessary notation. In Sec.\ \ref{sec:gradient-strategies}, we review three strategies of gradient computation: full automatic differentiation, semi-automatic differentiation, and hard-coded gradients. Sec.\ \ref{sec:numerical-gradients} illustrates the derivation and memory-efficient implementation of the gradients for the most commonly used cost function contributions. The numerical implementation of HG in this work further improves the efficiency of state propagation. In Sec.\ \ref{sec:scaling-analysis}, we analyze and compares the scaling of runtime and memory usage for HG, AD, and semi-AD. Sec.\ \ref{sec:benchmark-studies} presents results from concrete benchmark studies. In Sec.\ \ref{sec:decision-tree}, we formulate a decision tree that facilitates the choice of optimal numerical strategy for GRAPE-based quantum optimal control. 
\section{Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering: Theory and Notation}
\label{sec:grape-theory}
This section briefly sketches the basics of GRAPE \cite{Khaneja2005}, mainly to establish the notion used in subsequent sections. 
\subsection{Pulse Parametrization and Time Discretization}
\label{subsec:pulse-param}
Consider a system described by the Hamiltonian
\begin{align}
     H(t) = H_s + a(t)\, h_c.
\end{align}
Here, $H_s$ denotes the static system Hamiltonian and $h_c$ is an operator coupling the classical control field $a(t)$ to the system\footnote{For simplicity, we consider one control channel with real-valued $a(t)$. Generalizations to multiple control channels and complex $a$ are straightforward.}. Quantum optimal control aims to adjust $a(t)$ such that a desired unitary gate or state transfer is performed within a given time interval $0\le t \le T$. To facilitate optimization, the total control time $T$ is divided into $N$ intervals of duration $\Delta t=T/N$, and the control field is specified by the amplitudes at the discrete times $t_n=n\Delta t$ ($n=1,2,\dots,N$). The discretization interval $\Delta t$ is chosen such that control amplitudes are approximately constant within each $\Delta t$. This treatment is actually quite close to modeling the actual drive output of an arbitrary waveform generator, where $\Delta t$ can be chosen to align with the available resolution. Denote the time-discretized values by
\begin{align}
\label{eq2}
    a_n \coloneqq a(t_n),
\end{align}
and group these to form the vector $\textbf{a}\in \mathbb{R}^{N}$.  
Under the influence of this piecewise-constant control field, the system's quantum state $\dket{\psi_n}\coloneqq \dket{\psi(t_n)}$ evolves by a single time step according to 
\begin{align}
\label{statepropagation}
  |\psi_n\rangle=U_n|\psi_{n-1}\rangle.
\end{align}
Here, $U_n$ is the short-time propagator
\begin{align}
\label{propagator}
  U_n\coloneqq U(t_n, t_{n-1}) =e^{-iH_n\Delta t}\qquad(\hbar=1),
\end{align}
where $H_n=\, H_s+a_n\, h_c $. 

\subsection{Cost Functions: State-Transfer Infidelity, Gate Infidelity, and Additional Contributions}
\label{subsec: cost functions}
Optimization of the control field proceeds via minimization of a cost function $C$. Typically, $C$ includes the state-transfer or gate infidelity, along with additional cost contributions employed to moderate pulse characteristics such as maximal power or bandwidth. This section introduces the cost functions studied in chapter.

For state-transfer problems, a central objective is to minimize the final-time state-transfer infidelity
\begin{equation}
\label{C1st}
C_1^{\mathrm{st}}
=
1-\left|\left\langle \phi_T \middle| \psi_N \right\rangle\right|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\dket{\phi_T}$ is the target state and $\dket{\psi_N}$ is the realized state at the final time $t_N$.

For gate optimization, the corresponding objective is the gate infidelity
\begin{equation}
C_1^{g}
=
1-\left|\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_T^\dagger U_R\right)/d\right|^2,
\end{equation}
where $U_T$ is the target unitary gate, $U_R=U_N\cdots U_1$ is the realized gate, and $d$ is the Hilbert-space dimension.

In addition to these final-time objectives, we also consider cost contributions that depend on every time step. One example is
\begin{equation}
\label{c2st}
C_2^{\mathrm{st}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N \dbra{\psi_{n'}}\Omega \dket{\psi_{n'}},
\end{equation}
which penalizes the integrated expectation value of a Hermitian operator $\Omega$. Depending on the choice of $\Omega$, this term can be used to suppress unwanted  occupation of states with bad coherence time.

Another useful contribution is
\begin{equation}
C_3^{\mathrm{st}}
=
1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\left\langle \phi_T \middle| \psi_{n'} \right\rangle
\right|^2.
\end{equation}
Unlike $C_1^{\mathrm{st}}$, which only penalizes the final-time infidelity, $C_3^{\mathrm{st}}$ accumulates the infidelity over all time steps. This term encourages the system to approach the target state earlier in the evolution and can therefore favor shorter state-transfer times.

For gate operations, the analogous cost is
\begin{equation}
C_3^{g}
=
1-\frac{1}{N d^2}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_T^\dagger U_{n',1}\right)
\right|^2,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
U_{n',1}=U_{n'}\cdots U_1
\end{equation}
is the propagator from the initial time to time step $n'$. This cost helps minimize time of gate operations.

 
\subsection{Pulse Optimization Based on Gradient Information}
\label{subsec:pulse-opt-gradient}

After choosing cost functions of interest, a composite cost function $C(\textbf{a})=\sum_{\nu}\alpha_\nu C_\nu \parenthesis{\textbf{a}}$ is constructed, , where $\alpha_\nu$ are empirically chosen weight factors and $C_\nu$ are individual cost contributions. GRAPE utilizes the method of steepest descent to minimize $C(\textbf{a})$ by updating the control amplitudes according to the cost function gradient: 
\begin{align}
  \textbf{a}^{(j+1)}=\textbf{a}^{(j)}-\eta_j\,\nabla_{\textbf{a}}C(\textbf{a}^{(j)}).
\end{align}
Here, $j$ enumerates steepest-descent iterations and $\eta_j>0$ is the learning rate governing the step size.

\section{Gradient Computation Strategies}
\label{sec:gradient-strategies}
A critical ingredient for GRAPE is the computation of cost function gradients. We discuss three gradient Computation Strategies in this section.

\subsection{Full Automatic Differentiation}
\label{subsec:full-ad}

In quantum optimal control, introducing new cost terms often requires deriving additional gradient terms by hand. Automatic differentiation (AD) avoids this by representing the objective as a computational graph of elementary operations and evaluating gradients efficiently through reverse propagation. AD calculates gradients with the accuracy and stability of analytic derivatives while removing the need for problem-specific manual gradient calculations.

The central idea of AD is that a complicated objective function can be represented as a composition of elementary operations. One first specifies a set of primitive operations, such as addition and multiplication, together with their local derivative rules. During the forward pass, the computational graph is evaluated sequentially from the inputs to the output, and the intermediate values are stored. During the backward pass, the derivative rule associated with each primitive operation is applied in reverse order, and the chain rule is used to accumulate gradients with respect to the input variables. In this way, AD computes gradients by systematically combining the predefined local derivatives of the primitive operations with the stored intermediate values.

To illustrate the basic idea of AD, we consider the scalar cost function
\begin{equation}
    C(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 + x_2)e^{x_1}.
\end{equation}
This function can be decomposed into the intermediate variables
\begin{equation}
    z_0 = x_1 + x_2, \qquad
    z_1 = e^{x_1}, \qquad
    C = z_0 z_1.
\end{equation}
The corresponding computational graph consists of three primitive operations: addition, exponentiation, and multiplication. In the forward pass, the inputs \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are used to evaluate \(z_0\), then \(z_1\), and finally the output \(C\). The primal values \(z_0\), \(z_1\), and \(C\) are stored because they are reused in the backward pass.

In reverse-mode AD, adjoint variables are defined as the derivatives of the output with respect to each intermediate variable. The backward pass is initialized by setting
\begin{equation}
    \bar{C} \equiv \frac{\partial C}{\partial C} = 1.
\end{equation}
Applying the local derivative rule for the multiplication \(C = z_0 z_1\) gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar{z}_0 = \bar{C} \frac{\partial C}{\partial z_0} = z_1,
    \qquad
    \bar{z}_1 = \bar{C} \frac{\partial C}{\partial z_1} = z_0.
\end{equation}
These expressions show that the adjoints of the intermediate variables are obtained from the stored forward-pass values and the local derivative rule for multiplication. Proceeding further backward through the graph, the gradients with respect to the inputs are obtained using the derivative rules for the addition and exponentiation operations. Since \(z_0 = x_1 + x_2\) and \(z_1 = e^{x_1}\), the relevant local derivatives are
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial z_0}{\partial x_1} = 1,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial z_0}{\partial x_2} = 1,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial x_1} = e^{x_1} = z_1.
\end{equation}
The gradient with respect to \(x_1\) receives contributions from two distinct paths in the computational graph, namely \(x_1 \rightarrow z_0 \rightarrow C\) and \(x_1 \rightarrow z_1 \rightarrow C\). Reverse-mode AD accumulates these contributions automatically:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{x}_1
    =
    \bar{z}_0 \frac{\partial z_0}{\partial x_1}
    +
    \bar{z}_1 \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial x_1}
    =
    z_1 + z_0 z_1
    =
    e^{x_1}(x_1 + x_2 + 1).
\end{equation}
By contrast, \(x_2\) contributes through only a single path, \(x_2 \rightarrow z_0 \rightarrow C\), and therefore
\begin{equation}
    \bar{x}_2
    =
    \bar{z}_0 \frac{\partial z_0}{\partial x_2}
    =
    z_1
    =
    e^{x_1}.
\end{equation}
This simple example illustrates the essential structure of reverse-mode AD. Its implementation in quantum optimal control is discussed in Ref.~\cite{pacleung2017speedup}.
\subsection{Semi-Automatic Differentiation}
\label{subsec:semi-ad}
The convenience of AD comes at the cost of memory required to store the full computational graph. The semi-AD approach~\cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum} partially reduces this memory overhead compared to full automatic differentiation (full-AD). This is achieved by applying AD only to the differentiation of the final cost function with respect to selected intermediate variables. Using the example discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:full-ad}, AD is employed only to evaluate the top-level adjoints \(\bar z_0\) and \(\bar z_1\). Once these adjoints are initialized automatically, the remainder of the backward pass, namely the evaluation of \(\bar x_1\) and \(\bar x_2\), is carried out analytically. This avoids the need to store the intermediate values that full-AD would otherwise require to compute \(\bar x_1\) and \(\bar x_2\). For an implementation of semi-AD in quantum optimal control, see Ref.~\cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum}.\

\subsection{Hard-Coded Gradients: Analytical Derivation}
\label{subsec:hard-coded-gradients}
Semi-AD partially reduces memory overhead compared to full-AD. However, this reduction can still be insufficient to tackle optimal
control for quantum systems of rapidly growing size. Therefore, we revisit hard-coded gradients
in a computationally efficient scheme for key cost contributions.
Analytical gradient expressions for the cost functions introduced in Sec.\ \ref{subsec: cost functions} are presented next.

For the state-transfer infidelity $C_1^{\mathrm{st}}$, the gradient is
\begin{align}
\label{stgradient}
\frac{\partial C_1^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial a_n}
&=
-\left\langle \phi_T \middle| U_N\cdots \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n}\cdots U_1 \middle| \psi_0 \right\rangle
\left\langle \psi_N \middle| \phi_T \right\rangle
-\mathrm{c.c.}
\nonumber\\
&=
-\left\langle \phi_n \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1} \right\rangle
\left\langle \psi_N \middle| \phi_T \right\rangle
-\mathrm{c.c.},
\end{align}
where the backward-propagated state $\dket{\phi_n}$ is defined recursively by
\begin{equation}
\dket{\phi_n}=U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\phi_{n+1}},
\end{equation}
with terminal condition $\dket{\phi_N}=\dket{\phi_T}$.

For gate optimization, consider an orthonormal basis $\{\dket{\psi_0^h}\}_h$, where $h$ labels the basis states, and define
\begin{equation}
\dket{\psi_n^h}=U_n\cdots U_1 \dket{\psi_0^h}.
\end{equation}
Then the gradient of the gate infidelity $C_1^g$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{gategradient}
\frac{\partial C_1^g}{\partial a_n}
=
-\frac{1}{d^2}
\sum_h
\left\langle \phi_n^h \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1}^h \right\rangle
\sum_{h'}
\left\langle \psi_N^{h'} \middle| \phi_N^{h'} \right\rangle
-\mathrm{c.c.},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\dket{\phi_n^h}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_N^\dagger U_T \dket{\psi_0^h}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\dket{\phi_n^h}$ is obtained by applying the target unitary $U_T$ to the basis state $\dket{\psi_0^h}$ and then backward propagating the result to time step $n$.

The additional cost contributions are treated next. For
\begin{equation}
C_2^{\mathrm{st}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N \dbra{\psi_{n'}}\Omega \dket{\psi_{n'}},
\end{equation}
the gradient is
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial C_2^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial a_n}
&=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\dbra{\psi_{n'}}\Omega
\frac{\partial}{\partial a_n}
\prod_{n''=1}^{n'} U_{n''}\dket{\psi_0}
+\mathrm{c.c.}
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{N}
\left(
\sum_{n'=n}^N
\dbra{\psi_{n'}}
\Omega
U_{n'}\cdots U_{n+1}
\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n}
\dket{\psi_{n-1}}
\right)
+\mathrm{c.c.}
\nonumber\\
\label{C2stgrad}
&=
\frac{2}{N}
\Real\left(
\left\langle \Phi_n \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1} \right\rangle
\right),
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_n}
:=
\sum_{n'=n}^N
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_{n'}^\dagger \,
\Omega \dket{\psi_{n'}}.
\end{equation}
The vector $\dket{\Phi_n}$ satisfies the recursion relation
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_n}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\Phi_{n+1}}
+
\Omega \dket{\psi_n}.
\end{equation}

For the trajectory-dependent state-transfer cost
\begin{equation}
C_3^{\mathrm{st}}
=
1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\left\langle \phi_T \middle| \psi_{n'} \right\rangle
\right|^2,
\end{equation}
the gradient is
\begin{equation}
\label{state time gradient}
\frac{\partial C_3^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial a_n}
=
-\frac{2}{N}
\Real\left(
\left\langle \Phi_{T,n} \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1} \right\rangle
\right),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}}
:=
\sum_{n'=n}^N
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_{n'}^\dagger
\dket{\phi_T}
\langle \phi_T | \psi_{n'} \rangle.
\end{equation}
This vector obeys the recursion relation
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\Phi_{T,n+1}}
+
\dket{\phi_T}\langle \phi_T | \psi_n \rangle,
\end{equation}
which again leads to a forward-backward evaluation scheme.

Finally, for the gate analogue
\begin{equation}
C_3^{g}
=
1-\frac{1}{N d^2}\sum_{n'=1}^N
\left|
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_T^\dagger U_{n',1}\right)
\right|^2,
\end{equation}
the gradient becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C_3^g}{\partial a_n}
=
-\frac{2}{N d^2}
\sum_{h=1}^d
\Real\left(
\left\langle \Phi_{T,n}^h \middle| \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \middle| \psi_{n-1}^h \right\rangle
\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}^h}
:=
\sum_{n'=n}^N
U_{n+1}^\dagger \cdots U_{n'}^\dagger
U_T \dket{\psi_0^h}\,
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_{n',1}U_T^\dagger\right).
\end{equation}
The corresponding recursion relation is
\begin{equation}
\dket{\Phi_{T,n}^h}
=
U_{n+1}^\dagger \dket{\Phi_{T,n+1}^h}
+
U_T \dket{\psi_0^h}\,
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(U_{n,1}U_T^\dagger\right).
\end{equation}

All gradients considered here can be evaluated with an efficient forward-backward propagation scheme discussed right below.

\section{Numerical Evaluation of Hard-Coded Gradients}
\label{sec:numerical-gradients}
The analytical gradient expressions, such as $\nabla C_1^{st}$ in Eq.\ \eqref{stgradient}, are generally complicated and involve a large number of contributing quantities. Depending on grouping and ordering of operations, these quantities may need to be stored simultaneously or computed repeatedly with critical implications for runtime and memory usage. The scheme originally proposed by Khaneja\ \textit{et al.}\ \cite{Khaneja2005} strikes a favorable compromise that avoids the proliferation of stored intermediate state vectors. More specifically, the final quantum state $\dket{\psi_N}$ is obtained by forward propagating $\dket{\psi_0}$. Then, backward propagation of $\dket{\psi_N},\dket{\phi_T}$ is carried out to calculate the matrix element $\langle\phi_{n}|\frac {\partial U_n }{\partial a_{n}}|\psi_{n-1}\rangle$ needed for the $n$-th component of the gradient, thus building up the gradient in descending order. For both propagation directions, only the current intermediate states are stored, leading to a memory cost of $\Theta(1)$. \footnote{Here, $f(x)=\Theta
(g(x))$ is the usual Bachmann–Landau notation for $f(x)$ bounded above and below by $g(x)$ in the asymptotic sense.}
The scheme is illustrated by Fig.\ \ref{fig:GRAPE}.

The calculation of gradients in Eqs.\ \eqref{stgradient} and \eqref{gategradient} requires numerical evaluation of expressions of the form $U_n\dket{\Psi}$  and $\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}$. 
Here, the short-time propagator $U_n$ is given by a matrix exponential $e^A$ and $A=-iH_n\Delta t$. 
Numerically evaluating  $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ is not without challenge. As pointed out by Moler and Van Loan \cite{expmoler2003nineteen}, several methods exist, yet none of them is truly optimal. Three methods ranked highly in reference \cite{expmoler2003nineteen} are based on solving ordinary differential equations, matrix diagonalization and scaling and squaring (combined with series expansion). ODE solving, in this context, tends to be most expensive in runtime \cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum}. We mainly on focus on scaling and squaring but comment on situations where matrix diagonalization is preferable in Sec.\ \ref{sec:decision-tree}. 

\subsection{State Propagation via the Scaling and Squaring Method}
\label{subsec:scaling-squaring}

Scaling and squaring~\cite{codenotti1992error} is based on the identity
\begin{align}
\label{eq:scaling_squaring}
   e^A=\big(e^{A/s}\big)^s,
\end{align}
where the right-hand side matrix exponential now involves the matrix $B=A/s$ which has a norm that is reduced compared to $\norm{A}$. This generally allows for a lower truncation order $m $ of the exponential series\footnote{While Chebyshev expansion has faster convergence than Taylor expansion, their numerical implementations have the same runtime scaling. We focuse on Taylor expansion due to its simplicity.}, 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:eBm}
    e^B\approx e^B_m:=\sum_{q=0}^m\frac{ B^q}{q!}.
\end{align}
Typically, $m$ is smaller than the one required for the original $e^A$.
The goal is to approximate the state vector $e^A \dket{\Psi}\approx (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}$ where 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eBmpsi}
   e^B_m\dket{\Psi}= \parenthesis{1+B+\frac{B^2}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{B^m}{m!}}\dket{\Psi}
   =\dket{\Psi}+B\dket{\Psi}+\frac{B\parenthesis{B\dket{\Psi}}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{B\parenthesis{\cdots \parenthesis{B\dket{\Psi}}}}{m!}\text{   . }
\end{equation}
According to the last expression, approximation of $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ can thus proceed by repeated application of $B=A/s$ to a vector, without ever invoking matrix-matrix multiplication. This boosts runtime performance from $O(d^3)$ to $O(d^2)$.

To proceed with the evaluation of $e^A\dket{\Psi}$, the truncation order $m$ and scaling factor $s$ are determined in such a way that the error remains below the error tolerance $\tau$, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:error_def}
    \varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}(m,s) := \frac{\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-\llbracket (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}\rrbracket}}{\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}}}< \tau .
\end{equation}
Here, $\llbracket \bullet{}  \rrbracket$ denotes the floating point representation of $\bullet$. Since this error $\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}$ is difficult and costly to evaluate, we instead derive an upper bound $E_A(m,s)$ and resorts to the inequality 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:accuracy_inequality}
    \varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}(m,s)<E_A(m,s)\leq \tau.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Bounding Numerical Errors}
\label{subsubsec:ss-bounding-errors}
The approximation of $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ required the determination of an upper bound $E_A(m,s)$ for the error $\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}(m,s)$ [Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_def}]. The following shows how to obtain such a bound.

Considering the expression for the error [Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_def}], the denominator is simply 1. The error $\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}$ is bounded as follows:
\begin{align}
\nonumber    &\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}
    =\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}+(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}-\llbracket (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}\rrbracket}_2\\
\nonumber    &\quad \leq \underbrace{\norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}}_2}_{\varepsilon_t}+\underbrace{\norm{(e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}-\llbracket (e^B_m)^s\dket{\Psi}\rrbracket}_2}_{\varepsilon_r}.\\
\label{eq:epsilonA}
\end{align}
The error $\varepsilon_t$ arises from the truncation of the Taylor series and rounding error $\varepsilon_r$ is due to finite-precision arithmetics. The upper bounds for these two errors are derived separately below.

\paragraph{Upper bound for the truncation error \texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon_t$}{epsilon\_t}.}
The truncation error can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_t = \norm{e^A\dket{\Psi}-(e^B-R_m(B))^s\dket{\Psi}}_2=  \norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!} (-R_m(B))^i(e^B)^{s-i}\dket{\Psi}}_2,
\end{equation}
where $R_m(B)=\sum_{q=m+1}^{\infty}B^q/q!$ denotes the matrix-valued error of the truncated exponential series.
Employing the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity~\cite{higham2002accuracy}, one finds the bound
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_t \leq 
    \sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!}\big(R_m(\norm{B}_2)\big)^i\norm{e^B}_2^{s-i}\norm{\dket{\Psi}}_2=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!}\big(R_m(\norm{B}_2)\big)^i.
\end{equation}
Here the second equality utilizes the fact that the state is normalized and $e^B$ is unitary. 

This upper bound must be evaluated numerically to determine $m$ and $s$. However, computing the matrix 2-norm is inconvenient since it requires the use of an eigensolver. To mitigate this, we switch to the matrix 1-norm by exploiting\footnote{Generally, $\norm{B}_2\leq\sqrt{\norm{B}_\infty\norm{B}_1}$~\cite{wilkinson1994rounding} holds true for any $B$. When $B$ is anti-Hermitian, $\norm{B}_\infty=\norm{B}_1$ leads to the inequality in Eq.~\eqref{eq:norm_inequality}.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:norm_inequality}
    \norm{B}_2\leq\norm{B}_1.
\end{equation}
Monotonicity of $R_m$ leads to
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:truncation}
    \varepsilon_t \leq 
    \sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{s!}{i!(s-i)!}\big(R_m(\norm{B}_1)\big)^i
    < sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\frac{1-\big(sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\big)^s}{1-sR_m(\norm{B}_1)}.
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Upper bound for the rounding error \texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon_r$}{epsilon\_r}.}
\label{par:rounding_error}

The rounding error $\varepsilon_r$ originates from the finite precision of the floating-point representation of real numbers as well as the finite accuracy of basic arithmetic operations. The floating-point representation  $\llbracket \xi \rrbracket$ for the real number $\xi $ can be written as $\llbracket \xi \rrbracket=\xi(1+\delta)$ where $\delta=\delta(\xi)$ is the relative deviation~\cite{higham2002accuracy}. Its magnitude is always smaller than machine precision, i.e.\ $|\delta|<u$. Similarly, the complex number $z$ obeys 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:representation_error}
    \llbracket z\rrbracket=z(1+\delta)
\end{equation}
with $|\delta|\leq u$. Arithmetic operations such as addition and multiplication with two complex numbers $z_1, z_2$ incur a relative deviation $\delta=\delta(z_1,z_2)$ such that 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:operation_error}
    \llbracket z_1 \text{ + } z_2\rrbracket =(\llbracket z_1 \rrbracket+\llbracket z_2\rrbracket)(1+\delta),\ |\delta|\leq u, \quad\llbracket z_1 z_2 \rrbracket=\llbracket z_1\rrbracket \llbracket z_2\rrbracket(1+\delta),\ |\delta|\leq u',
\end{equation}
where $u':=2\sqrt{2}u/(1-2u)$~\cite{higham2002accuracy}. The relative deviation shown in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:representation_error}, \eqref{eq:operation_error} generally depend on both numbers involved as well as the operation in question. In the following, different $\delta$ are distinguished with subscript $\nu$.

Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:representation_error}, \eqref{eq:operation_error}, we derive an upper bound for the rounding error in the evaluation of the dot product, which is the basic arithmetic operation in evaluating $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:eBmpsi}.

\subparagraph{Upper bound for the rounding error associated with evaluating the dot product.}
The rounding error incurred when evaluating the dot product is given by $|\Sigma_d-\llbracket \Sigma_d\rrbracket|$ with $\Sigma_d=\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}z}$ ($\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol {z} \in\mathbb{C}^{d}$). Here, $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$ is a row of $B=i\delta t H/s$. Based on Eqs.~\eqref{eq:representation_error} and \eqref{eq:operation_error}, one has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c7}
    |\llbracket \Sigma_1\rrbracket-\Sigma_1|=|\llbracket w_1  z_1\rrbracket-w_1z_1|<|w_1||z_1|\big|\prod_{\nu=1}^3(1+\delta_\nu)-1\big|<\gamma_3 |w_1||z_1|,
\end{equation}
where $\big|\prod_{\nu=1}^n(1+\delta_\nu)-1\big|<\frac{nu'}{1-nu'}$~\cite{higham2002accuracy}, and $\gamma_n:=\frac{nu'}{1-nu'}$. For $d=2$, the upper bound of the error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c8}
     |\llbracket \Sigma_2\rrbracket-\Sigma_2|=|(\llbracket \Sigma_1\rrbracket+\llbracket w_2 z_2\rrbracket )(1+\delta_1)-\Sigma_2|<\gamma_4\sum_{i=1}^2|w_i||z_i|.
\end{equation}
The general upper bound for arbitrary $d$ is:
\begin{equation}
    |\llbracket \Sigma_d\rrbracket-\Sigma_d| <\gamma_{d+2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|w_j|| z_j|.
\end{equation}
Since $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$ is proportional to a row of the Hamiltonian matrix, sparsity of $H$ implies sparsity of $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$. Vanishing entries in $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$ are special because they do not incur rounding errors. Thus, one can obtain a stronger upper bound
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:C10}
    |\llbracket \Sigma_d\rrbracket-\Sigma_d|<\gamma_{\sigma+2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|w_j|| z_j|
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the number of non-zero elements in $\boldsymbol{w^\text{T}}$.

\subparagraph{Upper bound for the rounding error of $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$.}
The vector $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$ is evaluated recursively via
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recursive_relation_1}
    b_j= \frac{B}{j}b_{j-1},\qquad e^B_j b_0=b_{j-1}+b_j
\end{equation}
where $j=1,2,\cdots,m$ and $b_0$ is a vector representation of $\dket{\Psi}$ under a choice of orthonormal basis. Before evaluating the upper bound for the error $\norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-e^B_mb_0}_2$, an upper bound is first derived for the error of a vector component $\big|\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket^{(i)}-(e^B_mb_0) ^{(i)}\big|$.

For the case $m=0$:
\begin{equation}
    \big|\llbracket e^B_0b_0 \rrbracket^{(i)} -(e^B_0b_0)^{(i)} \big|
    =\big|\llbracket b_0\rrbracket ^{(i)} -b_0 ^{(i)}\big|<\gamma_2|b_0^{(i)}|.
\end{equation}
For $m=1$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:c17}
    &|\llbracket e^B_1b_0 \rrbracket^{(i)}-(e^B_1b_0 )^{(i)}|
    =\big|\big(\llbracket b_0\rrbracket ^{(i)}+\llbracket 
    b_1\rrbracket^{(i)}\big)(1+\delta_1)-(e^B_1b_0 )^{(i)}\big| \\
\nonumber    &\quad=\big|\llbracket b_0\rrbracket ^{(i)}(1+\delta_1)+\frac{\llbracket Ab_0\rrbracket}{s}^{(i)}\prod_{\nu=1}^3(1+\delta_\nu)^3-(e^B_1b_0 )^{(i)}\big| \overset{\eqref{eq:C10}}{<}\gamma_3|b_0^{(i)}| + \gamma_{\sigma_i+5}\sum_l|B^{(il)}||b_0^{(l)}|,
\end{align}
where $\sigma_i$ is the number of non-zero elements in the $i$th row of $B$.
The general upper bound for arbitrary $m$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c16}
    \big|\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket^{(i)} -(e^B_mb_0 )^{(i)}\big|<\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma_i+2)+m+2}\big(\frac{|B|^k}{k!}|b_0|\big)^i,
\end{equation}
where $|B|,|b_0|$ denote the matrix and the vector with elements $|B^{il}|$, $|b_0^{i}|$, respectively. Defining
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigmapdef}
    \sigma':=\max_i \sigma_i
\end{equation} 
and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:c16}:
\begin{align}
\nonumber    & \norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket -(e^B_mb_0 )}_2=\big|\big||\llbracket e^B_mb_0 \rrbracket -(e^B_mb_0 )|\big|\big|_2<\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\big|\big|\frac{|B|^k}{k!}|b_0|\big|\big|_2\\
\label{eq:B23} &\quad <\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\frac{\big|\big||B|\big|\big|_2^k}{k!}\big|\big||b_0|\big|\big|_2
 \overset{\eqref{eq:norm_inequality}}{<}\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\frac{\big|\big||B|\big|\big|_1^k}{k!}\big|\big||b_0|\big|\big|_2=\beta||b_0||_2
\end{align}
with $\beta:=\sum_{k=0}^m\gamma_{k(\sigma'+2)+m+2}\frac{||B||_1^k}{k!}$. Here, $\beta||b_0||_2$ is the desired upper bound for the rounding error of $e^B_m\dket{\Psi}$. 

\subparagraph{Upper bound for the error $\varepsilon_r$.}
\label{par:upper_bound_eps_r}
The vector $(e^B_m)^sb_0 $ is evaluated by the recursive relation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recursive_relation2}
    c_l=e^B_m c_{l-1},\qquad l=1,2,\cdots,s,
\end{equation}
where $c_0=b_0$. 
The error $\varepsilon_r=\norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^sb_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^sb_0 }_2$ is bounded for $s=1,2$ below, and the general result is given subsequently. 

For $s=1$: 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s1}
    \norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-e^B_mb_0 }_2\overset{\eqref{eq:B23}}{<}\beta||b_0||_2=\beta.
\end{equation}
For $s=2$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:C22}
\nonumber    \norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^2b_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^2b_0 }_2 &\leq\norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^2b_0\rrbracket-     
    e^B_m\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket}_2+\norm{e^B_m\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^2b_0}_2\\
   &\, <\norm{\llbracket e^B_m \llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket\rrbracket-     
    e^B_m\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket}_2+\beta\norm{e^B_m}_2,
\end{align} 
where the last step is enabled by Eq.~\eqref{eq:s1}.
The matrix norm in the second term is bounded by
\begin{equation}
    \norm{e^B_m}_2=\norm{e^B-R_m(B)}_2\leq 1+R_m(\norm{B}_2)\overset{\eqref{eq:norm_inequality}}{\leq} 1+R_m(\norm{B}_1)=:\alpha.
\end{equation}
Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:B23} holds for any complex vector $b_0$, the error in Eq.~\eqref{eq:C22} is further bounded by:
\begin{align}
    \norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^2b_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^2b_0 }_2\overset{\eqref{eq:B23}}{<}\beta\norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket}_2+\beta\alpha &=\beta\norm{\llbracket e^B_mb_0\rrbracket-e^B_mb_0+e^B_mb_0}_2+\beta\alpha\quad\\
    &\overset{\eqref{eq:s1}}{<}\beta(\beta+\alpha)+\beta\alpha.
\end{align}
Generalizing this strategy further yields the expression for the upper bound of $\varepsilon_r$: 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:rounding}
\nonumber    \varepsilon_r=\norm{\llbracket (e^B_m)^sb_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^sb_0 }_2 &\leq \sum_{l=0}^{s-1}\norm{(e^B_m)^l \llbracket (e^B_m)^{s-l}b_0\rrbracket-(e^B_m)^{l+1}\llbracket (e^B_m)^{s-l-1}b_0\rrbracket}_2\\
    &<\beta\sum_{l=0}^{s-1}(\alpha+\beta)^{s-l-1}\alpha^{l}=(\alpha+\beta)^s-\alpha^s.
\end{align}    

Combining the upper bounds for $\varepsilon_r$ and $\varepsilon_t$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilonA} yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overallupperbound}
\varepsilon_{A,\dket{\Psi}}< E_A(m,s)=sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\frac{1-\big(sR_m(\norm{B}_1)\big)^s}{1-sR_m(\norm{B}_1)}+(\alpha+\beta)^s-\alpha^s,
\end{equation}
where $\norm{B}_1=\norm{A}_1/s$, $\alpha=\alpha(\norm{A}_1,m,s)$ and $\beta=\beta(\sigma',\norm{A}_1,m,s)$.

\subsubsection{Choosing scaling factor and truncation order}

For a given $A$ when computing state propagation by scaling and squaring discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:scaling-squaring}, the inequality~\eqref{eq:accuracy_inequality} generally has multiple solutions for $m$ and $s$. As shown in the previous section, the evaluation requires $ms$ matrix-vector multiplications, which are observed to dominate the runtime. Since systematic minimization of $ms$ requires significant runtime itself, we instead (1) select solutions to Eq.~\eqref{eq:accuracy_inequality} with the smallest $s$, which is denoted by $\mathbb{s}$, and (2) among these pairs, choose the one with smallest $m$ (denoted by $\mathbb{m}$). 
 
Thus, calculating $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ requires
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mudefnition}
    \mu:=\mathbb{m}\mathbb{s}
\end{equation}
matrix-vector multiplications.
As shown in the following, for two concrete examples, this method for evaluating $e^A\dket{\Psi}$ can achieve better runtime performance and accuracy than \texttt{expm\_multiply} function~\cite{virtanen2020scipy,al2011computing} implemented in \texttt{SciPy}.

\subsection{Propagator Derivative Acting on a State via the Auxiliary Matrix Method}
\label{subsec:auxiliary-matrix}

Having addressed the challenges associated with numerically evaluating $e^A\dket{\Psi}$, we now turn to the calculation of $\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}$, which is crucial for computing cost function gradients as in Eqs.~\eqref{stgradient} and~\eqref{gategradient}. This evaluation is based on the approximation 
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}\approx \frac{\partial (e^{B_n}_m)^s}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi},
\end{align}
where $B_n={-iH_n\Delta t}/{s}$. I proceed by using the auxiliary matrix method based on the identity~\cite{auxigoodwin2015auxiliary,najfeld1995derivatives}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:auxiliary}
    \begin{aligned}
    (e^{\mathcal{A}_n/s}_m)^s
    \binom{0}{|\Psi\rangle}&=
    \binom{
    \frac{\partial (e^{B_{n}}_m)^s}{\partial a_n}  \dket{\Psi}}{{(e^{B_n}_m)^s|\Psi\rangle}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal{A}_n$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_n:=
{\biggl(\begin{matrix}
-iH_n \Delta t& -ih_c\Delta t \\
0 & -iH_n\Delta t
\end{matrix}\biggr)},
\end{equation}
which is a $2\times2$ matrix of operators each acting on the Hilbert space with dimension $d$. Once a basis is chosen, $\mathcal{A}_n$ can be represented as a $2d\times2d$ matrix of complex numbers. Numerical evaluation of the left-hand side of the identity~\eqref{eq:auxiliary} gives a $2d$-dimensional vector. The first $d$ entries of the vector correspond to $\partial (e^{B_n}_m)^s / \partial a_n \dket{\Psi}$, which is an approximation of $\frac{\partial U_n}{\partial a_n} \dket{\Psi}$.

As an alternative to Taylor series and scaling-and-squaring for $U_n=e^{-iH_n\Delta t}$, the propagator can be built from a diagonalization of $-iH_n\Delta t$, with $\partial U_n/\partial a_n$ obtained from the closed form in Appendix~\ref{app:matdiag}. That route changes the asymptotic costs of HG: memory and runtime scale as summarized in the scaling discussion there and enter the comparison with scaling-and-squaring in Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling-analysis} and in the decision-tree discussion below.

\section{Scaling Analysis of Runtime and Memory Usage for Three Gradient Strategies}
\label{sec:scaling-analysis}

Having established the numerical machinery for evaluating hard-coded gradients above, this section compares three methods for computing gradients within GRAPE: evaluation of hard-coded gradients (HG), full automatic differentiation (full-AD)~\cite{pacleung2017speedup}, and semi-automatic differentiation (semi-AD)~\cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum}. To determine the method most appropriate for a given problem, the memory usage and runtime scaling of these three approaches are inspected.

Runtime and memory usage can pose significant challenges when performing optimization tasks for quantum systems with large Hilbert space dimension ($d$) or for time evolution requiring a significant number of time steps ($N$). In this section, we analyze the scaling of memory usage and runtime with respect to both $N$ and $d$, focusing on the cost contribution $C_1$ for state-transfer and unitary-gate optimization. The findings can be generalized to other cost contributions beyond $C_1$.

\subsection{Hard-coded gradients}
\label{subsec:scale-hard-coded}

Consider first state-transfer problems. The \textit{memory usage} for evaluating $\nabla C_1^{st}$ is mainly determined by the required storage of quantum states and the Hamiltonian. In forward-backward propagation, the number of states and instances of Hamiltonian matrices stored at any given time is of the order of unity. Each individual state vector of dimension $d$ consumes memory $\sim\Theta(d)$. Memory usage for the Hamiltonian depends on the employed storage scheme. In the ``worst'' case of dense matrix storage, the memory required for the Hamiltonian is $\Theta(d^2)$. For sparse matrix storage, memory usage generally depends on sparsity and is determined by the number of stored Hamiltonian matrix elements (here denoted as $\kappa$).
For example, for a diagonal or tridiagonal matrix, memory usage is $\kappa\sim\Theta(d)$; for a linear chain of qubits with nearest-neighbor $\sigma_z$ coupling, it is $\Theta(d\, \log_2 d)$. Combining the scaling relations above gives an overall asymptotic behavior of memory usage $\Theta(d+\kappa)$.

The \textit{runtime} of the scheme is dominated by the required $\Theta(N)$ evaluations of propagator-state products and their derivative counterparts. Numerically approximating a single propagator-state product requires $\mu$ matrix-vector multiplications [see Eq.~\eqref{eq:mudefnition}], where the runtime of one instance of matrix-vector multiplication scales as $\Theta(\kappa)$.  The implicit dependence of $\mu=\mu(d)$ on $d$ is specific to each particular system and can be difficult to obtain analytically. Numerically, for the cavity--transmon model $H_1(t)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fockH}, $\mu\sim\Theta(\sqrt{d})$; for a linear chain of coupled qubits, the scaling is $\Theta(\log_2 d)$; see Appendix~\ref{app:muscaling} for more details.
Once combined, the above individual scaling relations lead to an overall runtime asymptotic behavior
$\Theta(N\mu\, \kappa)$. For example, in the case of the linear chain of coupled qubits, runtime scales as $\Theta(Nd\, (\log_2 d )^2)$.

For gate-optimization problems, the scaling behavior of memory usage and runtime is as follows. The gradient $\nabla C_1^{ g}$ is calculated by sequentially
applying forward-backward propagation to each of the $d$ basis states. The memory usage is $\Theta(d+\kappa)$, the same as for $\nabla C_1^{st}$. The runtime scaling is $\Theta(N\mu\kappa d)$, which is $d$ times larger than the runtime scaling for $\nabla C_1^{st}$. (Another way of calculating $\nabla C_1^{ g}$ consists of parallelized forward-backward propagation of all basis states. The concrete scaling behavior then depends on the parallelization scheme; that discussion is beyond the scope of this work.)

Thanks to the similarities in the expressions and the existence of recursion relations, forward-backward schemes akin to Fig.~\ref{fig:GRAPE}
can also be applied to $\nabla C_2^{st}$, $\nabla C_3^{st}$, and $\nabla C_3^{g}$. As a result, the scaling analysis leads to the same memory usage and runtime requirements as $\nabla C_1^{st}$ and $\nabla C_1^{g}$.



\subsection{Full automatic differentiation}
\label{subsec:scale-full-ad}

Reverse-mode automatic differentiation extracts gradients by a forward pass and a reverse pass through the computational tree. With the forward pass the cost function $C$ is computed, and the backward pass accumulates the gradient via the chain rule.

The \textit{memory usage} for evaluating $\nabla C_1^{st}$ is determined by the requirement that all intermediate numerical results must be stored as part of the forward pass and act as input to the reverse pass. Specifically, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:GRAPE}, the forward pass evolves the initial state vector to the final state vector in $N$ time steps. The total number of vectors that have to be stored along the way is $\Theta
(N\mu)$, since computation of a single short-time propagator-state product via scaling and squaring necessitates $\mu$ iterations. In addition, considering the memory usage for the Hamiltonian, the full scaling is $\Theta(N\mu d +\kappa)$.

The \textit{runtime} for evaluating $\nabla C_1^{st}$ combines forward and reverse passes. It is dominated by the evaluation of $\Theta(N)$ propagator-state products, each of which has the runtime scaling $\Theta(\mu\kappa)$, leading to an overall runtime scaling of $\Theta(N\mu \kappa )$.

For gate-optimization problems, $C_1^{ g}$ is evaluated by evolving not only a single initial state but a whole set of $d$ basis vectors. Evaluating $Nd$ propagator-state products requires $\Theta(N\mu d^2+\kappa)$ memory, where the number of stored Hamiltonian matrix elements $\kappa$ takes into account storage of the Hamiltonian. Since $\kappa$ has the worst-case scaling $\Theta(d^2)$ in the case of a dense Hamiltonian matrix, the overall scaling is $\Theta(N\mu d^2)$. Assuming that the evolution of individual basis vectors is not parallelized, runtime also grows by a factor of $d$, so that runtime scaling for $\nabla C_1^{ g}$ is $\Theta(N\mu\kappa d)$.

Despite the specific differences among the expressions of cost contributions $C_2^{st}$, $C_3^{st}$, and $C_3^{g}$, the scaling of runtime and memory usage is again set by the need to store intermediate vectors and repeatedly perform matrix-vector multiplications. Consequently, the scaling remains the same as for the cost contributions discussed above.

\subsection{Semi-automatic differentiation}
\label{subsec:scale-semi-ad}

For gradients of cost contributions that are tedious to derive analytically, semi-automatic differentiation~\cite{semiadgoerz2022quantum} offers an alternative approach in which some derivatives are hard-coded and others treated by automatic differentiation. Compared to full-AD, semi-AD has the benefit that the memory usage scaling is independent of $\mu$ while maintaining the same runtime scaling.

& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{\textsc{State transfer}}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{\textsc{Gate operation}}}\\

\\

\end{table}

The results of this scaling analysis are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:scalingtable}. For full-AD, memory usage grows linearly with respect to $\mu$ and number of time steps $N$. By contrast, memory usage for HG does not increase with $\mu$ and $N$, but remains constant. This advantage is not bought at the cost of worse runtime scaling. Therefore, HG is preferable over full-AD in the optimal control of large quantum systems where memory usage would otherwise be prohibitively large. Semi-AD presents an interesting compromise viable whenever the memory bottleneck is not too severe. Note that the runtime scaling $\Theta(N\mu\kappa d)$ of HG via scaling and squaring can in principle exceed the scaling $O(Nd^3)$ for matrix exponentiation based on matrix diagonalization (see Appendix~\ref{app:matdiag}). In that case, the latter is the better option.

\section{Benchmark Studies of Runtime and Memory Usage for the Three Gradient Strategies}
\label{sec:benchmark-studies}
Following the scaling analysis in previous section, we present benchmark studies of specific state-transfer and gate-operation
problems to illustrate the scaling behavior.
\subsection{State-Transfer Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:state-transfer-benchmarks}

The first benchmark example studies state transfer in a driven flux-tunable transmon capacitively coupled to a superconducting cavity, from the cavity vacuum to the 20-photon Fock state, using the Hamiltonian $H_1(t)$ already introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fockH}.

Optimization proceeds by minimizing the infidelity $C_1^{\mathrm{st}}$ and limiting the occupation of higher transmon levels by including the cost contribution $C_2^{\mathrm{st}}$ [Eq.~\eqref{c2st}]. The latter has the additional benefit of enabling the truncation to only a few transmon levels. We measure runtime and peak memory usage of full-AD,\footnote{AD is implemented by the package \texttt{Autograd}~\cite{maclaurin2015autograd} in this benchmark. This package does not support sparse linear algebra, so dense matrix storage for the Hamiltonian is implemented in both full-AD and HG for fair comparison.} semi-AD, and HG for a single optimization iteration. The runtime is measured by the package \texttt{Temci}~\cite{temci} and
the peak memory usage is monitored by the \texttt{memory\_profiler} package~\cite{memory}. The optimization is performed on an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3970X 32-Core CPU with 3.7\,GHz frequency.

  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{chapter3/figures/state.pdf}

(a)~Runtime per iteration and (b)~memory usage versus Hilbert space dimension $d$. The dimension is varied by increasing the cavity dimension while fixing the transmon dimension to 6.   \label{fig:state_time}

Benchmark results for the state-transfer optimization are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:state_time}. We consider a single time step ($N=1$) and measures the runtime versus $d$. The results in Fig.~\ref{fig:state_time}(a) confirm that full-AD, semi-AD, and HG have the same scaling of runtime per time step with respect to $d$ (Table~\ref{tab:scalingtable}). The observed runtime scaling $\Theta(d^{5/2})$ is consistent with $\kappa \sim \Theta(d^2)$ due to dense matrix storage for $H_1$, and $\mu\sim \Theta(d^{1/2})$ (see Appendix~\ref{app:muscaling}). The scaling of the required memory resources is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:state_time}(b): memory usage scales with dimension $d$ as $\Theta(d^2)$ for full-AD, semi-AD, and HG, consistent with $\kappa\sim \Theta(d^2)$ being the dominant contribution. As anticipated, the scaling of memory with $N$ differs between full-AD, semi-AD, and HG [Fig.~\ref{fig:state_time}(c)]. For full-AD and semi-AD, the scaling is linear with $N$, while it is independent of $N$ for HG. The slope of full-AD is much larger than the one of semi-AD, since the former has extra dependence on $\mu$ (Table~\ref{tab:scalingtable}), which is a constant $\approx 100$ in this benchmark. The results confirm the favorable memory efficiency of HG and semi-AD compared to full-AD in state-transfer problems with large number of time steps.

\subsection{Gate-Operation Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:gate-operation-benchmarks}

We next turn to unitary gate optimization for three driven transmons with nearest-neighbor coupling. The target gate consists of simultaneous Hadamard operations on each transmon, using the Hamiltonian $H_2(t)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gateH}. The goal is to minimize the gate infidelity $C_1^{\mathrm{g}}$. The numerical setup (Autograd, dense storage for fair comparison, timing tools) matches the state-transfer benchmark above.

Fig.~\ref{fig:gate} records the benchmarks based on a single time step ($N=1$). The observed runtime scaling with dimension $d$ is $\Theta(d^{11/3})$ for full-AD, semi-AD, and HG, see Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}(a). \section{Decision Tree: A Practical Guide for Selecting Optimization Strategies}
\label{sec:decision-tree}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{chapter3/figures/decision_tree.pdf}
\caption[Decision trees for selecting the optimal numerical strategy.]{Decision trees for selecting the optimal numerical strategy. (a)~Choosing among three numerical methods for evaluating gradients: full automatic differentiation, semi-automatic differentiation, hard-coded analytical gradients. (Here, $\mu\sim\Theta(d)$ is assumed; for different scaling see Table~\ref{tab:scalingtable}.) (b)~Determining the appropriate strategy for evaluating propagator-state products.}
\label{fig:decision_tree}
\end{figure}

There are several considerations which determine whether full-AD, HG, or semi-AD is the optimal strategy for a given optimization problem, see the decision tree in Fig.~\ref{fig:decision_tree}. Whenever the Hilbert space dimension $d$ and the number of time steps $N$ are sufficiently small, memory usage may not be a bottleneck. In this case, full-AD may be preferable, since it eliminates the need for hard-coded gradients. If the memory cost of full-AD is not affordable but analytical gradients are tedious to compute, semi-AD may be a viable compromise, though still less memory efficient than HG. In all other cases, HG is the method of choice, since it can handle optimization with larger $N$ and $d$ compared to full-AD and semi-AD.

The choice of implementing HG via scaling and squaring or matrix diagonalization should be informed by whether the Hamiltonian is sparse and $\kappa$ scales better than $\Theta(d^2)$. In the latter case, scaling and squaring is preferable and leads to an overall memory usage $\sim \Theta(d+\kappa)$, which is better than the memory scaling $O(d^2)$ of matrix diagonalization. By contrast, for Hamiltonians with $\kappa\sim \Theta(d^2)$, these two methods are equivalent in memory usage scaling, and the one with better runtime for a specific optimization task should be selected. While the runtime for optimization using matrix diagonalization scales as $O(d^3)$ [Appendix~\ref{app:matdiag}], the runtime for optimization based on scaling and squaring differs between state-transfer and gate optimizations: (1)~for state transfer, the runtime scales as $\Theta(\mu d^2)$; hence, whenever $\mu$ scales better than $\Theta(d)$, scaling and squaring wins over matrix diagonalization; (2)~for gate optimization, the runtime scales as $\Theta(\mu d^3)$; hence matrix diagonalization is preferable.

The scaling analysis and benchmarks presented in this section confirm that hard-coded gradients provide the most memory-efficient approach for GRAPE-based optimization of large quantum systems.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

Quantum computing promises to solve certain problems far more efficiently than classical computing. The clearest example is the simulation of quantum systems. The Hilbert-space dimension of a quantum many-body system grows exponentially with system size, so classical simulation quickly becomes impractical. For example, specifying the state of 50 qubits requires $2^{50} \approx 10^{15}$ complex amplitudes \cite{Feynman1982,Lloyd1996}. Quantum algorithms also offer advantages in cryptography, optimization, and machine learning \cite{Dalzell_2025}.

Large-scale quantum algorithms require fault-tolerant quantum computation. Quantum error correction enables fault tolerance by encoding one logical qubit into many physical qubits. Logical errors can be suppressed by increasing the code size or by lowering the physical error rate. In the surface code, error correction becomes effective when the physical error rate is below a threshold of order $10^{-2}$ \cite{Fowler2012SurfaceCodes,Campbell2017Roads}. In practice, however, being below threshold is not enough. The overhead still depends strongly on the physical error rate. Using the empirical scaling relation of Fowler \textit{et al.}, reducing the physical error rate from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ lowers the number of physical qubits needed to reach a logical error rate below $10^{-15}$ from about $4.8\times10^{3}$ to about $8.4\times10^{2}$ \cite{Fowler2012SurfaceCodes}.

Several hardware platforms are being pursued for fault-tolerant quantum computation \cite{Monroe2013,Bruzewicz2019,Kok2007,Slussarenko2019,Doherty2013,Pezzagna2021,Saffman2016,Bluvstein2022}. Superconducting circuits are especially attractive because they support fast gates, flexible microwave control, and scalable fabrication with mature lithographic methods \cite{Krantz2019,Kjaergaard2020}. However, superconducting devices remain sensitive to noise. Decoherence and gate errors still limit fault-tolerant operation. This thesis studies these limitations in superconducting qubits.

Coherence in superconducting circuits has improved greatly since the first demonstration of coherent control in the Cooper-pair box \cite{Nakamura1999}. The Cooper-pair box showed basic qubit control, but strong sensitivity to offset-charge noise limited coherence to the nanosecond scale \cite{Nakamura2002,Astafiev2004}. The transmon reduced charge noise by shunting the Cooper-pair box with a large capacitance, greatly improving coherence at the cost of reduced anharmonicity \cite{Koch2007,Schreier2008,Houck2008}. Fluxonium instead uses a large inductive shunt, combining protection against charge offsets with strong anharmonicity and high coherence \cite{Manucharyan2009,Nguyen2019}. More recently, protected circuits such as the $0\text{--}\pi$ qubit, the protomon, and $\cos(2\phi)$-based qubits have aimed for stronger intrinsic noise protection, though with more complex circuit designs \cite{Brooks2013,Gyenis2021,Kumar2024Protomon,Smith2020}. In parallel, bosonic qubits based on superconducting microwave cavities have emerged as a complementary approach in which quantum information is stored in long-lived linear oscillator modes \cite{Cai2021,Copetudo2024,Paik2011,Reagor2013,Reagor2016,Milul2023,Romanenko2020,Joshi2021}. These systems are attractive because hardware-efficient bosonic encodings can protect against errors with lower overhead. However, preserving coherence in cavity-based architectures remains challenging, because control usually relies on nonlinear elements that introduce extra decoherence channels. Understanding and mitigating these inherited decoherence channels is essential for realizing the potential of cavity-based architectures.

Control methods have also evolved. Early demonstrations of qubit control could use simple analytic pulse optimization. As superconducting processors became more complex, this approach became less reliable. Weak anharmonicity, spectral crowding, and crosstalk make analytic methods fragile \cite{Motzoi2009DRAG,Dumas2024}. This motivates numerical optimal-control methods that can include these constraints directly \cite{Brif2010Control,Khaneja2005,deFouquieres2011GRAPE}. One widely used method is Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering (GRAPE). With automatic differentiation, GRAPE can evaluate cost-function gradients on the fly. However, it can require substantial memory. This limits its use in large quantum systems.

This thesis addresses two challenges in superconducting quantum hardware: efficient optimal control and coherence engineering. Chapter~\ref{chap:sc-qubits} introduces the background on superconducting qubits, decoherence, and control. Chapter~\ref{chap:grape} presents the optimal-control results of this thesis. It studies gradient-based pulse optimization and compares three methods for evaluating cost-function gradients in memory use and runtime. It then gives practical guidance for choosing an efficient gradient-evaluation method for a given task. Chapter~\ref{chap:stark-sweet-spots} presents the coherence-engineering results of this thesis for cavity-based superconducting architectures. High-$Q$ cavities can have negligible intrinsic dephasing. However, their control often depends on nonlinear Josephson elements. Frequency fluctuations in these elements are inherited by the cavity and cause dephasing. We show that a weak off-resonant drive on the nonlinear element creates an AC Stark shift that cancels the associated Lamb-shift fluctuations. This creates a Stark-induced sweet spot. The mechanism extends the cavity dephasing time by more than an order of magnitude. Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the main results and discusses future directions.
